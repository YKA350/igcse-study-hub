Respiration releases energy

Respiration releases energy (1/2)

Respiration

The process that releases energy from food molecules in all living cells. It is not the same as breathing.

Aerobic respiration uses oxygen to oxidise food, mainly glucose, and releases carbon dioxide and water.

It happens in all cells of humans, animals, plants and many other organisms.

Respiration releases energy

Respiration releases energy (2/2)

Most energy is used to make ATP, some is released as heat, which helps keep body temperature constant.

It is a sequence of small steps, each catalysed by a different enzyme. The later aerobic steps release most energy and happen in the mitochondria.

Respiration releases energy

ATP, the energy currency

ATP is adenosine with three phosphate groups. Breaking off one phosphate gives ADP and releases energy.

Respiration uses energy to add a phosphate to ADP, remaking ATP.

ATP carries energy to muscle contraction, active transport and building large molecules such as proteins.

Anaerobic respiration

Anaerobic respiration

Anaerobic respiration (1/2)

Anaerobic respiration

Release of energy from glucose without oxygen. It gives less energy than aerobic respiration.

Occurs when muscles are short of oxygen, as in a sprint or lifting a heavy weight.

Lactate builds up in muscles and blood. After exercise it is broken down to carbon dioxide and water, which needs oxygen, so you keep breathing fast and deep.

Anaerobic respiration

Anaerobic respiration (2/2)

Oxygen debt is the oxygen needed to oxidise the lactate. The maximum is about 17 dm3 and repaying can take up to an hour.

In long races the body adjusts and lactate is broken down during running, the possible cause of second wind.

Lactate was once thought to cause fatigue but it actually delays muscle fatigue.

Structure of the gas exchange system

Respiration and gas exchange

Structure of the gas exchange system

Structure of the gas exchange system (1/2)

Keeping airways clean Goblet cells secrete mucus that traps dirt and bacteria. Ciliated cells have cilia that beat to sweep the mucus up to the mouth.

The lungs sit in the thorax protected by the ribcage and the diaphragm, a domed muscle sheet. Intercostal muscles link the ribs.

Air path

nose or mouth, trachea, two bronchi, bronchioles, alveoli, the air sacs where gas exchange happens.

Structure of the gas exchange system

Structure of the gas exchange system (2/2)

Cartilage rings keep the trachea and bronchi open. Rings in the trachea are C-shaped with the gap at the back against the oesophagus so food can pass.

Pleural membranes form an airtight envelope round the lungs. Pleural fluid in the pleural cavity lubricates them.

Moving air in and out of the lungs, using changes in thorax volume and pressure.

Ventilation

Ventilation (3/3)

Normal exhaling needs only elastic lungs and the weight of the ribs. Internal intercostals are used in forced breathing.

Like a bicycle pump

Gas exchange in the alveoli

Respiration and gas exchange

Gas exchange in the alveoli

Gas exchange in the alveoli (1/3)

Oxygen

Inhaled air: About 21%; Exhaled air: About 16%

Carbon dioxide

Inhaled air: About 0.04%; Exhaled air: About 4%

Nitrogen

Inhaled air: About 78%; Exhaled air: About 79% (percentage rises as oxygen is removed)

Gas exchange in the alveoli

Gas exchange in the alveoli (2/3)

Exhaled air is warmer and saturated with water vapour.

About 700 million alveoli give about 60 m2 of surface.

Adaptations

huge surface area, wall one cell thick plus a capillary wall (under a thousandth of a millimetre) so a short diffusion path, moist lining where oxygen dissolves, dense capillary network, and ventilation and blood flow keep a steep gradient.

Gas exchange in the alveoli

Gas exchange in the alveoli (3/3)

Oxygen diffuses from alveolus to blood, carbon dioxide from blood to alveolus. Say the alveolus wall is made of cells, not that it has cell walls.

Gas exchange in the alveoli

Practical: carbon dioxide in inhaled and exhaled air

Breathe gently through a middle tube so exhaled air goes through one tube of limewater and inhaled air through another.

Limewater in the exhaled tube turns cloudy first. Hydrogencarbonate indicator goes from red to yellow.

Safety

breathe slowly, do not blow, use a clean mouthpiece.

Gas exchange in the alveoli

Practical: exercise and breathing rate

Rest for 5 minutes, count breaths per minute until the resting rate is steady.

Exercise 3 minutes, then count every minute until back to rest.

Fitter people return to resting rate faster. Rate stays high to repay oxygen debt and remove lactate and carbon dioxide.

Measuring breathing volumes

Respiration and gas exchange

Measuring breathing volumes

Measuring breathing volumes (1/2)

Tidal volume

Volume of air in a normal breath, about 0.4 dm3.

Vital capacity

Maximum breath in minus maximum breath out, from about 3.0 dm3 in a young woman to 6.0 dm3 in a trained athlete.

Residual volume

About 1.5 dm3 of air always left in the lungs.

Measuring breathing volumes

Measuring breathing volumes (2/2)

A spirometer has a floating lid, soda lime to absorb carbon dioxide and a pen on a rotating drum. The trace slopes down as oxygen is used.

Simple method

breathe in deeply, blow out into an inverted bell jar over water, keeping inside and outside water levels level, and read the volume.

Use a nose clip, wide tube, repeat readings and a fresh mouthpiece for each person.

Control of breathing rate

Control of breathing rate

Control of breathing rate (1/2)

Exercise raises carbon dioxide in the blood.

Chemoreceptors in the medulla and in the aorta and carotid arteries detect it.

They signal the respiratory centre in the medulla, which sends messages to the diaphragm and intercostals so rate and depth rise.

Control of breathing rate

Control of breathing rate (2/2)

Extra carbon dioxide is then exhaled, restoring the correct level.

The effects of smoking

Respiration and gas exchange

The effects of smoking

The effects of smoking (1/4)

Smoking is linked to lung cancer, bronchitis, emphysema, coronary heart disease, stomach ulcers and underweight babies.

Cilia are destroyed and smoke irritates the lining so extra mucus blocks airways, causing smoker's cough. Infected mucus causes bronchitis.

Emphysema

alveolar walls break down and fuse, cutting surface area, so less oxygen is absorbed. There is no cure. Chronic bronchitis plus emphysema is COPD.

The effects of smoking

The effects of smoking (2/4)

Lung cancer

a 1950s hospital study found more smokers among lung cancer patients than matched controls. This shows a correlation, not proof. Over 20 further studies agreed, and doctors who quit saw lung cancer deaths fall 38%.

Smoke has nicotine (addictive), over 7000 chemicals, and tar with carcinogens, which cause mutations and uncontrolled division forming a tumour. Tar, not nicotine, stains.

The effects of smoking

The effects of smoking (3/4)

Low-tar cigarettes reduce risk only slightly. Stopping greatly lowers risk within a few years.

Carbon monoxide binds haemoglobin tightly to form carboxyhaemoglobin, so blood carries less oxygen, harming heart and fetus growth.

Nearly 6 million deaths a year from tobacco. Smoking causes about 80% of lung cancer and bronchitis and emphysema deaths.

The effects of smoking

The effects of smoking (4/4)

Giving up is hard because of nicotine withdrawal. Aids are e-cigarettes, patches and gum, drugs and acupuncture.
