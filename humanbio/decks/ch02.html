This chapter in a nutshell

Diffusion: net movement down a concentration gradient, no energy needed.

Active transport: against the gradient, uses respiration energy and protein pumps.

Osmosis: net diffusion of water across a partially permeable membrane from higher to lower water potential.

Rate depends on gradient, surface area to volume ratio, distance and temperature.

Diffusion

Diffusion

Diffusion

Diffusion

The net movement of particles (molecules or ions) from a region of high concentration to a region of low concentration, down a concentration gradient.

  1. Particles move constantly because of kinetic energy.
  2. Example: respiration makes carbon dioxide so there is net movement out of the cell. Respiration uses oxygen so there is net movement of oxygen in.
  3. No energy from respiration is needed.

Three ways in and out

Diffusion

Net movement from high to low concentration. Passive. O₂ in, CO₂ out.

Osmosis

Active transport

Against the gradient (low to high), using energy from respiration and carrier proteins.

Diffusion

Factors affecting rate

Concentration gradient

A steeper gradient means faster diffusion.

Surface area to volume ratio

A larger surface area in proportion to volume speeds diffusion. Small cells have a larger ratio.

Distance

A longer distance means slower diffusion.

Temperature

Higher temperature gives particles more kinetic energy, so faster diffusion.

Diffusion

Practical: diffusion in agar jelly (1/2)

  1. Agar jelly with purple potassium permanganate is cut into cubes of side 2 cm, 1 cm and 0.5 cm.
  2. Dropped into hydrochloric acid, the purple colour disappears as acid diffuses in.
  3. The smallest cube has the largest surface area to volume ratio and turns colourless first. The largest cube is last.
  4. If cubes are cells, the biggest cell has most difficulty getting substances by diffusion.
  5. Safety: eye protection, avoid skin contact, use gloves and a scalpel on a cutting board.

Active transport

Active transport

The movement of particles against a concentration gradient, using energy from respiration.

  1. The cell pumps ions and molecules using large protein pumps in the membrane.
  2. Example: epithelial cells of the small intestine absorb glucose by active transport.
  3. Cells use it to control uptake of many substances.

Osmosis

Osmosis (1/2)

Osmosis

The net diffusion of water across a partially permeable membrane, from a solution with a higher water potential to one with a lower water potential. Also from a dilute to a more concentrated solution.

Water potential

A measure of how free water molecules are to move. Pure water has the highest. The more concentrated a solution, the lower its water potential.

Osmosis

Osmosis (2/2)

  1. Partially permeable means some molecules pass and others cannot, e.g. Visking tubing lets water through but not the larger sucrose. Visking tubing is used in kidney dialysis machines.
  2. A selectively permeable membrane controls which substances pass, e.g. cell membranes.
  3. Water molecules attracted to sugar are less free to move, so more water moves towards the concentrated solution.

Osmosis

Practical: Visking tubing

  1. A Visking tubing sausage of concentrated sucrose is attached to a capillary tube and put in water.
  2. Water enters by osmosis and the level in the capillary tube rises.
  3. It stops when the pressure of the column equals the pressure from osmosis.

Exchange surfaces

Movement of substances into and out of cells

Exchange surfaces

Exchange surfaces

  1. Parts specialised for exchange have a very large surface area in proportion to volume.
  2. Air sacs of the lungs give a large surface for oxygen and carbon dioxide exchange (Chapter 5).
  3. The small intestine is lined with villi giving a large surface for absorption of digested food, partly by diffusion and partly by active transport (Chapter 4).
