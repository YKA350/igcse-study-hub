What is a disease? (1/3)

Bacteria

How it causes disease: Release toxins as they multiply; Examples: Typhoid, TB, gonorrhoea, cholera, pneumonia

Viruses

How it causes disease: Enter cells and take over, making more viruses; Examples: Flu, polio, HIV, measles, rubella, colds, Ebola

Fungi

How it causes disease: Hyphae secrete enzymes digesting tissue, damage tissue, some make toxins or cause allergy; Examples: Athlete's foot, thrush, ringworm

Protozoa

How it causes disease: No set pattern; Examples: Malaria, sleeping sickness

Disease

A condition with a specific cause in which part or all of the body functions abnormally and less efficiently.

What is a disease?

What is a disease? (3/3)

Causes

lifestyle (smoking, alcohol), genes (haemophilia, cystic fibrosis), or microorganisms. Infectious diseases pass from person to person.

Pathogens are disease-causing organisms

bacteria, viruses, some fungi and protozoa.

2

Bacteria

Microorganisms

Bacteria

Bacteria (1/2)

Single-celled, 1 to 5 micrometres long (an animal cell is 10 to 50), shapes: spheres (cocci), rods, spirals.

Structure

cell wall (protection and shape), sometimes a capsule or slime layer, cell membrane, cytoplasm, no nucleus, DNA as a single circular chromosome loose in cytoplasm, plasmids (small DNA rings, in about three quarters of species, used in genetic engineering), sometimes flagella.

Bacteria

Bacteria (2/2)

Most are harmless. Some make food by photosynthesis. Decomposers recycle dead matter, they treat sewage and make cheese and yoghurt.

Bacteria

Reproduction and growth curves (1/2)

Binary fission

cell grows, chromosome copied, copies move to opposite ends, membrane folds in, two new cell walls form, cells separate. It is simpler than mitosis.

Under good conditions some divide every 15 to 20 minutes. They are grown on nutrient agar in Petri dishes or in broth.

Bacteria

Reproduction and growth curves (2/2)

Growth curve phases

lag (adjusting, making enzymes), growth or exponential (cells double at equal intervals), stationary (births equal deaths, limiting factors like food, oxygen or waste), death (more die than form).

3

Viruses

Microorganisms

Viruses

Viruses (1/2)

All viruses are parasites that reproduce only inside living host cells. Very small, 0.01 to 0.1 micrometres.

A core of DNA or RNA (few genes) in a protein coat, sometimes an envelope stolen from the host membrane. No nucleus or cytoplasm.

Viruses

Viruses (2/2)

They do not feed, respire, excrete, move or grow, so most biologists say they are not living.

Reproduce by entering a host cell and making it produce new viruses, then the cell dies and releases them.

HIV and AIDS

Microorganisms

HIV and AIDS

HIV and AIDS (1/3)

AIDS (acquired immune deficiency syndrome) is a syndrome caused by HIV. HIV is an RNA virus.

It infects T-helper lymphocytes. It makes DNA from its RNA, inserts it into the host DNA, which is later activated to make new virus particles.

Antigens on the cell surface mark it for destruction by the immune system and the new viruses escape to infect more cells.

HIV and AIDS

HIV and AIDS (2/3)

Antibodies appear, the person is HIV positive. During the latency period (up to 20 years) the body replaces cells as fast as they die, no symptoms but infectious.

Eventually T-helper cells fall, the immune system fails, and opportunistic infections such as TB, pneumonia and Kaposi's sarcoma kill.

Transmitted by unprotected sex, blood contact (shared needles, transfusions), not by kissing, sharing a cup, skin contact or a toilet seat.

HIV and AIDS

HIV and AIDS (3/3)

Control

condoms, sterile needles, fewer partners, testing blood. No vaccine, antiviral drugs delay AIDS.

Ebola

Microorganisms

Ebola

Ebola (1/2)

Severe, often fatal viral disease, first in Africa in 1976, largest outbreak 2014 in Guinea, Liberia and Sierra Leone.

Incubation 2 to 21 days, not infectious until symptoms. Symptoms: fever, muscle pain, headache, sore throat, then vomiting, diarrhoea, rash, liver and kidney damage, and bleeding.

Natural host is fruit bats. Infection of humans from bats and animals, then person to person through contact with body fluids (broken skin or mucous membranes) and infected bedding.

Ebola

Ebola (2/2)

No proven cure or vaccine (vaccine trials began 2015). Treatment is supportive care, oral rehydration and intravenous fluids.

Prevention

avoid bats and monkeys, wear gloves and cook meat thoroughly, protective clothing and handwashing, survivors use condoms for 12 months, safe burials, contact tracing, isolation, community engagement.

Oral rehydration therapy

Microorganisms

Oral rehydration therapy

Oral rehydration therapy

Diarrhoea and vomiting dehydrate and upset salts, causing brain and kidney damage. It is a major killer of babies and children.

Treatment

a solution of salts and glucose in sterile water, given by mouth. It rehydrates tissues even while diarrhoea continues.

Used since the 1970s, cutting infant diarrhoea deaths from 5 million to 1.5 million by 2009. Cheap and simple.

Cholera, typhoid, gonorrhoea

Microorganisms

Cholera, typhoid, gonorrhoea

Cholera, typhoid, gonorrhoea (1/6)

Cholera Vibrio cholerae bacterium in contaminated water or food, and undercooked seafood from polluted water. Endemic where sewage reaches water.

Three quarters of infected people are symptomless carriers. The toxin damages the intestine lining causing water and salt loss, severe diarrhoea, dehydration, weakness and possible death in hours.

Treat with oral rehydration therapy. Prevent with clean water, sanitation, sewage treatment, chlorinated water and hygiene.

Cholera, typhoid, gonorrhoea

Cholera, typhoid, gonorrhoea (2/6)

Typhoid Salmonella typhi, spread by water contaminated with faeces or flies moving bacteria to food. Common in poor, crowded places with bad sanitation such as slums of megacities and disaster camps.

Over 20 million cases and 200 000 deaths a year. Incubation about 2 weeks, fever, headache, cough, cramps, diarrhoea, delirium, gut bleeding and perforation.

Treat with antibiotics, oral rehydration and vaccine. Prevent with sanitation and hygiene.

Cholera, typhoid, gonorrhoea

Cholera, typhoid, gonorrhoea (3/6)

Gonorrhoea Sexually transmitted disease from Neisseria gonorrhoeae.

Men have pus discharge and painful urination, half of those infected (mainly women) show no symptoms, women may have discharge and untreated infection spreads to the uterus, oviducts and ovaries, causing infertility.

Cured with antibiotics, often combined. Prevention: avoid risky sex, condoms (99% effective). Antibiotic resistance is growing.

Cholera, typhoid, gonorrhoea

Cholera, typhoid, gonorrhoea (4/6)

Athlete's foot Fungus whose hyphae feed on skin between the toes, spread by spores in changing rooms and by contact. Sore raw patches may get infected.

30 to 40% cured by the immune system, others by antifungal drugs. Prevent with protective foot covers, cotton socks, loose shoes, short toenails, and do not reuse infected shoes and socks.

Malaria Plasmodium protozoan spread by the female Anopheles mosquito's saliva when she takes blood.

Cholera, typhoid, gonorrhoea

Cholera, typhoid, gonorrhoea (5/6)

The parasite spends 2 weeks to several months in the liver, then infects red blood cells, which burst, releasing more and sex cells that infect other mosquitoes.

Symptoms

alternating cold sweats and fever, vomiting, joint pains and anaemia, coma and death.

Control

insecticides, drain swamps and pools, drugs against the parasite stages, Tilapia fish to eat larvae, repellents, long sleeves, nets.

Cholera, typhoid, gonorrhoea

Cholera, typhoid, gonorrhoea (6/6)

Treatment with quinine and modern drugs, and travellers take preventive drugs. Hard for the immune system as the parasite hides in red cells. Only one poorly effective vaccine as of 2016.

Defence: immunity

Microorganisms

Defence: immunity

Defence: immunity (1/4)

Active (own antibodies)

Natural: Infection; Artificial: Vaccination

Passive (antibodies from elsewhere)

Natural: Across placenta or in milk; Artificial: Injection of antibodies

Vaccination Gives antigens without the disease, usually by injection.

Defence: immunity

Defence: immunity (2/4)

Types

harmless strain (polio), dead microorganisms (typhoid), modified toxins (tetanus), antigens alone (flu), genetically modified harmless bacteria carrying antigens (hepatitis B).

Boosters maintain immunity. Smallpox was eradicated: last case 1977, declared free in 1980.

General defence is phagocytosis. Specific response: lymphocytes have receptor proteins that bind antigens on a pathogen. Activated lymphocytes divide and make antibodies. Some become memory cells.

Defence: immunity

Defence: immunity (3/4)

Antibodies are Y-shaped proteins, specific to one antigen. They neutralise bacteria and toxins, mark bacteria for phagocytes, cause clumping, and mark virus-infected or cancer cells.

B-lymphocytes make antibodies. T-lymphocytes destroy infected and cancer cells, T-helper cells help them (HIV attacks these).

Memory cells last for years, not the antibodies, and give a faster, stronger secondary immune response.

Defence: immunity

Defence: immunity (4/4)

Active lasts longer because of memory cells. Natural passive protects babies until their immune system works. Artificial passive gives immediate protection, for example after exposure to measles.

Antibiotics

Microorganisms

Antibiotics

Antibiotics (1/3)

Penicillin

Action: Stops cell wall being made; Result: Water enters by osmosis, cell bursts, bactericidal

Nalidixic acid

Action: Stops DNA replication; Result: Cells cannot divide, bacteriostatic

Tetracycline

Action: Stops protein synthesis; Result: No enzymes, bactericidal

Antibiotics

Antibiotics (2/3)

Resistance and MRSA A random mutation makes a bacterium resistant. Where antibiotics are used heavily, it survives and multiplies, as bacteria divide very fast (72 generations in a day at 20 minutes).

MRSA (methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus) is a hospital super bug. Bacteria become resistant, people become immune. Doctors now prescribe antibiotics less, and not for viral colds and flu.

Chemicals that kill or slow microorganisms, mostly bacteria, a few fungi. They do not work on viruses.

Antibiotics

Antibiotics (3/3)

Natural ones come from bacteria and fungi to compete. Penicillin (from the fungus Penicillium) was discovered in 1929 and first used in the 1940s. Streptomycin and chloramphenicol are from bacteria.

Semi-synthetic and fully synthetic ones exist.

Bactericidal kills, bacteriostatic stops reproducing. Broad spectrum acts on many bacteria.

Antibiotics

Practical: antibiotics on bacterial growth (1/2)

Spread a harmless bacterium on a sterile nutrient agar plate. Using sterilised forceps, place paper discs with different antibiotics or concentrations, and a control disc with no antibiotic.

Open the lid briefly. Tape the lid on but do not seal fully, label it, incubate at about 30 °C for 48 hours without opening.

Antibiotics

Practical: antibiotics on bacterial growth (2/2)

Measure the clear zone around each disc. A bigger diameter means a more effective antibiotic. The control shows no clear area. Can also test disinfectants, mouthwashes and antiseptics.

Safety

avoid skin contact, handle discs with forceps, wash hands.

Decomposers

Microorganisms

Decomposers

Decomposers

Fungi and bacteria in the soil break down dead remains and waste into simple substances, recycling elements.

Carbon cycle

respiration puts carbon dioxide in air, photosynthesis uses it, decomposers release carbon dioxide from dead matter.

Nitrogen

proteins are broken down into ammonia and then nitrates, which plants need to make proteins. Used in sewage treatment.

12

Sewage treatment

Microorganisms

Sewage treatment

Sewage treatment (1/3)

Pit latrines Without sewers, a pit latrine is a 1 to 2 m trench where microorganisms break down waste. The ventilated improved pit (VIP) is at least 3 m deep with a vent pipe covered by a fly screen.

The pipe acts as a chimney, drawing air through, flies are drawn up the pipe and trapped, then die, so cannot spread disease. Earth, sand or sawdust reduces smell.

Sewage is wet waste from homes, factories and farms. If untreated it spreads pathogens (cholera) and aerobic bacteria use up oxygen so fish die.

Sewage treatment

Sewage treatment (2/3)

Percolating filter method

screening, settling tank, sewage sprayed over a filter bed where aerobic bacteria, fungi and protozoa oxidise the organic matter, then effluent released.

Activated sludge method

screening, settling, aeration tank with air pumped in for aerobic bacteria, sedimentation tank, some sludge returned to aeration tank, effluent released.

Sewage treatment

Sewage treatment (3/3)

Both depend on aerobic organisms. Fungi and bacteria convert protein to ammonia and nitrates, protozoa and worms feed on microbes.

Sludge

anaerobic digestion in a fermentation tank by methanogens produces biogas (methane and carbon dioxide) for fuel, leftover solid is fertiliser or landfill.
