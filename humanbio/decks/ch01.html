DNA, genes and chromosomes

DNA, genes and chromosomes (2/2)

  1. DNA is found in the nucleus in chromosomes. Genes control a cell by deciding which proteins it makes, including enzymes.
  2. DNA is two strands of nucleotides coiled in a double helix.
  3. Each nucleotide has deoxyribose sugar, a phosphate and a base.
  4. Bases: adenine (A), thymine (T), cytosine (C), guanine (G).
  5. Base-pairing rule: A with T, C with G (complementary bases).
  6. Strands are held by weak hydrogen bonds, easily broken so strands separate.

DNA, genes and chromosomes

DNA replication

  1. Before a cell divides it copies each DNA molecule.
  2. The strands separate. Each acts as a template for a new strand.
  3. Enzyme DNA polymerase assembles the new strand from nucleotides.
  4. Two identical molecules form, each with one old and one new strand. DNA is the only chemical that can copy itself.

DNA, genes and chromosomes

The genetic code

  1. Only one strand, the template strand, codes for protein.
  2. Proteins are chains of amino acids. Three bases code for one amino acid, so it is a triplet code. TGT codes for cysteine.
  3. A gene is the base sequence for all the amino acids of a protein.
  4. The code is universal across organisms.
  5. Proteins can be enzymes, structural (keratin, myosin) or functional (haemoglobin, some hormones).

Protein synthesis

Protein synthesis (2/3)

Transcription

In the nucleus the DNA unzips. RNA nucleotides pair with the template strand (G-C, C-G, T-A, A-U) to form mRNA. mRNA leaves through nuclear pores. DNA zips up.

Translation

At a ribosome, mRNA codons (three bases) are matched by the anticodons of tRNA, each carrying its amino acid. Start codon AUG codes for methionine. Amino acids join in a polypeptide until a stop codon releases it.

  1. 20 amino acids but more than 20 codons, because some use more than one, e.g. GGU, GGC, GGA, GGG all code for glycine.

Mutations

Mutations

Mutations (1/2)

Mutation

A random change in the DNA, usually a mistake in replication, that changes the base sequence and may change the amino acid sequence and protein.

Duplication and deletion

A nucleotide inserted twice or lost shifts every following triplet, so the whole gene gives a different protein.

Substitution

One nucleotide replaced. One triplet changes and may or may not change the amino acid.

Inversion

The order of bases in a triplet is reversed. Effects like substitution.

Cell division

Cells

Cell division

Cell division

  1. Human body cells have 46 chromosomes in 23 homologous pairs: diploid. Sex cells have 23: haploid.
  2. Mitosis makes all cells except sex cells, for growth and replacing damaged cells. Meiosis makes gametes (Chapter 11).
  3. A zygote is the cell formed when gametes join at fertilisation.
Spec 1.2 Describe the functions of the cell structures
Cell division

Mitosis

  1. DNA replicates first, forming two chromatids per chromosome.
  2. Prophase: chromatids visible, joined at a centromere, nuclear membrane breaks down.
  3. Metaphase: spindle forms, chromosomes line up at the equator.
  4. Anaphase: spindle fibres shorten and pull chromatids to opposite poles.
  5. Telophase: two new nuclei form and the cytoplasm divides.
  6. Result: two genetically identical diploid daughter cells, a clone. Used for growth, repair, cloning and asexual reproduction.
Exam tipRoot tip cells of an onion are used to see mitosis because plant cells are large and clear.

Differentiation, tissues and organs

Differentiation, tissues and organs

Differentiation, tissues and organs (1/2)

  1. A zygote divides by mitosis into an embryo. Cells become specialised under gene control: differentiation. All cells hold the same genes but switch different genes on and off to make different proteins.
  2. Examples: nerve cell (long axon), smooth muscle, ciliated epithelium (cilia move mucus), white blood cell (changes shape), red blood cell (haemoglobin), sperm (tail and head with genes).
  3. An adaptation is a structure suited to a function.
Tissue

A group of similar cells with one function. An organ is a group of different tissues working together.

Stem cells

Cells

Stem cells

Stem cells

Stem cell

A cell that can divide many times by mitosis without differentiating and later become specialised.

Embryonic stem cells

From early embryos, can become any cell type. Possible treatments for diabetes, Parkinson's and nerve damage but still experimental. Ethical objections because embryos are used.

Adult stem cells

Found in bone marrow, skin and intestine lining. Limited to a few cell types. Bone marrow transplants treat leukaemia and replace cells lost in chemotherapy, used for over 30 years.

Genetic engineering

Genetic engineering

Genetic engineering

Recombinant DNA

DNA made by cutting a gene from one species and inserting it into another. The receiving organism is transgenic.

  1. Bacteria have a chromosome plus small circular plasmids that can be swapped.
  2. Restriction enzymes cut DNA at a specific base sequence (e.g. GAATTC on both strands). Staggered cuts give sticky ends.
  3. Ligase joins cut ends.
  4. The plasmid is a vector. Steps: isolate plasmids, cut the gene from donor DNA, open plasmid with the same enzyme, mix with ligase, bacteria take up the plasmid, culture in fermenters.

Genetic engineering

Genetically modified plants

  1. Every cell must receive the gene, then whole plants are grown from a few cells.
  2. Agrobacterium is a soil bacterium vector that inserts plasmids into plant cells. It does not infect cereals, so a gene gun fires DNA-coated gold pellets.
  3. Golden rice has three genes (two from daffodil, one from a bacterium) to make beta-carotene, converted to vitamin A, to prevent blindness. Ethical and environmental objections exist.
  4. Potatoes, bananas, lettuce, carrots and tobacco have been engineered to make hepatitis B proteins for vaccines.
