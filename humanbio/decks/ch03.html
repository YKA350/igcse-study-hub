Carbohydrates, lipids and proteins

Carbohydrates

  1. Monosaccharide: a single sugar unit that cannot be broken down, e.g. glucose (C6H12O6) and fructose (in fruit).
  2. Disaccharide: two monosaccharides joined. Sucrose = glucose + fructose. Lactose (milk sugar) = glucose + galactose.
  3. Polysaccharide: long chain of monosaccharides. Starch is a polymer of hundreds of glucose units, found in plants (rice, potatoes, bread, pasta).
  4. Glycogen is a similar glucose polysaccharide in animal liver and muscle, an energy store.
  5. In carbohydrates, hydrogen and oxygen are often in the ratio 2 : 1.

Carbohydrates, lipids and proteins

Lipids

  1. Lipids are fats and oils. A fat is solid at room temperature, an oil is liquid. Fats are more common in animals, oils in plants.
  2. Same three elements as carbohydrates but much less oxygen.
  3. A lipid is one glycerol molecule joined to three fatty acids. Many different fatty acids give the variety of lipids, e.g. tristearin in meat.
Spec 2.1 Know the chemical elements present in carbohydrates, proteins and lipids
Carbohydrates, lipids and proteins

Proteins

  1. Proteins are chains of 20 different amino acids. All contain C, H, O and N, and two contain sulfur.
  2. Chains fold into shapes or spirals held by cross-links.
  3. The order of amino acids sets the shape, and shape sets function.
  4. Examples: collagen and keratin (structural), enzymes and haemoglobin.

Food tests (2/2)

  1. Glucose is a reducing sugar because it reduces alkaline copper(II) sulfate to copper(I) oxide. All monosaccharides and some disaccharides, e.g. lactose, are reducing. Sucrose is not and stays blue.
  2. Always use a water bath for the glucose test, never heat the tube directly. Wear eye protection.

Enzymes (1/3)

Enzyme

A protein that acts as a biological catalyst, speeding up a reaction without being used up.

Metabolism

The sum of all the chemical (metabolic) reactions in a cell.

Spec 2.6, 2.7 Explain the role of enzymes as biological catalysts in metabolic reactions
Enzymes

Enzymes (2/3)

  1. Genes control enzyme production and enzymes catalyse reactions, so what a cell does depends on which enzymes it can make.
  2. Body temperature is low (37 °C) so without enzymes reactions would be far too slow.
  3. Thousands of enzymes exist because protein shapes vary hugely.
Substrate

The molecule an enzyme acts on. It fits the active site, a small area on the enzyme's surface.

Spec 2.6, 2.7 Explain the role of enzymes as biological catalysts in metabolic reactions
Enzymes

Enzymes (3/3)

  1. Lock and key model: the substrate fits the active site like a key in a lock, so each enzyme is specific to one reaction.
  2. Binding lowers the energy needed. Products leave and the enzyme can be reused.
  3. Enzymes can also build large molecules from smaller ones.
  4. Digestive enzymes work outside cells (extracellular). Most are intracellular.

Factors affecting enzymes

Biological molecules

Factors affecting enzymes

Factors affecting enzymes (1/3)

Temperature

Rate rises to an optimum (about 37 °C in humans) because molecules have more kinetic energy and collide more. Above about 40 °C the enzyme is denatured: its active site changes shape and the substrate no longer fits. Denaturing is permanent, like a boiled egg white.

pH

Most enzymes have an optimum pH near 7. Away from it the enzyme shape and active site change. Pepsin in the stomach works at low pH.

Factors affecting enzymes (2/3)

Substrate concentration

Rate increases as more active sites fill, then levels off when all are saturated. Enzyme concentration is then the limiting factor.

Competitive inhibitor

Similar shape to the substrate, sits in the active site temporarily and blocks it. Its effect is reduced by a high substrate concentration.

Non-competitive inhibitor

Binds elsewhere and changes the shape of the whole enzyme including the active site. More substrate does not help. Heavy metal ions (Ag, Hg, Pb) act this way.

Temperature and pH

Heating

More kinetic energy, more collisions. Too hot: active site changes shape, substrate no longer fits. Denatured, permanently.

pH

Each enzyme has an optimum pH, e.g. pepsin pH 2, amylase pH 7. Wrong pH changes the active site.

Common mistakeCold does not denature enzymes. It only slows them.

Practicals

Temperature and amylase (1/2)

  1. Amylase breaks starch to maltose. Drops of iodine are put on a spotting tile.
  2. Starch and amylase are warmed separately in a water bath at a set temperature (20 to 60 °C) then mixed.
  3. A sample is added to iodine every 30 seconds until it stays yellow, showing all starch is used.
  4. Rate = volume of starch divided by time taken, e.g. 5 cm3 in 3.5 minutes.

pH and catalase

  1. Catalase breaks down toxic hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen.
  2. Potato is blended with water, extract is mixed with a buffer (resists pH change) then hydrogen peroxide is added.

Immobilised enzymes

Biological molecules

Immobilised enzymes

Immobilised enzymes (1/3)

Immobilised enzyme

An enzyme attached to, or trapped in, an insoluble material.

  1. Enzymes are used in biological detergents, converting starch to glucose, preservatives, clearing fruit juice, detecting blood glucose and measuring urea.
  2. Advantages: more stable at high temperature, resistant to pH change and solvents, products are uncontaminated, the enzyme can be reused and used in columns for continuous processes.
  3. Methods: attach to porous glass or cellulose, or trap in nylon or alginate gel beads.

Immobilised enzymes

Immobilised enzymes (2/3)

Lactose-free milk

Lactase breaks lactose into glucose and galactose. Lactose-intolerant people lack lactase and get cramps, nausea and diarrhoea. Immobilised lactase in porous beads in a column converts the milk and is reused, cutting cost.

Sucrose to invert syrup

Invertase from yeast, trapped in alginate beads, breaks sucrose into glucose and fructose for sweetening food and drinks.

Immobilised enzymes (3/3)

Glucose test strips

Glucose oxidase converts glucose to gluconic acid and hydrogen peroxide. Peroxidase then uses the peroxide to turn a colourless substance into a coloured one. The colour shows glucose level in blood or urine. Specific to glucose.

Practical: immobilising lactase
