A balanced diet

A balanced diet

A balanced diet

Balanced diet

A diet with enough of every nutrient in the correct proportions to keep you healthy.

Food gives fuel for energy, materials for growth and repair, and helps prevent disease.

A diet needs carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, minerals, vitamins, dietary fibre and water.

Carbohydrates, lipids and proteins

Nutrition and energy

Carbohydrates, lipids and proteins

Carbohydrates, lipids and proteins (1/4)

Carbohydrates About 1% of body mass but the main fuel, broken down as glucose in respiration. Sugars: glucose, fructose, lactose, sucrose (table sugar, moved through plant stems).

Most carbohydrate in the diet is starch (potato, rice, wheat, bread, pasta). Aim for about 50% of daily energy, under 5% from sugars. Hidden sugar is in cola (27 g, about 7 teaspoons) and baked beans.

Carbohydrates, lipids and proteins

Carbohydrates, lipids and proteins (2/4)

Fibre (cellulose) Humans cannot digest it as the gut lacks the enzyme. It gives the gut muscles something to push against, avoids constipation and helps prevent colitis and bowel cancer. About 30 g a day.

Lipids About 10% of body mass. Part of every cell, long-term energy store under skin and around heart and kidneys, insulation, and protect organs. Under 35% of energy and less than a third saturated.

Carbohydrates, lipids and proteins

Carbohydrates, lipids and proteins (3/4)

Too much cholesterol or saturated fat is linked to heart disease. Saturated lipids have no double bonds, mostly animal. Unsaturated, in plant oils, are healthier.

Proteins About 18% of body mass, second after water. For growth and repair. Rich in meat, fish, cheese, eggs, beans, peas, nuts. Humans need 20 amino acids and make 10, so 10 are essential in the diet.

Recommended 0.75 g per kg body weight per day.

Carbohydrates, lipids and proteins

Carbohydrates, lipids and proteins (4/4)

Kwashiorkor is caused by lack of protein

swollen belly (fluid in tissues), weight loss, poor muscle growth, weakness, flaky skin. Common in poorer countries.

A (0.8 mg)

Minerals and vitamins

Minerals and vitamins (3/5)

B group

Use: Cell respiration; Deficiency: B1 beri-beri, B3 pellagra; Sources: Cereals, yeast, liver

C (80 mg)

Use: Makes connective tissue to bind cells; Deficiency: Scurvy: wounds do not heal, gums bleed; Sources: Fresh fruit and vegetables

D (5 micrograms)

Use: Helps bones absorb calcium; Deficiency: Rickets, poor teeth; Sources: Fish liver oils, made in skin

Minerals and vitamins

Minerals and vitamins (4/5)

Vitamins

Chemicals needed in small amounts to stay healthy. Shown by experiments with rats fed pure carbohydrate, lipid and protein, which only grew with a little milk added.

Lack of calcium or vitamin D causes rickets: soft, deformed bones. A one-year-old needs about 0.6 g calcium daily.

Lack of iron causes anaemia

too little haemoglobin, so tiredness and lack of energy. A 16-year-old needs about 12 mg iron.

Minerals and vitamins

Minerals and vitamins (5/5)

Scurvy cure

James Lind (1753) used oranges and lemons, Captain Cook kept sailors healthy with fresh fruit, and by 1804 British sailors drank lime juice.

Vitamin A deficiency

children go blind and many die as they get severe infections like measles.

Only vitamins A, C and D need detailed knowledge.

Minerals and vitamins

Practical: vitamin C in lemon juice (1/2)

Vitamin C removes the blue colour of DCPIP.

Add 1% DCPIP drop by drop to 2 cm3 of 1% vitamin C until the blue stays. Note the volume. Repeat with lemon juice.

Compare volumes decolourised. Repeat for averages. A graduated pipette is more precise. Acidic juice ends at pale pink. For solids grind with distilled water.

Test ideas

fresh versus old juice, lemons versus oranges, effect of cooking heat.

Energy from food

Nutrition and energy

Energy from food

Energy from food (1/2)

Measured in kilojoules (kJ). 1 g carbohydrate gives about 17 kJ, lipid about 39 kJ (more than twice), protein about 18 kJ. Foods high in lipid (butter, nuts) contain most energy. Fruit and vegetables are mostly water.

Energy needs depend on age, size, activity, climate (more in cold), pregnancy (extra weight), breast-feeding (milk) and manual work. Men need more than women as they are heavier.

Younger women need extra iron (periods), breast-feeding women need more calcium.

Energy from food

Energy from food (2/2)

Practical

weigh the food, put 20 cm3 water in a boiling tube, record temperature, burn the food under it until it stops, stir and record the final temperature.

1 g water needs 4.2 J per °C and 1 cm3 water is 1 g. Do not use nuts (allergies). Safety: eye protection, spear downward onto a board.

Teeth

Nutrition and energy

Teeth

Teeth (1/3)

Tooth decay Bacteria feed on sugar and make acid that dissolves enamel, then dentine, forming a cavity. Reaching the pulp causes pain. Periodontal disease inflames and bleeds the gums.

Dental care Plaque is a bacterial layer that re-forms in 24 hours, so brush twice a day, floss, and check up every 6 months. Plaque hardens into tartar, removed by a dentist.

Fluoride strengthens enamel. Avoid sugary food and drink.

Teeth

Teeth (2/3)

Only the lower jaw moves. Four types

incisors (chisel-shaped, bite), canines (similar in humans), premolars and molars (flat crowns that chew and crush). Arrangement is dentition.

Structure

enamel (hardest substance in the body), dentine (as hard as bone), pulp cavity with blood vessels and nerves, cement with fibres anchoring the tooth in the jawbone but allowing slight movement.

Teeth

Teeth (3/3)

Two sets

milk teeth from a few months (20 by about 21 months), permanent teeth from about 7 years, full set of 32.

Digestion

Nutrition and energy

Digestion

Digestion (1/2)

Digestion

The process that converts large insoluble molecules into small soluble molecules that can be absorbed into the blood.

End products

sugars, fatty acids, glycerol and amino acids.

Digestion

Digestion (2/2)

Digestive enzymes are made by gut tissue and glands and pass into the gut.

Mechanical digestion is physical breakdown: chewing, and the stomach churning. Chewing increases surface area.

Digestion

Peristalsis

The gut wall has circular muscle and longitudinal muscle.

When circular muscle contracts and longitudinal relaxes, the gut narrows. The opposite makes it widen.

Waves of contraction push food along like squeezing toothpaste. Works against gravity. Fibre gives the muscles something to push on.

Digestion

The digestive system (3/4)

Bile Made in the liver, stored in the gall bladder, passed down the bile duct to the duodenum. It has no enzymes.

It emulsifies lipid into tiny droplets, giving more surface area for lipase, and is alkaline, neutralising stomach acid.

Gut is about 8 m long. Mouth, oesophagus, stomach, small intestine (duodenum then ileum), large intestine (colon, rectum), anus. Pancreas and liver make juices.

Digestion

The digestive system (4/4)

The stomach holds food for hours. It secretes hydrochloric acid (kills bacteria, optimum pH about 2 for pepsin). A sphincter muscle releases food into the duodenum.

The colon absorbs water leaving faeces, stored in the rectum. Egestion is removal of faeces, not excretion.

Absorption in the ileum

Nutrition and energy

Absorption in the ileum

Absorption in the ileum (1/2)

Huge surface area from length, folds and villi (millions, about 300 m2 in total, a tennis court). Microvilli on surface cells add area.

Each villus has blood capillaries and a lacteal. Most digested food enters the blood, fat digestion products enter the lacteal (lymph).

Absorption in the ileum

Absorption in the ileum (2/2)

Surface is a single epithelium cell layer, so a short diffusion distance. Many mitochondria supply energy for active transport. Muscle fibres keep villi moving to keep a steep gradient.

Blood goes to the liver by the hepatic portal vein. The liver processes and stores, e.g. glucose to glycogen.

Food hygiene

Nutrition and energy

Food hygiene

Food hygiene (1/2)

Food-borne bacteria

Salmonella enterica (food poisoning), Salmonella typhi (typhoid), Listeria monocytogenes (listeriosis), Clostridium botulinum (botulism, toxin, can be fatal). Microorganisms also spoil food.

Prevent transmission

keep microorganisms out, slow their growth (preservation), cook food properly.

Food hygiene

Food hygiene (2/2)

Packaged foods show display until and use by dates.

At home

store raw and cooked food apart, cook and eat thawed food straight away and do not re-freeze, do not leave food open to air and flies.

Food hygiene

Food preservation (1/3)

Salting

High salt makes bacterial cells lose water by osmosis and die

Pickling

Vinegar (ethanoic acid) gives a low pH that inactivates enzymes

Pasteurisation

63 °C for 30 min or 71.5 °C for 15 s, then cooled to 10 °C. Kills bacteria but not spores, cooling stops spores growing. Milk, juice, beer

Food hygiene

Food preservation (2/3)

UHT

Superheated steam up to 160 °C for 2 s kills bacteria and spores. Milk

Canning

Sealed cans heated to 105 to 160 °C kill bacteria and spores, cans keep new bacteria out

Drying

Hot air removes water so bacteria cannot feed

Food hygiene

Food preservation (3/3)

Freezing

-12 to -18 °C stops bacteria multiplying. Rapid freezing avoids ice crystals changing texture

Irradiation

Gamma radiation kills all bacteria and spores but not toxins already made
