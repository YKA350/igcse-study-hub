Stimulus and response

Stimulus and response (1/2)

Stimulus

A change in the environment. A response is a reaction to that change.

Chain

stimulus to receptor to coordination (nervous system) to effector to response.

Stimulus and response

Stimulus and response (2/2)

Effectors are muscles and glands. Information travels as small electrical nerve impulses.

External environment is outside the body, internal environment is inside, such as temperature and blood glucose.

Receptor

Detects a stimulus by turning its energy into electrical nerve impulses.

A sense has several parts

touching a warm surface stimulates touch, temperature and stretch receptors. Each sense detects different aspects: ears detect loudness and frequency, eyes detect brightness and colour.

Receptors

Practical: distribution of touch receptors (1/2)

A blindfolded subject is touched on the skin with one or two points of a U-shaped paper clip 2 cm apart, randomly ten times, and says one or two.

Repeat with the points closer together and on other skin areas such as fingertips, palm and back of arm.

Receptors

Practical: distribution of touch receptors (2/2)

Areas with more touch receptors (fingertips) can tell the points apart at smaller distances, a smaller threshold distance.

Safety

blunt clip, no sharp points.

The eye

The eye (4/5)

Rods work in dim light and see black and white. Cones need bright light and give colour: three types respond to red, green and blue, equal stimulation gives white. Cones are concentrated in the fovea and give a sharper image.

Light is refracted by the cornea and lens, giving an inverted image on the retina, which the brain turns upright.

Two eyes cancel blind spots, widen the field of view and give stereoscopic (3D) vision, helping judge distance, depth and speed.

The eye

The eye (5/5)

Humans can see form, colour and brightness, while snails only detect light.

The eye

The iris reflex

Bright light

circular muscles contract, radial relax, the pupil constricts. Dim light: radial contract, circular relax, the pupil dilates.

Pathway

light intensity (stimulus), retina (receptor), optic nerve, unconscious brain, iris muscles (effector), pupil size changes.

It is automatic and protects rods and cones from damage while letting enough light in to form an image.

Accommodation

The changes in the eye that let it focus on objects at different distances, done by changing the shape of the lens.

Eye pressure from the watery fluid pushes outwards. The eye at rest is focused for distance.

Defects of vision

Sensory receptors: the eye and the ear

Defects of vision

Defects of vision (1/3)

Cause

Long sight: Lens too flat or eyeball too short; Short sight: Lens too convex or eyeball too long

Problem

Long sight: Near objects focus behind the retina; Short sight: Distant objects focus in front of the retina

Difficulty

Long sight: Seeing near objects; Short sight: Seeing distant objects

Defects of vision

Defects of vision (2/3)

Correction

Long sight: Convex lenses converge light; Short sight: Concave lenses diverge light

Astigmatism

cornea or lens is rounder in one direction than another, giving blur in one direction. Regular type is corrected by glasses, irregular type needs contact lenses.

Defects of vision

Defects of vision (3/3)

Cataract

lens becomes cloudy and opaque, common in the elderly, cannot be fixed by glasses, treated by surgery replacing the lens with an artificial one.

Corneal transplant

a diseased cornea is replaced by a donor cornea. Highly successful, with low rejection, and plastic artificial corneas are used if needed.

The ear

Sensory receptors: the eye and the ear

The ear

The ear (1/4)

Balance The semicircular canals and the vestibule (with utricle and saccule) contain fluid and sensory hair cells that detect movement and position of the head and send impulses to the brain.

Noise damage Prolonged loud sound damages the sensory hair cells in the cochlea. They do not regenerate, so hearing is lost permanently, especially at high frequencies.

Outer ear directs sound to the eardrum in the auditory canal.

The ear

The ear (2/4)

Middle ear

three ossicles (malleus, incus, stapes) amplify the vibrations and pass them to the oval window.

The Eustachian tube links the middle ear to the throat and equalises pressure either side of the eardrum, as in ears popping in a plane.

The ear

The ear (3/4)

Inner ear

the cochlea is fluid-filled. The organ of Corti has receptor cells with sensory hairs that are stretched by the vibrations of the fluid, producing nerve impulses in the auditory nerve.

The round window vibrates in the opposite phase to release pressure.

The ear

The ear (4/4)

Pitch

hair cells near the oval window detect high frequencies, near the round window low frequencies. Loudness: larger amplitude vibrations give more impulses per second.

The ear

Practical: frequency range

Connect a signal generator to a loudspeaker and an oscilloscope. Frequency is measured in hertz (Hz) and generators give 1 to 100 000 Hz.

Alter the frequency to find the lowest and highest a person can hear. Speech is a few hundred hertz. Avoid very loud or prolonged sounds as some people feel sick.
