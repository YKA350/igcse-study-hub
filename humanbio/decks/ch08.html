Stimulus and response

Stimulus and response

Stimulus

A change in the environment. A response is a reaction to that change.

  1. Chain: stimulus to receptor to coordination (nervous system) to effector to response.
  2. Effectors are muscles and glands. Information travels as small electrical nerve impulses.
  3. External environment is outside the body, internal environment is inside, such as temperature and blood glucose.

Practical: distribution of touch receptors

  1. A blindfolded subject is touched on the skin with one or two points of a U-shaped paper clip 2 cm apart, randomly ten times, and says one or two. Repeat with the points closer together and on other skin areas such as fingertips, palm and back of arm.

The eye

The eye (3/3)

  1. Rods work in dim light and see black and white. Cones need bright light and give colour: three types respond to red, green and blue, equal stimulation gives white. Cones are concentrated in the fovea and give a sharper image.
  2. Light is refracted by the cornea and lens, giving an inverted image on the retina, which the brain turns upright.
  3. Two eyes cancel blind spots, widen the field of view and give stereoscopic (3D) vision, helping judge distance, depth and speed.
  4. Humans can see form, colour and brightness, while snails only detect light.

The eye

The iris reflex

  1. Bright light: circular muscles contract, radial relax, the pupil constricts. Dim light: radial contract, circular relax, the pupil dilates.
  2. Pathway: light intensity (stimulus), retina (receptor), optic nerve, unconscious brain, iris muscles (effector), pupil size changes. It is automatic and protects rods and cones from damage while letting enough light in to form an image.

Bright light vs dim light

A reflex

It is automatic and fast, so you do not think about it.

Why it helps

In bright light a small pupil protects the retina. In dim light a large pupil lets in more light so you can see.

Defects of vision

Defects of vision (2/2)

  1. Astigmatism: cornea or lens is rounder in one direction than another, giving blur in one direction. Regular type is corrected by glasses, irregular type needs contact lenses.
  2. Cataract: lens becomes cloudy and opaque, common in the elderly, cannot be fixed by glasses, treated by surgery replacing the lens with an artificial one.
  3. Corneal transplant: a diseased cornea is replaced by a donor cornea. Highly successful, with low rejection, and plastic artificial corneas are used if needed.

The ear

Sensory receptors: the eye and the ear

The ear

The ear (1/2)

  1. Outer ear directs sound to the eardrum in the auditory canal.
  2. Middle ear: three ossicles (malleus, incus, stapes) amplify the vibrations and pass them to the oval window. The Eustachian tube links the middle ear to the throat and equalises pressure either side of the eardrum, as in ears popping in a plane.
  3. Inner ear: the cochlea is fluid-filled. The organ of Corti has receptor cells with sensory hairs that are stretched by the vibrations of the fluid, producing nerve impulses in the auditory nerve. The round window vibrates in the opposite phase to release pressure.
  4. Pitch: hair cells near the oval window detect high frequencies, near the round window low frequencies. Loudness: larger amplitude vibrations give more impulses per second.

The ear

The ear (2/2)

Balance

The semicircular canals and the vestibule (with utricle and saccule) contain fluid and sensory hair cells that detect movement and position of the head and send impulses to the brain.

Noise damage

Prolonged loud sound damages the sensory hair cells in the cochlea. They do not regenerate, so hearing is lost permanently, especially at high frequencies.

The ear · diagram

Parts of the ear

Outer ear

Pinna and ear canal funnel sound to the eardrum, which vibrates.

Middle ear

Three tiny bones (ossicles) pass the vibrations on and make them bigger.

Inner ear

The cochlea turns vibrations into nerve impulses for the auditory nerve. The semicircular canals help balance.
