Homeostasis

Homeostasis (2/2)

  1. Internal environment is blood and tissue fluid, which is like plasma without plasma proteins.
  2. Water balance: gained by drinking, food and respiration, lost in urine, sweat, faeces and breath. About 1.5 dm3 of urine a day.
  3. Also controlled: carbon dioxide, blood pH, glucose and body temperature.
  4. Importance: too many solutes make cells lose water by osmosis, too dilute makes them swell. Wrong pH or temperature affects enzymes. Waste such as urea must not build up.
  5. Relies on negative feedback: change detected, correction made, switched off when normal.

Excretion

Excretion

Excretion (1/2)

Excretion

The removal from the body of the waste products of metabolism.

  1. Excretory products: urea (nitrogenous), carbon dioxide (lungs), water and some salts (skin, urine). Faeces are not excretion: they are undigested food, bacteria and dead cells, so removal is egestion.
  2. Urine contains water, urea (about 20 g per dm3), sodium chloride and other ions such as potassium, phosphate and ammonium.
  3. Why urea: carbohydrate and fat contain only C, H and O and can be stored, but excess protein or amino acids cannot be stored. In the liver they are broken down into carbohydrate (stored as glycogen) and urea, which goes in the blood to the kidneys.

The urinary system

The urinary system

The urinary system

  1. Renal artery brings blood from the aorta at high pressure, renal vein returns blood to the vena cava. Ureters carry urine to the bladder, a muscular bag. The urethra carries it out.
  2. Two sphincter muscles on the urethra: the lower is voluntary, the upper involuntary and relaxes when the bladder is full. A baby has no voluntary control.
  3. Kidney layers: cortex (outer, with blood vessels and nephron start), medulla (tubules, pyramids), and pelvis (collects urine, leads to the ureter).

The nephron (1/3)

  1. About a million nephrons in each kidney. Parts in order: Bowman's capsule with glomerulus, first coiled tubule, loop of Henle (into medulla), second coiled tubule, collecting duct. Also called proximal and distal convoluted tubules.

The nephron

The nephron (2/3)

Ultrafiltration

In the glomerulus the arteriole in is wider than the one out, so pressure is very high. It forces water, ions, glucose, urea and small molecules through the capillary wall, basement membrane (the fine filter) and capsule wall, into the capsule. Blood cells and proteins are held back. The fluid is glomerular filtrate: about 125 cm3 per minute, 180 dm3 per day.

Selective reabsorption

Over 99% of the filtrate is reabsorbed, so only 1.5 dm3 of urine is lost. In the first coiled tubule all glucose and most sodium and chloride ions are reabsorbed by active transport, and water follows by osmosis. More water and ions are reabsorbed in the rest of the tubule, and some solutes such as ammonium ions are secreted into it. Urea stays and becomes more concentrated.

Control of water content

Homeostasis and excretion

Control of water content

Control of water content

  1. ADH is made in the hypothalamus and released from the pituitary. When the blood is too concentrated (sweating, not drinking), osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus detect it and more ADH is released.
  2. ADH makes the collecting ducts more permeable to water, so more is reabsorbed, giving less, more concentrated urine. The hypothalamus also triggers thirst.
  3. When blood is too dilute, less ADH, ducts less permeable, a large volume of dilute urine.
  4. Negative feedback: change detected, correction, switched off when normal.

Kidney failure

Kidney failure (2/2)

Kidney transplant

Kidney from a relative or a person who died. Problem is rejection, reduced by tissue typing, immunosuppressant drugs, and radiation of bone marrow to cut white cells. About 80% survive over 5 years. Best treatment but donors are short.

Renal dialysis

Blood flows past a Visking tubing dialysis membrane in the opposite direction to dialysis fluid (salts and glucose at body concentrations). Urea, extra water and salts diffuse out, blood cells and proteins stay. Fluid constantly replaced, a large membrane area is needed. Patient has an artery joined to a vein for pressure. Takes many hours, two or three times a week, expensive, unpleasant. Only needed until a transplant.

Kidney failure

Practical: Visking tubing

  1. Fill a Visking tubing bag with starch, glucose and protein, rinse, put in a tube of water. After a time test the water: starch with iodine (blue-black), glucose with Benedict's and heating (brick red), protein with potassium hydroxide and copper sulfate (purple, biuret).
  2. Typically only glucose passes through, as starch and protein molecules are too big. Visking tubing is a model of the gut wall or the kidney filter.
  3. Safety: eye protection, water bath for heating.

Control of body temperature

Control of body temperature

Homeotherm

An animal that keeps its body temperature constant (about 37 °C in humans) despite external changes, using physiological change. Mammals and birds are endotherms. Reptiles change with their surroundings but use behaviour.

  1. Advantage: enzyme reactions run at a steady rate and metabolism does not slow in the cold. Too hot denatures enzymes.
  2. Temperature receptors in the skin signal the hypothalamus, which also monitors the blood temperature. This thermoregulatory centre triggers behaviour and physiology: a negative feedback loop.
