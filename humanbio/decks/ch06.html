The circulatory system

Internal transport

The circulatory system

The circulatory system (1/3)

Blood is pumped round a closed circuit of heart and vessels. It carries oxygen from lungs to the body, carbon dioxide to the lungs, nutrients from the gut, and urea from the liver to the kidneys.

It also carries hormones and antibodies, and spreads heat.

The circulatory system

The circulatory system (2/3)

Humans have a double circulation

pulmonary (heart to lungs and back, pulmonary arteries carry deoxygenated blood, pulmonary veins return oxygenated blood) and systemic (heart via the aorta to the body, back through the vena cava).

Blood passes through the heart twice per circuit.

It allows different pressures

higher in the systemic circuit than in the pulmonary one.

The circulatory system

The circulatory system (3/3)

Parts

heart (muscular pump), vessels (arteries away from the heart, veins towards, capillaries link them) and blood.

Two venae cavae

superior (head and arms) and inferior (rest of body).

The hepatic portal vein carries digested food from the intestine to the liver, the hepatic vein returns blood from the liver to the heart. Also vessels to kidneys (renal artery and vein) and lungs.

The heart

Internal transport

The heart

The heart

Two pumps

the right side sends deoxygenated blood to the lungs, the left side sends oxygenated blood to the body.

Wall is cardiac muscle

branching, striated fibres with several nuclei forming a mesh. It never fatigues, contracts about 70 times a minute (over two billion in 70 years) and contracts on its own.

Nerves only adjust the rate. The mesh spreads contraction in a coordinated way.

The heart

The cardiac cycle (1/2)

1: Whole heart in diastole, atria fill with blood

2: Atria contract (atrial systole), pressure forces open the bicuspid and tricuspid valves, blood enters ventricles

3: Ventricles contract (ventricular systole), pressure closes bicuspid and tricuspid valves

The heart

The cardiac cycle (2/2)

4: Pressure forces open the semilunar valves, blood leaves through the pulmonary artery and aorta

5: Ventricles relax, semilunar valves close, atria fill again

Systole is contracting, diastole is relaxing. Both atria contract together, then both ventricles together.

The heart

Adaptations (1/2)

A septum separates the two sides. The left ventricle wall is much thicker as it pumps blood round the whole body at higher pressure.

Valves ensure one-way flow. Bicuspid (mitral) and tricuspid valves are the atrioventricular valves.

Atria have thin stretchy walls.

The heart

Adaptations (2/2)

The coronary circulation supplies the muscle: coronary arteries to capillaries, coronary veins return blood to the right atrium.

Heart rate

Internal transport

Heart rate

Heart rate (1/2)

Exercise needs more oxygen, so heart rate and stroke volume increase. Sleep lowers heart rate.

Nerve control

receptors in the aorta and carotid artery detect extra carbon dioxide, send impulses by the sensory nerve to the cardiac centre in the medulla.

Heart rate

Heart rate (2/2)

The medulla sends impulses along the accelerator nerve to speed up and strengthen the beat (raising blood pressure), and along the decelerator nerve to slow it when carbon dioxide returns to normal.

Both are reflex actions.

Hormone control

adrenaline from the adrenal glands, in fear or anger (fight or flight), raises heart rate and diverts blood to muscles.

Arteries, veins and capillaries

Internal transport

Arteries, veins and capillaries

Arteries, veins and capillaries (1/5)

Direction

Artery: Away from heart; Vein: Towards heart; Capillary: Through organs

Pressure

Artery: High; Vein: Very low; Capillary: Falls

Wall

Artery: Thick, elastic tissue and smooth muscle; Vein: Thin, few fibres; Capillary: One cell thick

Arteries, veins and capillaries

Arteries, veins and capillaries (2/5)

Lumen

Artery: Small; Vein: Large; Capillary: Tiny, red cells just fit

Valves

Artery: Only at aorta start; Vein: Semilunar valves stop backflow; Capillary: None

Pulse

Artery: Yes; Vein: No; Capillary: No

Arteries, veins and capillaries

Arteries, veins and capillaries (3/5)

Pulse

blood spurts from the ventricle stretch the aorta, which recoils when the ventricle relaxes. A wave of stretch and recoil travels along arteries, weakening and vanishing by the capillaries.

It moves faster than the blood itself. Pulse rate equals heart rate. Feel it where an artery crosses bone, as in the wrist.

Arteries, veins and capillaries

Arteries, veins and capillaries (4/5)

Arterioles are small arteries with more muscle and nerve endings. They dilate or constrict to control flow into an organ. Constriction raises resistance and blood pressure.

All arteries carry oxygenated blood except the pulmonary artery and umbilical artery. All veins carry deoxygenated blood except the pulmonary and umbilical veins.

Arteries, veins and capillaries

Arteries, veins and capillaries (5/5)

Capillary walls are thin so exchange of oxygen and nutrients with cells is by diffusion over a short distance, with red cells close to the wall.

Arteries, veins and capillaries

Tissue fluid and lymph (1/2)

Leaky capillary walls let plasma leak out under pressure, forming tissue fluid. It is like plasma without the large proteins and bathes the cells, letting substances diffuse.

Most water returns to capillaries by osmosis. The rest enters lymph capillaries, forming lymph, which returns to the blood. Lacteals in the gut belong to the lymphatic system.

Arteries, veins and capillaries

Tissue fluid and lymph (2/2)

Lymph nodes filter lymph, removing dead cells and bacteria, and contain white blood cells. They swell during infection.

Pulse rate and fitness

Internal transport

Pulse rate and fitness

Pulse rate and fitness (1/2)

Resting heart rate is 40 to 100 bpm, average 70 for men and 75 for women. Training lowers it as the heart pumps more blood per beat. Recovery time after exercise is also a fitness measure.

Practical

count pulse for 15 s and multiply by 4, repeat three times for a resting average.

Pulse rate and fitness

Pulse rate and fitness (2/2)

Exercise a standard way (for example 5 minutes running on the spot), then count every 30 s until back to rest. Plot pulse against time with a line for resting rate.

Composition of blood

Composition of blood (1/5)

Plasma

Features: Mostly water; Function: Transport

Red blood cell

Features: Biconcave, no nucleus, haemoglobin; Function: Carry oxygen

Lymphocyte

Features: Large round nucleus; Function: Make antibodies

Composition of blood

Composition of blood (2/5)

Phagocyte

Features: Larger, lobed nucleus; Function: Engulf and digest microorganisms

Platelets

Features: Cell fragments; Function: Start clotting

Red blood cells Made in bone marrow, last about 100 days, destroyed in the spleen. Haemoglobin (iron protein) joins oxygen to form oxyhaemoglobin in the lungs and releases it in tissues.

Composition of blood

Composition of blood (3/5)

No nucleus gives room for more haemoglobin. Biconcave shape gives large surface area to volume and a short diffusion distance. They are flexible to squeeze through capillaries.

White blood cells Defend against pathogens. About 70% are phagocytes that use pseudopodia to engulf a microbe in a vacuole and digest it with enzymes (phagocytosis).

Composition of blood

Composition of blood (4/5)

About 25% are lymphocytes that make antibodies, soluble proteins that recognise antigens on pathogens (immune response). Memory cells give a faster, stronger secondary response and immunity.

Clotting Platelets start a chain reaction at a cut. Prothrombin becomes the enzyme thrombin, which converts soluble fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin fibres.

Composition of blood

Composition of blood (5/5)

They trap red cells and form a clot, then a scab, stopping blood loss and pathogens entering.

Blood is cells in plasma. Plasma is watery and carries cells, nutrients (glucose, amino acids), hormones, urea, heat and carbon dioxide, mainly as hydrogencarbonate ions.

Blood groups and transfusion

Blood groups and transfusion

Blood groups and transfusion (1/3)

A: A and O

B: B and O

AB

A, B, AB and O

Blood groups and transfusion

Blood groups and transfusion (2/3)

O: O only

Blood group depends on A and B antigens on red cells. Groups A, B, AB, O.

Plasma antibodies agglutinate (clump, not clot) cells with matching antigen. A has anti-B, B has anti-A, AB has neither, O has both. People never carry antibodies against their own antigens.

Blood groups and transfusion

Blood groups and transfusion (3/3)

Only donor antigens matter, as donor antibodies are diluted.

Group O is the universal donor (no antigens). AB is the universal recipient (no antibodies). Hospitals use matching groups.

Coronary heart disease

Internal transport

Coronary heart disease

Coronary heart disease (1/4)

Angioplasty and stents A catheter with a balloon and mesh stent is guided to the blockage. The balloon inflates, pushing plaque against the wall and expanding the stent, then is removed.

The stent keeps the artery open. Also used for arteriosclerosis, for example in the carotid arteries.

Coronary heart disease

Coronary heart disease (2/4)

Heart transplants First done in 1967, about 3500 a year, average survival 15 years. Rejection happens as lymphocytes see non-self antigens and make antibodies.

Tissue typing matches antigens, relatives and identical twins match best, and immunosuppressive drugs lower the risk but weaken defence against disease.

Coronary heart disease

Coronary heart disease (3/4)

Artificial hearts Plastic and metal devices, mainly keep patients alive until a transplant. Total artificial hearts have not worked long term. Ventricular assist devices help a failing heart.

Coronary arteries are narrow. Cholesterol deposits cause atherosclerosis, forming plaque that narrows them and may trigger clots. Reduced oxygen to the muscle gives angina (chest pain on exercise).

Coronary heart disease

Coronary heart disease (4/4)

Full blockage causes a heart attack.

Risk factors

heredity, hypertension, diet high in saturated fat and cholesterol, smoking (nicotine raises blood pressure and cholesterol, carbon monoxide cuts oxygen), stress, lack of exercise, diabetes.

Prevention

balanced diet, exercise, healthy weight, not smoking, and managing other conditions.

Exercise and the cardiovascular system

Internal transport

Exercise and the cardiovascular system

Exercise and the cardiovascular system (1/2)

Exercise raises carbon dioxide, causing a reflex rise in heart rate and blood pressure to deliver oxygen. Both stay high during exercise.

Recovery period

rate and pressure fall gradually while lactate formed anaerobically is oxidised, using extra oxygen. A fit heart makes less lactate so recovers faster.

Aerobic exercise (walking, jogging, swimming) uses ATP from aerobic respiration.

Exercise and the cardiovascular system

Exercise and the cardiovascular system (2/2)

It strengthens heart muscle, lowers resting rate and blood pressure, raises red cell count, controls weight, lowers lipids and cholesterol, builds muscle, strengthens tendons, raises vital capacity, boosts immunity, helps blood glucose, reduces some cancers and improves mood.

Weight lifting and sprinting are short high-intensity exercise that builds up lactate.

Other disorders and drugs

Internal transport

Other disorders and drugs

Other disorders and drugs (1/2)

Statins Lower blood cholesterol by inhibiting an enzyme in cholesterol synthesis in the liver. Prevent cardiovascular disease from atherosclerosis, especially in people with high cholesterol but no heart disease.

Plant stanol esters Plant molecules like cholesterol, added to yoghurts and spreads, lower blood cholesterol but have not been shown to affect disease development.

Other disorders and drugs

Other disorders and drugs (2/2)

Beta blockers Block adrenaline at receptors, lowering heart rate and stroke volume so the heart needs less oxygen. Treat angina, abnormal rhythms, heart failure and protect after a heart attack.

Atherosclerosis in other arteries can cause strokes (blood cut off from part of the brain).

Other disorders and drugs

Hypertension (1/2)

Persistently high blood pressure, a risk for CHD, strokes, heart and kidney failure. Measured as systolic over diastolic in mmHg, for example 120/70.

Systolic is from left ventricle contraction, diastolic when ventricles relax. Normal about 100 to 140 systolic and 60 to 90 diastolic.

Other disorders and drugs

Hypertension (2/2)

Causes

genes, high salt, kidney disease, stress, smoking, obesity, drinking. Prevent by healthy diet, exercise, weight control and not smoking.

ACE inhibitors

kidneys make renin, which turns angiotensinogen into angiotensin I (10 amino acids), ACE converts it to angiotensin II (8 amino acids), which constricts vessels and raises blood pressure.

ACE inhibitors block the enzyme so there is no rise.

Monoclonal antibodies

Internal transport

Monoclonal antibodies

Monoclonal antibodies (1/2)

Lab-made antibodies from one clone, each specific to one antigen.

Making

inject antigen into a mouse, take antibody-producing lymphocytes from the spleen, fuse them with cancer cells to make hybridoma cells that divide indefinitely and make antibody.

Select hybridomas in culture, grow single cells into clones, find the clone making the right antibody and culture it.

Monoclonal antibodies

Monoclonal antibodies (2/2)

Detecting cancer

antibodies to cancer antigens, labelled with dye, radioactive or fluorescent tags, show the antigen in tissue samples.

Treating disease

bind antigens on cancer cells and kill them or block signalling. Herceptin binds HER2 receptors on some breast cancer cells, stopping uncontrolled division and marking them for destruction.

Looking ahead: haemoglobin

Internal transport

Looking ahead: haemoglobin

Looking ahead: haemoglobin

The oxygen dissociation curve shows haemoglobin saturation against partial pressure of oxygen. It saturates in the lungs (high pressure) and unloads in respiring tissue (low pressure).

Bohr effect

high carbon dioxide shifts the curve right so haemoglobin has lower affinity and releases more oxygen to tissues. At 4 kPa oxygen, saturation falls from about 73% to about 40%.
