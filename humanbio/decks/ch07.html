The skeleton

The skeleton

The skeleton

  1. Functions: protection (cranium protects brain, eyes and ears, vertebrae the spinal cord, ribcage the heart and lungs), support, movement, and making blood cells. Red cells and platelets are made in bone marrow of the sternum, femur and pelvis.
  2. Axial skeleton: vertebral column, cranium, ribcage, sternum. Appendicular skeleton: scapulas, clavicles, pelvis and limb bones.

Bone structure

Bones, muscles and joints

Bone structure

Bone structure (1/3)

  1. Bone is hard because of calcium salts, mainly calcium phosphate, so it resists bending and compression. Protein fibres stop it being brittle. In acid the salts dissolve and the bone becomes flexible.
  2. Bone is a living organ of cells called osteocytes with blood vessels, so it can repair after breaks.
  3. In the embryo, bones start as cartilage that is replaced by bone, called ossification, nearly complete by birth.
  4. Compact bone is the hard outer layer. Osteocytes sit in rings called Haversian systems round canals holding blood vessels and nerves, secreting calcium phosphate and protein fibres into the matrix.

Bone structure

Bone structure (2/3)

  1. Spongy bone in the middle has spaces filled with marrow, so is lighter. Marrow makes blood cells and stores fat. Large bones have a hollow cavity of marrow.
  2. Cartilage at bone ends cushions joints. It is tough and flexible, with chondrocytes making a matrix of protein fibres. The periosteum is the tough membrane covering the bone.
  3. Ring-like layering resists compression yet allows some side flexibility.

Bone structure

Bone structure (3/3)

Bone growth

Long bones grow at cartilage regions near the ends, which grow and then turn to bone. Growth stops in the late teens but bone structure keeps changing under hormones, diet and exercise. Needs calcium and vitamin D (for calcium absorption). Lack of vitamin D causes rickets.

Osteoporosis

Bones lose calcium salts and become porous and fragile in older people. About a third of bone mass is lost by age 70. Women are more at risk after the menopause because of hormone changes. No cure, but diet, calcium and vitamin D supplements and hormone treatment help.

Joints

Joints (1/2)

Joint

The point where two bones meet, where bones articulate.

  1. A movable joint needs a way to hold bones together, reduce friction and absorb shock.
  2. Synovial joint: synovial membrane secretes oily synovial fluid that lubricates; cartilage on bone ends is a smooth shock absorber; a fibrous capsule surrounds the joint; ligaments connect bone to bone, with high tensile strength and some elasticity, preventing dislocation.

Inside a synovial joint

Cartilage

Smooth, slightly springy layer on the bone ends. Stops bones rubbing and absorbs shocks.

Synovial fluid

Oily liquid made by the membrane. It lubricates the joint so it moves freely.

Ligaments

Tough, slightly stretchy bands that hold bone to bone.

Muscles and movement

Muscles and movement

  1. Skeletal (voluntary) muscle is attached to bones by tendons and moves them by contracting and pulling. Tendons have high tensile strength and are inelastic, so they do not stretch when muscle contracts. Ligaments join bone to bone and are fairly elastic.
  2. Muscles can only pull, not push. They work in antagonistic pairs: one contracts while the other relaxes and is stretched.
  3. Biceps contracts to flex (bend) the arm at the elbow, triceps contracts to extend (straighten) it. The origin is the attachment to the stationary bone, the insertion to the moving bone.
  4. Other pairs: biceps femoris (flexes knee) and quadriceps (extends knee).
  5. The spine bends a little at each vertebral joint, giving a large total movement. Rectus muscles bend it side to side. Vertebrae differ in shape by region: lumbar vertebrae are large to carry body weight.

Muscle structure

Bones, muscles and joints

Muscle structure

Muscle structure (1/2)

  1. Skeletal muscle is bundles of muscle fibres in a connective tissue sheath. Each fibre has thick and thin protein filaments, which slide past each other to shorten it when contracting.
  2. Three types: skeletal (voluntary), cardiac (heart, involuntary), smooth (involuntary, in gut, bladder, uterus, sperm ducts and blood vessels).
  3. Contraction needs energy from respiration, with glucose and oxygen from the blood. Some energy is lost as heat.

Muscle structure

Muscle structure (2/2)

  1. In strenuous exercise: breathing rate and depth rise, heart rate rises, blood is diverted from the gut to muscles, and skin loses heat by vasodilation and sweating.
  2. Muscle tone: a few fibres always partially contracted keep muscles taut and the body upright, taking turns.
  3. Diet: muscles are largely protein, so amino acids are needed. A balanced diet supports bone and muscle development.
