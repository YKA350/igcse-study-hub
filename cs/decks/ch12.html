Why binary

Why binary

Why binary (1/3)

Binary

A base-2 number system using only the digits 0 and 1. Information is represented by only two values, e.g. voltage or no voltage.

Digital

Information represented by certain fixed values. Systems with separate states are digital.

Analogue

Signals represented by a continuously variable quantity, like a dimmer switch. Needs very accurate sending and receiving systems.

Why binary

Why binary (2/3)

The processor holds billions of transistors connected to form circuits.

Transistors act like switches with two states, on or off, so there are no communication errors from in-between values.

Numbers, text, graphics, sound and program instructions are all streams of 1s and 0s. The program decides how to interpret them.

Bits and combinations

Binary

Bits and combinations

Bits and combinations (1/2)

A bit (binary digit) is the smallest unit of data, 1 or 0.

1 bit gives 2 combinations, 2 bits give 4, 3 bits give 8, 4 bits give 16.

The number of combinations is 2 to the power of the number of bits.

Bits and combinations

Bits and combinations (2/2)

Representing 52 letters plus punctuation needs more bits, so bits are grouped.

A byte is the basic group, typically 8 bits.

Signed numbers

Binary

Signed numbers

Sign and magnitude (1/2)

Unsigned integers are zero or positive numbers only.

In sign and magnitude the left-most bit is the sign (0 positive, 1 negative) and the other bits are the magnitude.

Example

+28 is 00011100 and −28 is 10011100.

Signed numbers

Sign and magnitude (2/2)

It wastes a pattern on −0 and adding signed numbers needs extra rules.

Signed numbers

Two's complement (1/2)

Exam tip Adding −28 to +28 in two's complement gives 00000000, ignoring the carry out. Two's complement makes addition and subtraction work with the same circuit.

The left-most bit has a negative place value: −128 64 32 16 8 4 2 1.

To find the two's complement (the negative) of a number: write the positive number in binary, invert every bit, then add 1.

Signed numbers

Two's complement (2/2)

Example

+28 is 00011100. Invert to 11100011. Add 1 to get 11100100, which is −28.

Convert back by inverting and adding 1, or by using place values with −128.

Binary shifts

Binary

Binary shifts

Binary shifts

Logical shift Bits move left or right and empty places are filled with 0. A left shift multiplies an unsigned number by 2 for each place. A right shift divides by 2 for each place and may lose bits.

Arithmetic shift Used on signed (two's complement) numbers. The sign bit is kept so the sign stays correct. A right arithmetic shift copies the sign bit into the new left-most place.

Hexadecimal

Binary

Hexadecimal

Hexadecimal (1/2)

Hexadecimal

A base-16 number system using 0 to 9 and A to F, where A = 10 up to F = 15.

One hex digit represents 4 bits (a nibble).

Hex is shorter and easier for humans to read, remember and type than binary.

Hexadecimal

Hexadecimal (2/2)

It is used for memory addresses, colours and error codes.
