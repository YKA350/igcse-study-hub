Artificial intelligence

Current and emerging trends

Artificial intelligence

Artificial intelligence (1/2)

Artificial intelligence (AI)

The ability of a digital computer or computer-controlled robot to perform tasks commonly linked with intelligent beings, those that can adapt to changing circumstances, such as learning, problem solving and pattern recognition.

Machine learning

The ability of computers to learn without being explicitly programmed. A program tries something else if it fails and remembers what works.

Artificial intelligence

Artificial intelligence (2/2)

  1. Examples: self-driving cars, robots that mimic humans, machine learning.
  2. Normal programs repeat the same instructions and are not intelligent.
  3. A key problem is that nobody fully understands how natural intelligence works. The brain has billions of neurons (nerve cells) linked by electrical connections.

Artificial intelligence

Uses of AI

  1. Medicine: digital consultations that compare symptoms and history with a database, analysing scans trained on millions of confirmed diagnoses, and wearable health monitors for heart rate and activity.
  2. Recommendations by Amazon and Netflix learn from your past choices.
  3. Virtual assistants such as Siri and Cortana find answers and learn your needs.
  4. Airbus pilots fly manually only about 3 to 4 minutes, as motion sensors and GPS let software even land planes.
  5. Banks use AI to spot unusual transaction patterns.
  6. Sophia is a humanoid robot using speech recognition that learns from conversations.

DNA computing (1/2)

DNA

Deoxyribonucleic acid, a self-replicating material carrying genetic information in chromosomes.

Spec 6.1.4 Be aware of current and emerging trends in computing technology
DNA computing

DNA computing (2/2)

  1. DNA is digital but not binary. It has four bases, adenine (A), thymine (T), guanine (G) and cytosine (C).
  2. The equivalent of a byte is a codon of three bases: 4 × 4 × 4 = 64 values, compared with 256 for a byte.
  3. Advantages: large supply, cheap, clean biochips, tiny size. Half a kilogram could store more than all electronic computers.
  4. First used for computing in 1994 to find the shortest route through seven cities, but analysing the answer took days and needed human help.
  5. DNA storage is more successful and can last hundreds of thousands of years in cool, dry conditions.
  6. It is seen as combining biology and computing, not replacing silicon.

Nanotechnology

Current and emerging trends

Nanotechnology

Nanotechnology

Nanotechnology

Manipulating matter of 1 to 100 nanometres. A nanometre is one-billionth of a metre.

  1. It is interdisciplinary: biologists, chemists, physicists and engineers.
  2. Transistors have shrunk to about 14 nm. Silicon may fail below about 7 nm, so carbon nanotubes (cylinders of carbon atoms) are researched. They could be five times faster and use less energy.
  3. Uses: self-cleaning glass, UV-protective clothing with zinc oxide, scratch-resistant coatings, medicine delivering anti-cancer drugs straight to tumours, and strong light tennis rackets.

Quantum computing

Current and emerging trends

Quantum computing

Quantum computing (1/2)

  1. Quantum computers are not just faster classical computers. They use quantum mechanics, the physics of the very small.
  2. Superposition: a system can be in many states at once until measured, like Schrodinger's cat or a spinning coin.
  3. A qubit (quantum bit) can be 1 and 0 at the same time, so many outcomes are tried at once. Four qubits can represent all 16 combinations at once.

Quantum computing

Quantum computing (2/2)

  1. Entanglement: qubits linked so changing one changes the other instantly, even far apart. This greatly increases power.
  2. Qubits are fragile and noise from vibration or temperature causes errors. Superconducting circuits are cooled colder than deep space.
  3. Uses today: Volkswagen simulates battery chemistry, drug companies compare compounds, Airbus finds fuel-efficient flight paths, and future traffic management.
