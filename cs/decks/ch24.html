This chapter in a nutshell

Manufacture, use and disposal of computing harm the environment.

Manufacture uses non-renewable and hazardous materials, energy and water.

Use consumes huge energy, especially data centres.

Disposal creates e-waste.

Regulations (RoHS) and recycling help.

Computing also helps protect the environment.

The problem

Non-renewable materials

Manufacture

Manufacture

Manufacture (1/4)

Raw material extraction

Sand for glass, oil for plastic, and metals such as silver, gold, copper and palladium are in short supply. Arsenic, cadmium and chromium are hazardous. Radioactive uranium and thorium can contaminate air, soil and groundwater. Uncontrolled mining damages landscape, pollutes water, harms wildlife and exposes miners to injury and illnesses such as silicosis and lung cancer. Rare-earth metals are also used.

Manufacture

Manufacture (2/4)

Production

Materials go to factories, often thousands of miles away, to make components that are then assembled. A lot of energy from fossil fuels like coal and oil is used, producing carbon dioxide and global warming. Making semiconductors uses huge amounts of water, e.g. a factory making 40 000 a month uses about 20 million litres a day. A 24 kg computer and monitor needs about ten times its weight in fossil fuels (240 kg), a similar weight of chemicals and about 1500 litres of water.

Use

Computing and the environmental impact of technology

Use

Use (1/2)

  1. About 2 billion connected PCs and laptops and over 6 billion mobile devices together use huge amounts of electricity, more than the energy used making them.
  2. Efficiency has improved but use depends on the task: 3D modelling, complex calculations and games use a lot.
  3. Sleep mode can cut consumption by over 50 per cent when idle.
  4. Data centres use vast power to run and cool equipment, more than aviation. Small private or government centres are least efficient.

Use (2/2)

  1. Facebook built a centre near the Arctic Circle in Sweden to use hydroelectric power and cold air for cooling.
  2. Carbon footprint is the carbon dioxide an individual or organisation produces from the energy they consume.
  3. Green computing is efficient use of resources: energy efficiency, safer materials and eco-friendly systems.

Disposal

Computing and the environmental impact of technology

Disposal

Disposal

  1. E-waste is growing fast (UN StEP: soon as heavy as eleven great pyramids). It was expected to top 52 million tonnes by 2021 with only about 20 per cent recycled.
  2. Much is sent to developing countries and dumped in landfill. Toxic substances leak into the ground, water supplies, the food chain and the air.
  3. Each million mobile phones contain about 24 kg of gold, 250 kg of silver and 9 tonnes of copper, so poor local people strip them without protection, risking mercury and lead exposure and toxic fumes.
  4. Countries set targets for collection, recycling and recovery, and for safe disposal of dangerous substances. Many manufacturers run recycling programmes.
  5. Ways to extend life: reuse and refurbish pre-owned devices, and repair them.

Helping the environment

Helping the environment

Helping the environment (1/2)

Climate change

NASA analyses satellite data and sea temperatures. Wireless sensor probes under glaciers measure temperature, pressure and movement. Oxford uses spare home computer time to model whether warming caused UK floods.

Early warning

Tsunami warning systems use sensor networks and communications to evacuate coasts.

Helping the environment (2/2)

Conservation

GPS and satellites track Malaysian elephants. Transponders on bees study disease and pesticides. Mobile phones listen for illegal logging and alert rangers.

Energy

Sensors in buildings monitor light, temperature, people and electricity so use adjusts automatically. Solar farms track the sun and adjust panels. Battery storage holds surplus power.
