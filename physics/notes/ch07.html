Current and Voltage

Physics · Unit 2 · Electricity · Last-minute notes · Book pages 67–74

Chapter in a nutshell

  • Current = rate of flow of charge; in metals, electrons
  • Q = I × t (C, A, s); 1 A = 1 C/s
  • Voltage = energy transferred per unit charge
  • E = Q × V (J, C, V); 1 V = 1 J/C
  • Ammeter in series, voltmeter across

  • Current in = current out at a junction

1Current and charge
Conductors and insulators

Conductors

Charges flow easily. All metals are good conductors: some electrons are free to move.

Insulators

Very poor conductors, e.g. plastics. Used to stop charge flowing, like a plug casing.

What makes a current flow?

No voltage

Free electrons move randomly. Equal numbers go each way, so there is no current.

Voltage applied

More electrons drift from − to +. In metals, a current is a flow of negatively charged electrons.

Two directions, one circuit

Conventional current

Flows from + to −, out of the long line of the cell. Early scientists thought positive charges moved.

Electron flow

In metal wires it is really electrons moving the other way, from − to +.

Exam tipArrows on circuit diagrams show conventional current.
The ammeter goes in series
  1. Put the ammeter in the loop, so the current goes through it.
  2. It has a very low resistance, so it hardly changes the current.
  3. Current is measured in amperes (amps, A).
Coulombs and amps
Key term · current

Current is the rate of flow of charge.

Unit of charge

One electron's charge is tiny, so charge is measured in coulombs (C).

One coulomb

1 C is about the charge of 6 × 10¹⁸ electrons.

One amp

1 A means 1 C of charge passes each second. 1 A = 1 C/s

How much charge flows?
a) A current of 3 A flows for 5 s. How much charge flows?
Write downI = 3 A, t = 5 s
EquationQ = I × t
SubstituteQ = 3 × 5
Q = 15 C

b) How many electrons?

1 C ≈ 6 × 10¹⁸ electrons
15 × 6 × 10¹⁸ = 9 × 10¹⁹ electrons

WowThat is 90 million million million electrons in 5 seconds!
2Voltage and energy
Cells are electron pumps
Key ideaA cell gives energy to the charges. Its voltage = joules given to each coulomb.
What is voltage?
Key term · voltage

Voltage is the energy transferred per unit charge passed.

The volt

The volt is a joule per coulomb.
1 V = 1 J/C

Battery

Several cells connected together make a battery.

Direct current

Cells give d.c.: the current flows in one direction.

Energy = charge × voltage
energy transferred = charge × voltage
E = Q × V
E in JQ in CV in V
Given I and t?Q = I × t, so E = I × t × V

Where does the energy go?

Bulb

→ heat and light

Speaker

→ mostly sound

A 12 V bulb
50 C of charge passes through a 12 V bulb. How much energy is transferred?
Write downQ = 50 C, V = 12 V
EquationE = Q × V
SubstituteE = 50 × 12
E = 600 J
In wordsEach coulomb gives the bulb 12 J. 50 coulombs give 50 × 12 J.

Units check

joules = coulombs × volts

A heater on the mains
A 230 V heater takes 5 A for 5 minutes. How much energy is transferred?
Write downI = 5 A, V = 230 V, t = 5 × 60 = 300 s
EquationE = I × t × V
SubstituteE = 5 × 300 × 230
E = 345 000 J (345 kJ)
Watch outChange 5 minutes into 300 s before you multiply.
Big numbers1 kJ = 1000 J, so 345 000 J = 345 kJ.

  • It has a very high resistance, so very little current flows through it.

3Circuits and symbols
No gaps allowed

Complete circuit

No gaps, so charges flow and the bulb lights. A torch lights when its button closes the circuit.

Incomplete circuit

There is a gap, so there is no current. The bulb stays off.

The basics
RememberThe long line of a cell is the + side.
Meters and more

Lamps and LEDs show a current
Key factA lamp or LED glows when there is a current, so it shows the circuit is on.

LED

Light-emitting diode. Needs far less energy than a bulb.

Uses

TVs, DVD players and routers: an LED shows they are on or on standby.

Voltage = current × resistance
voltage = current × resistance

4Series and parallel
Series and parallel

Series

One loop: no branches, only one path.

Parallel

Branches and junctions: more than one path.

How series and parallel behave
Series (bulbs)Parallel (bulbs)
SwitchesOne switch turns all on or offEach bulb can be switched on its own
One bulb breaksGap in the circuit → all go outOnly bulbs on that branch go out
Adding bulbsEnergy shared → all get dimmerSame voltage → brightness unchanged
CurrentSame everywhere, not used upSplits at junctions: total in = total out
VoltageShared between componentsSame across each branch
The same all the way round
Key factIn series the current is the same at every point.

Not used up

The bulbs transfer the energy the charges carry. No charge disappears.

What sets the current?

Voltage supplied

Add a second identical cell: voltage doubles, current doubles.

Number of parts

More bulbs in series share the energy: all get dimmer.

Type of parts

The current also depends on what kind of components are in the loop.

Total in = total out
current in = current out
0.6 A = 0.4 A + 0.2 A

Why?

Current is conserved. Charge can't be made or lost, so electrons flowing in each second = electrons flowing out.

Parallel parts get the same voltage
Key factThe voltage across components connected in parallel is the same.

So…

Adding another bulb in parallel does not make the others dimmer.

Don't mix upVoltage is shared only in series.
Home lighting is parallel
  • Each light has its own switch.
  • Each gets the full mains voltage, so brightness doesn't change when others go on.
  • If one bulb breaks, the others still work.

Same reasons

Street lights and cooker parts are wired in parallel too.

Decorative lights are often in series

Why it works

Each small bulb needs only a low voltage. Even with the mains voltage shared, each gets enough energy to light.

DownsideIf one filament breaks, all the bulbs go out.
