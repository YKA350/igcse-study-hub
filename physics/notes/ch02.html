Forces and Shape

Physics · Unit 1 · Forces and motion · Last-minute notes · Book pages 18–27

Chapter in a nutshell

  • Force = push or pull, in newtons (N)
  • Forces change speed, direction or shape
  • Force is a vector: size + direction
  • Resultant: add forces with + and − signs
  • Balanced → no change in motion
  • Unbalanced → the object accelerates
  • Friction opposes motion → heat
  • Elastic: recovers its original shape
  • Normal reaction acts at 90° to the surface
  • Extension = stretched − original length
  • Hooke’s law: extension ∝ force
  • Past the elastic limit → permanent stretch
1What is a force?
What can a force do?
The unit of force: the newton
weight arrows drawn to scale
Key fact

Force is measured in newtons (N), named after Sir Isaac Newton.

  • A 1 kg bag of sugar weighs about 10 N.
  • An average apple weighs about 1 N.
Coming up in Ch 31 N makes a 1 kg mass accelerate at 1 m/s².
Force is a vector
Vector vs scalar

A vector has size and direction. A scalar has size only.

Scalar (size only)Vector (size + direction)
temperatureforce
massdisplacement
speedvelocity
distanceacceleration
Why it mattersSame size, different direction = a different effect. So always give a force’s direction.
2Types of force
Four forces on a pushed car

Look only at forces on one object. Ignore very small ones.

  1. Push from the man, forwards.
  2. Friction in the engine, gears and axles. It opposes the motion.
  3. Weight: the Earth’s pull, acting down.
  4. Normal reaction: the road pushing up on the wheels.
The surface pushes back
Key term · normal reaction

The push of a surface on an object resting on it.

“Normal” means at 90° to the surface.

RememberIt stops a car sinking into the road. Weight always acts straight down.

Drawing a force diagram
  1. Start each arrow on the object. Point it the way the force acts.
  2. Make the length show the size. Equal forces get equal arrows.
  3. Label every arrow with its proper name.
Common mistakeWrite weight, not “gravity”. Write normal reaction, not “the ground”.
3Resultant forces
Finding the resultant
Three examples

Balanced

F + (−F) = 0 N
Push = friction. The car does not move.

Same direction

300 + 300 = 600 N
Two people push. 600 N forwards.

With friction

300 + 300 − 300 = 300 N
Resultant is 300 N forwards.

A cyclist
A cyclist pushes forwards with 120 N. Air resistance is 40 N and friction is 20 N, both backwards. Find the resultant force.
Choose +forwards = +, backwards = −
Add120 + (−40) + (−20)
Work out120 − 40 − 20 = 60
60 N forwards
Common mistakeForgetting the direction. A resultant force needs a size and a direction.
Exam tipForces the opposite way are negative. Put in the signs before you add.
Balanced and unbalanced

Balanced forces

Same size, opposite directions. No change in motion: still stays still; moving keeps a steady speed in a straight line.

Unbalanced forces

One force is bigger. The object accelerates (or decelerates) in the direction of the larger force.

Tug of war and drag racer

Equal pulls: rope stays still (balanced). When one team tires, the forces become unbalanced and the rope accelerates towards the stronger team.

At first thrust ≫ friction + drag, so it accelerates. Friction and drag grow until they balance the thrust: then it moves at a steady speed.

What does the resultant do?
Resultant forceObject at rest…Object moving…
Zero (balanced)stays at restkeeps the same speed and direction
Not zero (unbalanced)starts to move (accelerates)speeds up, slows down or changes direction
Common mistake“Balanced” does not only mean “not moving”. It can also mean moving at a steady speed in a straight line.
4Friction
Friction opposes motion
Key term · friction

A force that opposes motion. It makes moving objects slow down and stop.

  • Kinetic energy is transferred to heat (thermal energy).
  • Acts when solids rub and when objects move through fluids.
Useful or a nuisance?

Useful for…

  • tyres gripping the road
  • effective brakes
  • walking without slipping
  • a parachute (fluid friction)

A nuisance when…

  • it wastes energy in machines
  • moving parts wear out
  • it slows racers: cyclists and swimmers wear special materials
On iceThere may be too little friction for even a powerful car to move. Skaters and sledges glide a long way.
Investigating friction
  1. Hanging weights pull the block over a pulley.
  2. Add weights until the block just starts to slide. Pull = maximum friction.
  3. Change one factor: the surfaces, or masses on the block.
  4. Rough surface (sandpaper) → more force needed.
SafetyCatch box with bubble wrap under the weights. Keep feet out of the drop zone.
The air-track glider

Linear air-track

Friction is tiny. After one small push the glider covers equal distances in equal times. Its velocity stays constant, though no force acts.

Extension: “weightless” in space

Objects in orbit only seem weightless. Gravity still acts, but the craft and everything in it fall around the Earth together.

5Forces change shape
Tension and compression

A rubber band stretches easily. Steel of the same length needs a huge force.

Temporary change

Shape returns when the force is removed: a bike suspension spring, a clock spring.

Permanent change

Shape stays changed: a crushed can, a crashed car, a bridge that fails.

Going back to the original shape
Key term · elastic behaviour

The ability of a material to recover its original shape after the forces causing deformation have been removed.

SpringsThey return to their original length, as long as they are not stretched too far.
Elastic, plastic and brittle
TypeBehaviourExamples
ElasticDoes not break easily; returns to its original shape when the force is removedrubber, springs, metal wires
PlasticChanges shape with small forces and stays changedputty, modelling clay
BrittleDoes not change shape easily: breaks instead of stretchingglass

Springs

Coiled metal that can be stretched or compressed. Used in car and bike suspension, beds, chairs, door locks and self-closing doors.

Hooke’s law
Hooke’s law

The extension of a spring is directly proportional to the force (load) applied, as long as the limit of proportionality is not exceeded.

extension = stretched length − original length

Robert Hooke (1635–1703) hung loads on springs and plotted force against extension.

double the force → double the extension
Two limits on the graph
Reading the straight part
A spring is 20 cm long. With a 2 N load it is 23 cm long. What is the extension?
Equationextension = stretched − original
Substitute23 − 20
3 cm
Key ideaThe first straight part of a force–extension graph shows Hooke’s law.
PredictIn that region, 4 N would give 6 cm extension (double the force, double the extension).
Common mistakeUsing the total length instead of the extension.
6Core practical
Springs: the method
Three different graphs

Spring

Straight line, then curves after the limit of proportionality.

Metal wire

Obeys Hooke’s law, then may stretch plastically before it breaks (✕).

Rubber band

Not a straight line: does not obey Hooke’s law.

Wires, rubber bands and safety
  • Elastic stretching: the wire returns to its original length.
  • Plastic stretching: it doesn’t, and may get thinner (necking).
  • Rubber bands: extension can differ when loading and unloading.

Safety

  • Wear eye protection: a snapping band can hit your eyes.
  • Clamp the stand firmly to the bench.
  • Keep hands and feet away from under the load.
  • Don’t use very large loads.
Common mistakes

“Gravity” on an arrow

Call it weight. The floor’s push is the normal reaction.

No direction

A resultant needs size and direction, e.g. 300 N forwards.

Ignoring signs

Forces in the opposite direction are negative.

Balanced = still?

Balanced can also mean a steady speed in a straight line.

Length, not extension

Extension = stretched − original length.

Rubber bands

They do not obey Hooke’s law.
