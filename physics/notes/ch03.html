Chapter in a nutshell

  • Only a resultant force causes acceleration
  • F = m × a (N, kg, m/s²)
  • 1 N gives 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m/s²
  • W = m × g, g ≈ 10 N/kg on Earth
  • Stopping = thinking + braking distance
  • Speed, mass, road and reaction time all matter
  • Locked wheels skid; ABS stops this
  • Drag rises with speed
  • Drag = weight → terminal velocity
  • Parachute open → drag > weight → slows down

Take-off

Short runway, so thrust ≫ friction + air resistance: a big unbalanced force.

Landing

Parachutes and drag wires give a big force backwards → a big deceleration.

RememberAn unbalanced force is also called the resultant force. No resultant force → no acceleration (velocity stays the same).
Only an unbalanced force accelerates

A car at constant speed still needs its engine force – but only to balance friction and air resistance. The resultant force is zero, so there is no acceleration.

  1. Tilt the ramp slightly so a gently pushed trolley keeps moving – this compensates for friction.

  3. For more force, move a mass from the trolley to the hanger – the total mass stays the same.

How brakes work

Brakes

Brakes on cars and bikes work by increasing friction between the turning wheels and the vehicle body, e.g. disc brakes.

Tyre tread

The grooves throw water away, keeping the rubber in contact with the road.

Grip between tyres and road

Maximum grip when…

  • tyres have a good tread
  • tyres are properly inflated
  • the road is dry

Less friction when…

  • the road is wet, icy or oily
  • tyres are worn or under-inflated
  • brakes are in poor condition

Friction also depends on the weight of the vehicle.

Brake too hard → skid

Brakes applied too hard → wheels lock → tyres lose grip. The car skids: the driver loses control and it takes longer to stop.

ABS (anti-lock braking system): a computer senses a wheel is about to lock and releases the brakes for a very short time. The driver can brake as hard as possible without skidding.

Thinking + braking
stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance
The three distances
Stopping distance

The distance covered from the moment the driver sees the need to stop until the vehicle stops.

Thinking distance

Distance travelled during the driver's reaction time (before the brakes come on).

Braking distance

Distance travelled while the brakes are applied.

RememberDuring the reaction time the car is still going at full speed.
Faster = much further

Braking distance rises much faster: 6, 14, 24, 38…

4Falling and terminal velocity
Air resistance, or drag
Key term · drag

The force that opposes motion through air is called air resistance or drag.

  • Its size depends on the object's shape and its speed.
  • Drag increases as speed increases.
  • Cars have a low drag coefficient; fast trains are streamlined.
The three stages of falling

A · Just released

No drag yet. Only weight acts → accelerates at g.

B · Speeding up

Drag grows. Resultant = weight − drag, so a decreases.

C · Terminal

Drag = weight. Balanced → constant velocity.

Explain terminal velocity in 5 steps
  1. At first only weight acts, so the object accelerates downwards.
  2. As its speed increases, air resistance (drag) increases.
  3. The resultant force (weight − drag) gets smaller, so the acceleration decreases.
  4. Eventually drag = weight: the forces are balanced, resultant = 0, acceleration = 0.
  5. The object keeps falling at a constant, maximum speed – its terminal velocity.

Modelling terminal velocity

In air, terminal velocity needs high speeds. Drag in a liquid is much bigger, so terminal velocity is low and easy to see.

  1. Fill a tall measuring cylinder with water (or a thicker liquid such as oil).
  2. Drop small balls (glass balls 1–2 mm across, or ball bearings).
  3. Time the ball between equally spaced marks (or use a light gate and data logger).
  4. When the time between marks becomes constant, the ball has reached terminal velocity.
Free-fall and "weightlessness"

Astronauts in the ISS or in training aircraft seem weightless, but gravity still pulls them (W = mg). They are in free-fall.

Newton's laws are over 300 years old but accurate enough to land craft on the Moon, Mars and a comet. They only fail near the speed of light (3 × 10⁸ m/s).
