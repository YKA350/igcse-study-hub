Counter

Rate meter

Activity and the becquerel
Key term · activity

The number of unstable nuclei that decay each second.

Key term · becquerel (Bq)

1 Bq = 1 disintegration per second.

The activity of a source decreases over time.

2Background radiation
Where does it come from?
Key term · background radiation

Low-level ionising radiation around us all the time, from natural and artificial sources.

Most of it is natural. Amounts vary from place to place.

In experimentsMeasure the background and subtract it.
Source 1: the Earth

Rocks

Crust rocks still hold radioactive isotopes from when Earth formed 4.5 billion years ago.

Radon and thoron

Made by uranium decay. Dense gases that collect in basements.

Granite areas

Levels are higher, for example in Cornwall.

Sources 2 and 3: space and living things

Cosmic rays

Very energetic particles from stars and exploding stars (supernovae). Lower-energy ones come from the Sun. The atmosphere protects us, but some reach the surface.

Living things

Our bodies contain radioactive atoms, such as carbon-14, constantly renewed while we are alive. All food and drink contains radioactive isotopes.

Human-made sources

Medical

x-rays, tracers and cancer treatment.

Nuclear power

Leaks and accidents: Three Mile Island (1979), Chernobyl (1986), Fukushima (2011).

Weapons and industry

Nuclear weapons testing and industrial tracers.

Decay productsWhat a radioactive element decays into may itself be radioactive.
3Random decay and half-life
Decay is random
Key idea

Radioactive decay is a random (unpredictable) process, like throwing a coin.

  • You can't say which nucleus decays, or when.
  • With huge numbers of nuclei, a predictable fraction decays in a given time.
Why activity falls
A nucleus decaysit is "out of the game"
→
Fewer nuclei leftfewer that can decay
→
Fewer decays each secondso the activity falls over time
The coin model
  1. Throw 1000 coins.
  2. Remove the "heads" each time.
  3. About half go every throw.
LimitationReal samples have millions of nuclei, not 1000.
The exponential decay curve

The amount left, and the activity, falls steeply at first, then more and more slowly.

The smaller the amount, the slower it decreases.

PatternIt halves every half-life: m → m/2 → m/4 → m/8 …
What is half-life?
Key term · half-life

The average time taken for half the nuclei (half the original radioactive material) in a sample to decay.

Half-life and activity

Same time, same fraction

Every half-life, half of what is left decays.

Activity halves too

When the amount halves, the activity halves. Activity is what we measure.

Different isotopes

Every radioactive isotope has its own half-life.

Say it rightHalf-life is the time for half the nuclei to decay, not half the time it takes for all of them to decay.

Short half-life

Dies away fast, so it is used as a medical tracer.

Long half-life

Lasts a very long time, so it is used for dating.

4Measuring and calculating
How to measure a half-life
  1. Measure the background count rate first (for example an average over 5 min) = x.
  2. Measure the sample's count rate at regular intervals.
  3. Corrected count rate C = reading − x.
  4. Plot C against time. Read the time for C to halve. Repeat and average.

TimingFor t½ of 10–20 min, take readings every few minutes for about an hour.
Reading half-life from a graph
  1. Find the initial count rate.
  2. Halve it. Go across to the curve.
  3. Go down to the time axis. That time is the half-life.
  4. Repeat from another start point and average.
