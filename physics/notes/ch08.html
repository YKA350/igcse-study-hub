Electrical Resistance

Physics · Unit 2 · Electricity · Last-minute notes · Book pages 75–83

Chapter in a nutshell

  • V = I × R; resistance is in ohms (Ω)
  • Series: R total = R₁ + R₂
  • Series: same current; voltages add up to the supply
  • More voltage → more current; more R → less current
  • Practical: ammeter in series, voltmeter in parallel
  • I–V: steeper line = lower resistance
  • Wire/resistor: straight line through the origin
  • Filament lamp: S-curve – R rises as it gets hot
  • Diode: current one way only, after about 0.6 V
  • Thermistor: hotter → lower R. LDR: brighter → lower R

The doorbell
There is 12 V across a doorbell and the current is 0.1 A. What is its resistance?
Write downV = 12 V, I = 0.1 A
EquationV = I × R, so R = V ÷ I
SubstituteR = 12 ÷ 0.1
R = 120 Ω
Big ideaWe find resistance by comparing the current through a component with the voltage across it.
Quick check÷ 0.1 is the same as × 10.

2Resistors in series
In series, just add them up
total resistance = R₁ + R₂
Rtotal = R₁ + R₂
all in ohms (Ω)
Why?The charge has to go through both resistors, one after the other.
Good newsYou only need the series rule – not a parallel rule.
2 Ω + 4 Ω on a 12 V supply
a) Total RR = 2 + 4 = 6 Ω
CurrentI = V ÷ R = 12 ÷ 6 = 2 A
b) 2 ΩV = I × R = 2 × 2 = 4 V
4 ΩV = I × R = 2 × 4 = 8 V
Check: 4 V + 8 V = 12 V ✓
Same currentThe 2 A flows through both resistors, because they are in series.
The four series rules
  1. Total resistance = the sum of the resistances.
  2. The current is the same through every component.
  3. The supply voltage is shared: the voltages add up to the supply.
  4. The bigger resistor gets the bigger share of the voltage.
Exam orderTotal resistance → current → each voltage.

3Core practical: I–V graphs
Setting up the circuit

Ammeter

In series – measures the current through the test item.

Voltmeter

In parallel across the test item – measures its voltage.

Variable resistor

Changes the current, so you get different readings.

Taking the readings
  1. Set the variable resistor to its maximum.
  2. Close the switch. Record the current and voltage.
  3. Change the variable resistor. Record a new pair of readings.
  4. Repeat to get at least six pairs. For a diode, also reverse the supply to get negative values.
  5. Make a table. Plot current (I) against voltage (V).
Safe and accurate
SafetyThe resistance wire can get hot enough to burn skin if the current is too large.

Keep currents small

Then the wire's temperature stays constant, so its resistance does not change.

Switch off

Switch off between readings so the wire does not heat up.

4The three I–V graphs
Wire or fixed resistor

Shape

A straight line through the origin.

Meaning

Current is directly proportional to voltage. Double V → double I.

ResistanceConstant slope → R does not change (at constant temperature).
Filament lamp

Shape

An S-shaped curve. It gets flatter at higher voltages.

Why?

As the current rises, the filament gets hotter, so its resistance increases.

LinkFlatter line = higher resistance.
Diode: a one-way street

Reverse

Zero current – very high resistance.

Forwards

Almost no current until about 0.6 V. Then the current rises steeply – low resistance.

Why does a hot filament have more resistance?
Superconductivity
Zero resistance

Near absolute zero (−273 °C) the vibrations stop. Some conductors then have zero resistance.

Uses

No energy loss, so electricity could be sent without losses. Maglev trains use superconducting magnets to hover.

Scientists are searching for materials that superconduct at higher temperatures.

5Using resistance
Keeping the current safe

What they do

Fixed resistors control the size of currents and voltages.

Example

A resistor in series with a 1.5 V bulb limits the current, so the bulb doesn't “blow”.

Turn the knob, change the current

Decrease R

Bigger current → bulb brighter.

Increase R

Smaller current → bulb dimmer.

UsesVolume knobs, dimmer switches and controlling the speed of motors.
Thermistor: hot → low R
LDR: light → low R

How it behaves

High resistance in the dark. Resistance decreases as light gets brighter.

Uses

Street and security lights, burglar alarms, camera exposure meters.

Diodes let charge flow one way

Forwards

Low resistance → current flows, bulb glows.

Reversed

Very high resistance → current almost zero.

Picture itLike aeroplane steps – you can only climb them from one side.
Rectifiers and LEDs

Rectifier circuits

Use diodes to convert a.c. into d.c.

LEDs

Diodes that glow when current flows.

Test yourself

1. A lamp has 6 V across it and a current of 0.5 A. What is its resistance?R = V ÷ I = 6 ÷ 0.5 = 12 Ω
2. A 3 Ω and a 9 Ω resistor are in series with a 6 V supply. Find the current.R total = 12 Ω, so I = 6 ÷ 12 = 0.5 A
3. In question 2, what is the voltage across the 9 Ω resistor?V = 0.5 × 9 = 4.5 V (and 1.5 V across the 3 Ω)
4. Why does the I–V graph for a filament lamp curve?The filament gets hotter as the current rises, so its resistance increases.
5. What happens to the resistance of an LDR when a light is switched off?It increases – an LDR has a high resistance in the dark.
6. What happens to the current through a diode when the supply is reversed?It is almost zero – the diode has a very high resistance in reverse.
