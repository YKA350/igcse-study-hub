Chapter in a nutshell

  • Nucleus: protons (+1) and neutrons (0); electrons (−1) around it
  • Z = protons; A = protons + neutrons; N = A − Z
  • Isotopes: same Z, different number of neutrons
  • Unstable nuclei decay at random, giving out ionising radiation
  • α: helium nucleus, stopped by paper. β⁻: fast electron, stopped by mm of Al
  • γ: EM wave, cut down by thick lead. Neutrons make atoms radioactive
  • α: A − 4, Z − 2 · β⁻: Z + 1 · γ: no change · n: A − 1
  • Balance equations: top = top, bottom = bottom. Activity is in Bq
1Inside the atom
A tiny nucleus, electrons around it

The nucleus

Made of protons and neutrons packed together in the middle.

The electrons

Move rapidly round the nucleus in a cloud or shell.

Mostly empty space

The nucleus is about 10 000 times smaller than the atom.

How small is the nucleus?
Picture itIf the nucleus were the size of a full stop, the atom would be about 2.5 m across.

In simple words

An atom is mostly empty space with a tiny, heavy nucleus in the middle.

Ratio

Atom : nucleus = about 10 000 : 1 in size.

Two numbers describe every nucleus
Atomic (proton) number, Z

The number of protons. Carbon is always Z = 6.

Mass (nucleon) number, A

Protons + neutrons. Electron mass is tiny, so it is ignored.

Never changesAtoms may gain or lose electrons, but Z never changes.

2Isotopes and unstable nuclei
Same protons, different neutrons
Key term · isotopes

Atoms of an element with different numbers of neutrons.

Same and differentSame Z and electrons: same chemistry. Different neutrons: different A, mass and stability.
Carbon-12 and carbon-14
In simple wordsBoth are carbon because both have 6 protons. The two extra neutrons make carbon-14 unstable.
A tug of war inside the nucleus
Nuclear force

Very strong but acts over a tiny distance.

Neutrons matter

Too many or too few neutrons → the nucleus is unstable.

Decay

An unstable nucleus eventually decays: it gives out energy and may emit α or β particles.

Radioactive decay is random
Random

You cannot predict which nucleus will decay next, or when.

What we can say

Only the probability that a proportion will decay in a given time (Chapter 23).

Think popcornYou cannot say which kernel pops next, but lots will pop in the next minute.
What “ionising” means
Ionising radiation

Makes atoms gain or lose electrons, forming ions.

Example

A neutral atom hit by radiation loses an electron and becomes a positive ion.

Where from?α, β⁻ and γ are ionising radiations emitted from unstable nuclei in a random process.
3Alpha, beta, gamma and neutrons
Alpha (α): a heavy helium nucleus
  • A helium nucleus: 2 protons + 2 neutrons
  • Mass number 4, charge +2 — relatively heavy
  • Strongly ionising, so it loses its energy fast
  • Short range: a few cm of air; stopped by paper
In simple wordsAlpha is big and clumsy: it bumps into lots of atoms, so it runs out of energy quickly.
Beta (β⁻): a fast electron from the nucleus
Beta-minus

Made when a neutron in the nucleus splits into a proton and an electron.

  • Very light, charge −1; medium ionising
  • Travels far in air; goes through paper
  • Stopped by a few mm of aluminium
Not from the shellsβ comes from the nucleus, not from the electron shells.
Gamma (γ): a ray, not a particle
Gamma

High-energy electromagnetic radiation with a very short wavelength.

  • No mass, no charge
  • Weakly ionising, so very penetrating
  • Stopped by several cm of lead or about a metre of concrete
RememberGamma is a wave like light, not a particle. It has no mass and no charge.
Neutrons make other atoms radioactive
Neutrons

The only type of radiation that can make other atoms radioactive.

  • Symbol 10n: mass ≈ proton, no charge
  • Does not ionise directly
  • Key in fission (Chapter 25); screened by water or concrete
In simple wordsA neutron has no charge, so it can slip right into another nucleus.

What stops each radiation?

Alpha

Stopped by paper.

Beta

Stopped by a few mm of aluminium.

Gamma

Cut down by several cm of lead (or about 1 m of concrete).

Two ways to detect radiation

Geiger–Müller (GM) tube

A GM tube detects radiation. A counter shows the count.

Photographic film

Radiation darkens photographic film.

Measuring activity in becquerels
Key term · becquerel (Bq)

A measure of how many unstable nuclei disintegrate every second.

4Practical: penetrating power
Set-up: source, absorber, GM tube
  1. With no source, measure the background count for 5 min. Repeat ×3, take the mean.
  2. Put the source 2–4 cm from the GM tube. Count for 5 min.
  3. Add an absorber and count again.
Fair testSubtract the background count from each reading. Keep the distance fixed.
What the counts show

Alpha source

Thick paper: the count falls to background. α is stopped by paper.

Beta source

At 1–2 mm of aluminium the count is at background. β is stopped by aluminium.

Gamma source

Only a few cm of lead bring the count down. γ is very penetrating.

Stay safe with sources

Store

In lead-lined boxes, in a locked metal cupboard with a warning label.

Handle

With tongs, held away from the body. Never point a source at anyone.

Use

For the shortest time. Log their use and return them immediately.

Supervise

Closely supervised by qualified staff. (UK: under-16s may not handle sources.)

Identify the radiation

Common mistakes — and the fix
Mixing up A and ZA is on top (nucleons). Z is at the bottom (protons).
β comes from the shellsIt comes from the nucleus: a neutron → proton + electron.
Gamma changes A or ZIt changes neither. It is pure energy.
Forgetting decay is randomYou cannot predict which nucleus decays, or when.
“Alpha is the most dangerous”Only inside the body. Outside, β and γ are riskier because they can get in.
