Applications of Radioactivity

Physics · Unit 7 · Radioactivity and particles · Last-minute notes · Book pages 241–249

Chapter in a nutshell

  • Medicine: γ tracers (short half-life), α/β targeted treatment, γ sterilising
  • Industry: γ radiography, β thickness, γ levels, tracers for leaks
  • Irradiation = exposed from outside. Contamination = material on or in it
  • C-14 halves every 5600 years: age = n × t½
  • Dating limits: once-living material, up to 50 000–60 000 years
  • Radiation can damage cells, cause mutations and cancer
  • α worst inside the body. β and γ worst outside
  • Safety: small sources, tongs, distance, shielding, badges. Waste: sealed storage
Three questions for every use

1 · Which radiation?

γ if it must get out of the body or through metal.

α or β if it must stop close by.

2 · What half-life?

Short for use inside people, so the activity dies away.

Long for dating and gauges that must last.

3 · Is it safe?

Lowest dose, shortest time, greatest distance, and shielding.

In simple wordsPick the right radiation, the right half-life, and keep everyone safe.
1Radioactivity in medicine
Tracers: seeing inside the body
  • A chemical with a gamma emitter is swallowed or injected.
  • A γ camera outside follows it round the body and makes an image of organs.

Why γ?

It passes out of the body and is only weakly ionising.

Why a short half-life?

Activity falls to a very low level within days.

Two tracers to know

Iodine-123

  • Absorbed by the thyroid gland just like normal iodine.
  • Emits γ, so we get an image of the thyroid.
half-life ≈ 13 hours

Technetium-99m

  • The most widely used medical imaging isotope.
  • Low-energy γ. Attached to drugs to target organs, tracked with a γ camera.
half-life = 6 hours
Both tracersGamma emitters with short half-lives.
What radiation does to cells

Low dose

Often no lasting effect.

Higher dose

Damages DNA, which can cause abnormal growth: cancer.

Very high dose

Kills cells.

Use itA very high dose kills cells, so it can be used to destroy a tumour.
Treating cancer
  • Radioactive chemicals are targeted at the tumour.
  • They emit α or β. Short range, so only nearby tissue is affected.

Iodine-131

A β emitter, half-life about 8 days. Treats thyroid disease.

DownsideHealthy cells nearby are damaged too.
Sterilising with gamma rays
  • γ kills bacteria and viruses on surgical instruments.
  • They are sealed in bags first. γ passes through the packaging, so they cannot be re-contaminated.

Safe result

The item is not damaged and does not become radioactive.

Food too

Kills bacteria and keeps vitamins. It cannot remove toxins already made.

Irradiation vs contamination
Irradiation

Exposed to radiation from outside. It does not become radioactive.

Contamination

Radioactive material gets onto or into it. It keeps emitting.

2Radioactivity in industry
Gamma radiography
  • Works like an airport x-ray scanner.
  • γ passes through metal. A crack lets more γ through, so it shows on the film or camera.

Checks welds and castings

Find faults without cutting the metal open.

Easy to use anywhere

Sources are small and need no power supply.

Gauging the thickness of a sheet

Thicker sheet

More β absorbed, so less reaches the detector.

Automatic control

The reading adjusts the rollers.

Why β?

α would not get through. γ would pass through whatever the thickness.

Gauging the level in a hopper
  • A hopper is a big container, here full of coal.
  • Coal absorbs γ: the lower detector reads low.
  • The empty top lets γ through: the upper detector reads high.
So we knowThe level is between the two detectors.
Why use radioactive gauges?

No contact

The gauge never touches the material.

Works in dust

Coal dust would fool a light (optical) system. Dust is much less dense than coal, so γ still works.

Continuous

It reads all the time and is automatic.

Tracing leaks and flows
Pipe leak: the reading rises above the leak.
Sewage: sensors show how far it spreads.
Which tracer?A γ (or β) emitter with a short half-life. A tiny amount is easy to detect.

How carbon-14 is made

  • A neutron hits nitrogen-14 (about 80% of air).

While alive

C-14 that decays is replaced, so the proportion stays constant.

After death

No more replacing. C-14 decays, so the proportion falls.

Carbon-14 as a clock
Half-life of C-14

About 5600 years.

  1. Measure the activity of the old sample.
  2. Compare it with the same mass of living material.
  3. Count the halvings. Each is 5600 years.
In simple wordsLess C-14 activity left means older.

The limits of radiocarbon dating

1 · Cosmic rays vary

Corrected using samples of known age: Egyptian mummies, very old trees.

2 · Not beyond 50 000–60 000 years

After 10 half-lives (56 000 years) only 1/1024 is left.

3 · Once-living only

Wood, bone, cloth. Not rocks.

Dating rocks
  • A parent nuclide decays to a daughter nuclide. A chain is a decay series.
  • The ratio of parent to stable daughter gives the age.
ParentDaughterHalf-life
potassium-40argon-401.25 billion yr

ResultThe Earth is about 4.5 billion years old.
4Dangers, safety and waste
How radiation harms living things
Outside or inside the body?
Outside: β and γ are the most dangerous.
Inside: α is the most dangerous. It is right next to cells and strongly ionising.
Reduce your dose

Short time

Handle sources for as little time as possible.

Distance

Keep sources far from your body.

Shielding

Lead, concrete or thick glass absorb radiation.

Lowest dose

Use very small sources.

Radiation, the radon riskRadon is an α emitter. Smokers draw it deep into their lungs.
Handling and monitoring

At school

Very small sources, kept in lead-lined, labelled containers in a locked metal cabinet. Use tongs, far from the body, for a short time.

Workers wear badges

Film badge: fogs with exposure. Thermoluminescent badge: gives out more light when warmed after more exposure. Checked regularly to measure total dose.

Large sources

Industry and research

Large sources are handled remotely, behind lead shields, concrete and thick glass.

Spent fuel rods

Stored under water until the radiation falls to a safe level.

Radioactive waste
The problemSome waste has a very long half-life. It stays active for thousands to tens of thousands of years.
  • Stored in sealed containers that stay secure for a very long time.
  • Risk reduced by sealing, shielding, storing far from people and monitoring.

Test yourself

1. Why is technetium-99m a good medical tracer?γ escapes the body and is weakly ionising. Its 6 hour half-life means the activity soon drops.
2. Mo-99 becomes Tc-99m. A stays 99, Z goes 42 → 43. Which decay?β decay. A is unchanged so it is not α, and Z goes up by 1.
3. Iodine-131: what does a small dose do, and a large dose?Small: shows how much the thyroid takes up. Large: β kills thyroid cells.
4. Living wood: 24 Bq. Same mass of old wood: 3 Bq. How old is it?24 → 12 → 6 → 3 = 3 half-lives. 3 × 5600 = 16 800 years.
5. What is the difference between irradiated and contaminated?Irradiated: exposed from outside, not radioactive. Contaminated: radioactive material is on or in it.
6. 4.5 × 10⁹ years ÷ 1600 years ≈ 2.8 million. What does it show for radium?That many half-lives have passed, so the original radium is gone. Today's radium is made by uranium decaying.
