Light Waves

Physics · Unit 3 · Waves · Last-minute notes · Book pages 113–122

Chapter in a nutshell

  • Light is a transverse wave; it reflects and refracts
  • Reflection: i = r, measured from the normal
  • Periscope: two mirrors (or prisms) at 45°
  • Into glass: slows, bends towards the normal
  • n = sin i ÷ sin r (glass ≈ 1.5, no units)
  • Practical: glass block, crosses, protractor
  • Critical angle: refraction angle = 90°
  • sin c = 1 ÷ n (glass c ≈ 42°)
  • TIR: denser → less dense, and i > c
  • Uses: fibres, endoscopes, prisms, reflectors
1Light and reflection
How do we see things?
Light is a transverse wave
  • The vibrations are at 90° to the direction the wave travels.
  • Light can be reflected and refracted.
Key term · medium

A material, such as glass or water, through which light can travel. Plural: media.

How fast does light travel?

Vacuum (and air)

3 × 10⁸ m/s

Air is almost exactly the same as a vacuum.

Glass

≈ 2 × 10⁸ m/s

Light slows down when it goes into glass.

Why it mattersLight changes speed when it moves from one medium into another. This change of speed causes refraction (section 2).
The law of reflection
angle of incidence = angle of reflection
i = r
Key term · normal

A line drawn at 90° to the mirror where the ray hits it.

i is between the incident ray and the normal. r is between the reflected ray and the normal.

Drawing ray diagrams

Ruler + protractor

Draw the normal as a dashed line at 90°.

Arrows

Put an arrow on every ray to show its direction.

Label angles

Mark i and r between each ray and the normal.

The simple periscope

Two plane mirrors, each at 45°.

  1. Light hits the top mirror at 45° to the normal and is turned through 90°.
  2. The bottom mirror turns it another 90°.
  3. It leaves parallel to how it started, so you can see over walls.
2Refraction and refractive index
Refraction: light changes direction
Key term · refraction

The change in direction of a ray when it crosses the boundary between two media, because its speed changes.

Rainbows

Rainbows are caused by refraction.

Towards or away from the normal?

Air → glass

Slows down. Bends towards the normal, so r is smaller than i.

Glass → air

Speeds up. Bends away from the normal.

Parallel sides

The ray comes out parallel to the way it went in.

Hitting the boundary at 90°

If the ray meets the boundary along the normal (at 90° to the surface), it does not change direction.

  • Angle of incidence = 0°, so angle of refraction = 0°.
  • It still slows down in the glass.
Used laterSemi-circular blocks and prisms let light in this way, so it goes in without bending.
Refractive index, n
refractive index = sin (angle of incidence) ÷ sin (angle of refraction)
n = sin i ÷ sin r
i = angle in airn has no units
  • Tells you how much a material bends light.
  • n is always greater than 1.
Finding the refractive index
A ray enters glass with i = 30° and is refracted at r = 19°. Find n.
Write downi = 30°, r = 19°
Equationn = sin i ÷ sin r
Substituten = sin 30° ÷ sin 19° = 0.5 ÷ 0.33
n = 1.52 (no units)

Glass vs water

Glass ≈ 1.5, water ≈ 1.3. Glass has the bigger n, so it refracts light more.

Common mistakePutting the angle in the glass on top. i is always the angle in air, so n > 1.

3Practicals with glass blocks
Refractive index of glass: set up
  1. Shine a ray from a ray box into one side of a rectangular glass block, so it comes out of the opposite side.
  2. Mark both rays with crosses.
  3. Draw around the block, then remove it.
Refractive index of glass: measure
  1. Join the crosses for the rays in and out. Join the entry and exit points for the ray inside.
  2. Draw the normal where the ray enters. Measure i and r with a protractor.
  3. Work out n = sin i ÷ sin r. Repeat for several angles and find the average.
Using a graph to find n

Plot sin i (up) against sin r (across) for each angle you tested.

Key factStraight line through the origin. Gradient = n.

Example

gradient = up ÷ across
= 0.6 ÷ 0.4 = 1.5

Finding the critical angle
  1. Aim the ray through the curved side at the centre of the flat side. It goes in along a radius (a normal), so it does not bend.
  2. Slowly increase the angle of incidence.
  3. When the ray runs along the flat surface (refraction angle 90°), i = critical angle.

Glass c ≈ 42° · Water c ≈ 49°

Triangular prisms and safety
  • A triangular prism refracts light twice.
  • It splits white light into a spectrum.
  • A 45° prism can also totally internally reflect light (section 5).
SafetyRay-box lamps get hot enough to burn skin and char paper. Glass blocks and prisms can splinter or shatter if knocked together.
4Critical angle and total internal reflection
What happens as i gets bigger?
i < c

Most light refracts out, away from the normal. A weak reflection.

i = c

The refracted ray runs along the boundary. Angle of refraction = 90°.

i > c

All the light is reflected: total internal reflection.

Critical angle and TIR
Key term · critical angle (c)

The angle of incidence for which the angle of refraction is 90°. It is the smallest angle of incidence at which light is totally internally reflected.

Key term · total internal reflection

Happens only when light travels towards a less optically dense medium (lower refractive index, e.g. glass → air) and the angle of incidence is greater than c.

The critical angle equation
sin (critical angle) = 1 ÷ refractive index
sin c = 1 ÷ n
c in degreesn has no units
  • To find c: work out 1 ÷ n, then use sin⁻¹.
  • Bigger n → smaller critical angle.
Finding the critical angle
A material has a refractive index of 1.45. What is its critical angle?
Write downn = 1.45
Equationsin c = 1 ÷ n
Substitutesin c = 1 ÷ 1.45 = 0.69
Inversec = sin⁻¹(0.69)
c = 43.6°
5Uses of total internal reflection
Turning light with prisms

Glass has c ≈ 42°. In a 45° prism, light hits the long face at 45°, which is more than 42°, so it is totally internally reflected.

Turn 90°

One TIR.

Turn 180°

Two TIRs send light back: bicycle and car reflectors.

Prismatic periscopes and binoculars
  1. Light enters face AB at 90°, so it does not bend.
  2. It hits face AC at 45°. TIR turns it 90°.
  3. A second prism turns it back.
Better than mirrorsA brighter, clearer image.

Binoculars: two prisms on each side fold the light path, so they can be short but still magnify a lot.

Optical fibres: light pipes

A very thin fibre: a glass core with a high refractive index, surrounded by glass with a lower refractive index.

The fibre is so narrow that light always hits the boundary at more than the critical angle, even when bent.

Endoscopes in medicine
  1. One bundle of fibres carries light down to light up the inside of the body.
  2. A second bundle carries the reflected light back to an eyepiece or camera to form an image.
Keyhole surgerySmall holes: less stressful, faster recovery. Engineers also use them to see inside machinery.
