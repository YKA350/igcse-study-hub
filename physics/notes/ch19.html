Solids, Liquids and Gases

Physics · Unit 5 · Solids, liquids and gases · Last-minute notes · Book pages 181–192

Chapter in a nutshell

  • Solid: close, regular, vibrate. Liquid: close, jumbled, move past. Gas: far apart, fast and random.
  • Heating raises particle KE (temperature) — or changes the state at constant temperature.
  • s.h.c. = energy to heat 1 kg by 1 °C (J/kg °C)
  • ΔQ = m × c × ΔT · practical: ΔQ = V × I × t, insulate
  • Gas pressure = molecules colliding with the walls
  • Boyle: p₁V₁ = p₂V₂ at constant temperature
  • Absolute zero = −273 °C = 0 K · K = °C + 273
  • Pressure law: p₁/T₁ = p₂/T₂ at constant volume, T in K
1Particles in solids, liquids and gases
Everything is made of moving particles

How the particles are arranged and how they move decides the state. Water can be ice, water or steam.

Spacing, arrangement, motion

Solid

  • Touching, in neat rows
  • Strong forces hold them
  • Vibrate on the spot

Liquid

  • Touching, but jumbled
  • Strong forces, not fixed
  • Move past each other

Gas

  • Far apart, no pattern
  • Very weak forces
  • Fast, random motion

Exam tipFor each state say the spacing (close / far), the arrangement (regular / random) and the motion (vibrate / move past each other / fast and random).
The particles explain everything

Solids

  • Rigid shape — strong forces lock particles in place.
  • Often very dense — closely packed.
  • Can’t be squashed — already as close as possible.

Liquids

  • Flow and take the shape of the bottom of the container.
  • Particles attract (stick together).
  • Much denser than gases, very hard to squash.

Gases

  • Large spaces → very low density.
  • Spread out to fill any container.
  • Easy to squash. Particles hitting the walls cause gas pressure.
Energy in → the temperature rises…

Heating increases the energy stored in the system.

Particles move and vibrate faster → more kinetic energy.

We measure this as a rise in temperature.

…or the state changes

Melting

Particles break free from their fixed positions and move around.

Evaporating / boiling

Particles escape from the liquid → a widely spaced gas.

RememberThe energy separates the particles, so the temperature stays constant until the change is complete.
2Specific heat capacity
Some things are harder to heat up
Key term · specific heat capacity, c

The energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C.

Different substances need different amounts of energy for the same temperature rise.

Heating water in a kettle
300 g of water is heated from 15 °C to boiling (100 °C). c = 4200 J/kg °C. How much energy is needed?
Write downm = 300 g = 0.3 kg, ΔT = 100 − 15 = 85 °C
EquationΔQ = m × c × ΔT
SubstituteΔQ = 0.3 × 4200 × 85
ΔQ = 107 100 J
Common mistakeForgetting to change g → kg. Use the change in temperature, not the final one.

In real life…

More energy is needed: some heats the kettle, some is lost to the surroundings, and some water evaporates.

Measuring specific heat capacity
  1. Find the mass m of the block with a balance.
  2. Record the start temperature.
  3. Switch on. Record voltage V, current I and time t.
  4. Note the highest temperature; find ΔT.
  5. Energy ΔQ = V × I × t, then c = ΔQ ÷ (m × ΔT).
  6. Water: weighed water in an insulated beaker with a lid and an immersion heater.
Finding c for aluminium
A 0.5 kg aluminium block warms from 20 °C to 50 °C when 16 500 J is supplied. Find c.
Write downm = 0.5 kg, ΔT = 50 − 20 = 30 °C
Equationc = ΔQ ÷ (m × ΔT)
Substitutec = 16 500 ÷ (0.5 × 30)
c = 1100 J/kg °C
Too big!Heat is lost, and some heats the heater and thermometer.
Finding c for water
A 2.4 kW kettle heats 1 kg of water from 20 °C to 100 °C in 2½ minutes. Find c.
EnergyΔQ = P × t = 2400 W × 150 s = 360 000 J
Write downm = 1 kg, ΔT = 100 − 20 = 80 °C
Substitutec = 360 000 ÷ (1 × 80)
c = 4500 J/kg °C
Why higher?Energy is lost to the surroundings and heats the kettle.
Why the answer comes out too big

Errors & fixes

  • Heat lost → ΔT too small → c too big. Fix: insulation (and a lid).
  • Energy also heats the heater and thermometer.
  • A drop of oil in the thermometer hole improves thermal contact.
SafetyHeater and block get hot enough to burn. Use a low-voltage supply. In water, don’t submerge the top of the immersion heater.
3Temperature during a change of state
The flat part of the graph

A · Heating water

Rises to 100 °C, then stays constant until all the water has turned to steam.

B · Heating naphthalene

The solid warms to 80 °C, stays flat while melting, then the liquid warms up.

Melting naphthalene
  1. Put solid naphthalene and a thermometer in a boiling tube, in a beaker of water (a water bath).
  2. Heat steadily. Record the temperature every 30 s.
  3. Plot a temperature–time graph. The flat section = the melting point.
Why naphthalene?Its melting point is low (80 °C), so a water bath is hot enough.
Energy goes into separating particles

The energy is used to overcome the forces between the molecules — not to increase their kinetic energy.

So the temperature doesn’t change until the change of state is complete.

Cooling curveThe same in reverse: it drops, stays flat while freezing or condensing, then drops again.

Cooling curve

Naphthalene: stay safe

Why a water bath?

It melts at 80 °C, so a water bath is hot enough. It is flammable, so heat it indirectly.

Safety

  • Avoid skin contact and breathing the vapour.
  • Stop heating once it has melted.
  • Don’t pull the thermometer out of the solid.
4Gas pressure and Boyle’s law
Why a gas pushes on its container
Key term · pressure

The force acting per unit area.

  • Molecules move randomly.
  • Each collision with a wall exerts a tiny force.
  • Huge numbers of collisions → a steady force per area = pressure.
Air is squashy!

Put your thumb over a syringe and push the plunger → the air compresses.

Let go → it springs back.

Gases compress because the molecules are far apart.

Squash a gas → its pressure goes up

A · p against V

A curve: double the pressure → the volume halves.

B · p against 1/V

A straight line through the origin: p ∝ 1/V.

Pressure × volume stays the same
p₁V₁ = p₂V₂
for a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature
p in kPa or PaV in m³ or cm³

Same temperature → same average speed. Smaller volume → walls hit more often → bigger average force → higher pressure.

Squashing 2 m³ of air
2 m³ of air at 100 kPa is squashed to 0.2 m³ at constant temperature. What is the new pressure?
Write downp₁ = 100 kPa, V₁ = 2 m³, V₂ = 0.2 m³
Equationp₁V₁ = p₂V₂ → p₂ = p₁V₁ ÷ V₂
Substitutep₂ = 100 × 2 ÷ 0.2
p₂ = 1000 kPa
UnitsAny units are fine (kPa, cm³) as long as you use the same ones on both sides.
Sense checkVolume ÷ 10 → pressure × 10.
5Absolute zero and the Kelvin scale
Cool a gas → its pressure falls

Fixed volume of gas, varying temperature

Extend the line back

The line reaches zero pressure at about −273 °C.

The coldest possible temperature

Pressure falls as the gas cools — but it can’t go below zero.

So there is a lowest possible temperature: absolute zero.

At absolute zero, particles have no kinetic energy → they exert no pressure.

−273

°C = absolute zero = 0 K

Kelvin starts at absolute zero
K = °C + 273
°C = K − 273 · a 1 K step = a 1 °C step
0 °C0 + 273 = 273 K (water freezes)
20 °C20 + 273 = 293 K (room)
400 K400 − 273 = 127 °C
Symbolsθ for °C, T for kelvin.
Hotter gas → faster molecules

Heating a gas gives its molecules more kinetic energy, so their average speed increases.

Key fact

The Kelvin temperature of a gas is proportional to the average kinetic energy of its molecules.

Heat a gas → its pressure goes up
p₁ ÷ T₁ = p₂ ÷ T₂
for a fixed mass of gas at constant volume
p in kPa or PaT in kelvin, K
T must be in kelvinAdd 273 to every °C value first.

p ∝ T: 200 K → 400 K doubles the pressure.

What the molecules are doing

Constant volume: heat it

  • Molecules move with a higher average speed.
  • They hit the walls harder and more often.
  • So the pressure increases. Cooling → slower → lower pressure.

Constant temperature: squash it

  • Average speed stays the same.
  • Same molecules in less space hit the walls more often.
  • So the pressure increases as the volume decreases.
Fixed amount of gasWhat happens
p and V, constant TVolume down → pressure up (p₁V₁ = p₂V₂)

Heating a sealed tin
A sealed tin holds air at 100 kPa and 20 °C. It is heated to 50 °C. What is the new pressure?
Kelvin firstT₁ = 20 + 273 = 293 K, T₂ = 50 + 273 = 323 K
Equationp₁/T₁ = p₂/T₂ → p₂ = p₁ × T₂ ÷ T₁
Substitutep₂ = 100 × 323 ÷ 293
p₂ ≈ 110 kPa
Common mistakeUsing °C: 100 × 50 ÷ 20 = 250 kPa — very wrong!
Sense checkA small rise in kelvin → a small rise in pressure.
Gas laws in real life

Cork pops

Rigid container + cork, heated: particles move faster → pressure rises → the cork pops out.

Piston

Heat the gas: its pressure pushes the piston out → the gas expands.

Beaker pushed into water

Water pressure rises with depth → the trapped air is compressed.

Close to 0 K

It gets harder and harder. Gases have been cooled to within thousandths of a degree — even helium and hydrogen liquefy. The gas laws stop working once a gas becomes a liquid.

Five ways to lose marks
Using °C in p₁/T₁ = p₂/T₂Always convert to kelvin: + 273.
Forgetting g → kg300 g = 0.3 kg before using ΔQ = m × c × ΔT.
Final temperature for ΔTΔT is the change: final − start.
Forgetting the conditionBoyle: constant temperature. Pressure law: constant volume.
“It gets hotter while melting”No — the temperature stays constant during a change of state.
