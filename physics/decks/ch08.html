Resistance and V = I × R

Resistance is how hard it is to push charge through something.

What is resistance?

Everything pushes back a bit

Key idea · resistance

All components oppose the flow of charge to some extent. Resistance is measured in ohms (Ω).

Low resistance

Charges pass easily and lose little energy.

High resistance

Lots of energy is needed to push charges through – usually lost as heat.

All components oppose the flow of charge. Low resistance: charges pass easily. High resistance: lots of energy needed, usually transferred as heat. Turning a volume knob changes the resistance in a circuit.

The equation

Voltage = current × resistance

voltage = current × resistance
V = I × R
V in volts (V)I in amps (A)R in ohms (Ω)

Rearranged

R = V ÷ I  and  I = V ÷ R

Exam warningWrite the real equation, not just the triangle.
We find the resistance of a component by comparing the current through it with the voltage across it.

Worked example

The doorbell

There is 12 V across a doorbell and the current is 0.1 A. What is its resistance?
Write downV = 12 V, I = 0.1 A
EquationV = I × R, so R = V ÷ I
SubstituteR = 12 ÷ 0.1
R = 120 Ω

Resistors in series

Add the resistances, then share out the voltage.

Resistors in series

In series, just add them up

total resistance = R₁ + R₂
Rtotal = R₁ + R₂
all in ohms (Ω)
Why?The charge has to go through both resistors, one after the other.
Good newsYou only need the series rule – not a parallel rule.

Worked example

2 Ω + 4 Ω on a 12 V supply

a) Total RR = 2 + 4 = 6 Ω
CurrentI = V ÷ R = 12 ÷ 6 = 2 A
b) 2 ΩV = I × R = 2 × 2 = 4 V
4 ΩV = I × R = 2 × 4 = 8 V
Check: 4 V + 8 V = 12 V ✓
Same currentThe 2 A flows through both resistors, because they are in series.

Series rules

The four series rules

  1. Total resistance = the sum of the resistances.
  2. The current is the same through every component.
  3. The supply voltage is shared: the voltages add up to the supply.
  4. The bigger resistor gets the bigger share of the voltage.
Exam orderTotal resistance → current → each voltage.

What controls the current?

More push, or more pushing back?

Start

1 cell, 1 resistor: current I.

More voltage

Double the cells → double the current.

More resistance

More or bigger resistors → smaller current.

RememberLess resistance → bigger current. More resistance → smaller current.
The current in a series circuit depends on the voltage of the supply and on the number and type of the other components.

Core practical · method

Taking the readings

  1. Set the variable resistor to its maximum.
  2. Close the switch. Record the current and voltage.
  3. Change the variable resistor. Record a new pair of readings.
  4. Repeat to get at least six pairs. For a diode, also reverse the supply to get negative values.
  5. Make a table. Plot current (I) against voltage (V).

Ohm's law · looking ahead

Why does a hot filament have more resistance?

SoHot filament → higher resistance. Cooling a wire lowers its resistance.
Ohm's law

The current in a conductor is directly proportional to the voltage across it, provided its temperature remains constant.

Cooling a wire lowers its resistance.

Extension

Superconductivity

Zero resistance

Near absolute zero (−273 °C) the vibrations stop. Some conductors then have zero resistance.

Uses

No energy loss, so electricity could be sent without losses. Maglev trains use superconducting magnets to hover.

Scientists are searching for materials that superconduct at higher temperatures.

Thermistor

Thermistor: hot → low R

How it behaves

Resistance decreases as temperature increases – a big change for a small temperature change.

Uses

Fire alarms, thermostats, freezers, computers.

Some thermistors' resistance increases with temperature, but for the exam you only need the ones whose resistance decreases as temperature increases.
