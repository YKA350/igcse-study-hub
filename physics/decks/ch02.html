Types of force

Spot every force on one object, give each its proper name, and draw it as an arrow.

Spotting forces

Four forces on a pushed car

Look only at forces on one object. Ignore very small ones.

  1. Push from the man, forwards.
  2. Friction in the engine, gears and axles. It opposes the motion.
  3. Weight: the Earth’s pull, acting down.
  4. Normal reaction: the road pushing up on the wheels.

Normal reaction

The surface pushes back

Key term · normal reaction

The push of a surface on an object resting on it.

“Normal” means at 90° to the surface.

RememberIt stops a car sinking into the road. Weight always acts straight down.

Resultant forces

Add up the forces along a line to find the one overall force.

Resultant force

Finding the resultant

When forces act along the same line, combine them into one resultant force.

  1. Choose one direction as positive (+). The other way is negative (−).
  2. Add all the forces, with their signs.
  3. Give the size and direction.
The resultant force is the one force that has the same effect as all the forces together.

Resultant force

Three examples

Balanced

F + (−F) = 0 N
Push = friction. The car does not move.

Same direction

300 + 300 = 600 N
Two people push. 600 N forwards.

With friction

300 + 300 − 300 = 300 N
Resultant is 300 N forwards.

Worked example

A cyclist

A cyclist pushes forwards with 120 N. Air resistance is 40 N and friction is 20 N, both backwards. Find the resultant force.

Work out120 − 40 − 20 = 60

Resultant force

Balanced and unbalanced

Balanced forces

Same size, opposite directions. No change in motion: still stays still; moving keeps a steady speed in a straight line.

Unbalanced forces

One force is bigger. The object accelerates (or decelerates) in the direction of the larger force.

Spec 1.11 describe the effects of forces between bodies such as changes in speed, shape or direction
Real examples

Tug of war and drag racer

Equal pulls: rope stays still (balanced). When one team tires, the forces become unbalanced and the rope accelerates towards the stronger team.

At first thrust ≫ friction + drag, so it accelerates. Friction and drag grow until they balance the thrust: then it moves at a steady speed.

Friction

The force that opposes motion: sometimes useful, sometimes a nuisance.

Friction

Friction opposes motion

Key term · friction

A force that opposes motion. It makes moving objects slow down and stop.

  • Kinetic energy is transferred to heat (thermal energy).
  • Acts when solids rub and when objects move through fluids.
Spec 1.16 know that friction is a force that opposes motion
Friction

Useful or a nuisance?

Useful for…

  • tyres gripping the road
  • effective brakes
  • walking without slipping
  • a parachute (fluid friction)

A nuisance when…

  • it wastes energy in machines
  • moving parts wear out
  • it slows racers: cyclists and swimmers wear special materials
On iceThere may be too little friction for even a powerful car to move. Skaters and sledges glide a long way.
Practical

Investigating friction

  1. Hanging weights pull the block over a pulley.
  2. Add weights until the block just starts to slide. Pull = maximum friction.
  3. Change one factor: the surfaces, or masses on the block.
  4. Rough surface (sandpaper) → more force needed.
SafetyCatch box with bubble wrap under the weights. Keep feet out of the drop zone.

Almost no friction

The air-track glider

Linear air-track

Friction is tiny. After one small push the glider covers equal distances in equal times. Its velocity stays constant, though no force acts.

Extension: “weightless” in space

Objects in orbit only seem weightless. Gravity still acts, but the craft and everything in it fall around the Earth together.

Forces change shape

Stretching, squashing, elastic behaviour and Hooke’s law.

Changing shape

Tension and compression

A rubber band stretches easily. Steel of the same length needs a huge force.

Temporary change

Shape returns when the force is removed: a bike suspension spring, a clock spring.

Permanent change

Shape stays changed: a crushed can, a crashed car, a bridge that fails.

Going back to the original shape

The ability of a material to recover its original shape after the forces causing deformation have been removed.

SpringsThey return to their original length, as long as they are not stretched too far.

Hooke’s law

Hooke’s law

Hooke’s law

The extension of a spring is directly proportional to the force (load) applied, as long as the limit of proportionality is not exceeded.

extension = stretched length − original length

Robert Hooke (1635–1703) hung loads on springs and plotted force against extension.

double the force → double the extension

Hooke’s law

Two limits on the graph

Limit of proportionality

Where the line starts to curve. After it, Hooke’s law is not obeyed.

Elastic limit

Stretch past it and the spring is permanently deformed. It will not go back to its original length.

The initial straight part of the force-extension graph is where the spring obeys Hooke's law.

Core practical

How does extension vary with force for springs, wires and rubber bands?

Core practical

Springs: the method

  1. Measure the unstretched length. Keep the half-metre rule vertical.
  2. Add loads one at a time. Read each length with a set square.
  3. Extension = new length − original length. Record a table.
  4. Check: unload one at a time and remeasure.
  5. Plot load (y) against extension (x).
Repeat with a metal wire and a rubber band instead of the spring.

Core practical

Wires, rubber bands and safety

  • Elastic stretching: the wire returns to its original length.
  • Plastic stretching: it doesn’t, and may get thinner (necking).
  • Rubber bands: extension can differ when loading and unloading.

Safety

  • Wear eye protection: a snapping band can hit your eyes.
  • Clamp the stand firmly to the bench.
  • Keep hands and feet away from under the load.
  • Don’t use very large loads.
