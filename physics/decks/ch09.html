Conductors and insulators

Why only insulators charge

Conductor

Electrons move freely, so any charge flows away – through you to earth.

Insulator

Electrons can’t move through it, so the charge stays where it is.

Charging by friction

Rub two insulators together and electrons move from one to the other.

Physics only
Practical

Charging by friction

  1. Check: hold the plastic rod, then the cloth, near tiny bits of paper. No pull → uncharged.
  2. Rub the rod and cloth together for a few seconds.
  3. Retest: both now attract the paper → charged.
  4. Repeat with conductors, or two objects of the same material. They do not charge.
ResultCharging needs two different insulators.

Practical

What happens when you rub?

Friction moves electrons from one material to the other – here from the rod onto the cloth.

Rod lost electrons → positive.
Cloth gained electrons → negative.

RememberCharge is separated, not created.

Forces between charges

Attract or repel?

+ and − pull together

The hanging rod swings towards the other rod.

+ and + (or − and −) push apart

The hanging rod swings away.

Key factCharged objects push and pull each other without touching.

Earthing

Hair-raising static

  • Charge spreads all over her body.
  • Each hair has the same charge, so the hairs repel.
  • The insulator stops the charge escaping. When she steps off, it flows away.
Key term · earthing

Providing a path for charges to escape to the ground.

Summary so far

The golden rules of static

  1. Only electrons move. Protons stay in the nucleus.
  2. Gain electrons → negative.
  3. Lose electrons → positive.

Like charges repel

Unlike charges attract.

Rubbing separates charge

It does not create it.

Attracting uncharged objects

A charged object makes electrons move in a neutral one. This gives induced charges.

Physics only
Induced charge

Why a balloon sticks to a wall

  1. A rubbed balloon gains electrons → negative.
  2. Near the wall, it repels electrons in the wall’s surface.
  3. The surface is left slightly positive.
  4. Unlike charges attract → the balloon sticks.

Induced charge

A charged ruler picks up paper

  1. The (+) ruler attracts electrons to the near edges of the paper.
  2. Near edges become negative; far edges are left +.
  3. The (−) parts are attracted to the ruler → the paper jumps up.
A (−) ruler?It pushes electrons away. The near side is left + and is still attracted.

Induced charge

What are induced charges?

Key term · induced charges

Charges that appear when a charged object makes the electrons in an uncharged object move.

Still neutral overallTake the charged object away and the charges spread out again.

Extension: a charged rod bends a thin stream of tap water, as charges are induced in the water.

Extension

The gold leaf electroscope

It detects charge. Bring a charged object near the metal plate.

(+) object: electrons are pulled up to the plate.

(−) object: electrons are pushed down onto the leaf.

Either wayRod and leaf get the same charge, so the leaf rises.

Exam skills

“Explain why the paper is attracted”

A full-mark answer

  1. The charged rod attracts (or repels) electrons in the paper.
  2. The near side of the paper gets the opposite charge (induced charge).
  3. Opposite charges attract, so the paper moves to the rod.
Common mistake“The paper is charged.” It is neutral overall – its electrons have only moved.

Uses of static

Paint spraying, inkjet printers, photocopiers and smoke precipitators.

Physics only
Uses of static

Electrostatic paint spraying

Drops spread out

All drops have the same charge, so they repel → a fine, even spray.

Drops are pulled in

The frame has the opposite charge, so paint reaches corners and the back. Less waste.

Uses of static

Inkjet printers

  1. Each tiny ink drop is given a charge.
  2. It falls between two deflecting plates.
  3. Electrostatic forces steer it to the right place on the paper.
  4. The plates’ charges change hundreds of times a second to build up letters and pictures.

Uses of static

How a photocopier works (1)

  1. A drum coated with selenium is given a positive charge.
  2. Bright light shines on the page. White parts reflect light onto the drum, which loses its charge there.
  3. Negatively charged toner (carbon powder) sticks to the charged areas.

Uses of static

How a photocopier works (2)

  1. Surplus toner is removed.
  2. Paper is pressed against the drum and picks up the toner image.
  3. The paper is heated to melt the toner and make the image permanent.
Key ideaCharge stays only where the image is dark, so toner only sticks there.

Uses of static

Cleaning smoke: precipitators

  1. Smoke from steel furnaces and coal-fired power stations carries ash and dust.
  2. Wires at about −50 000 V make the particles negative.
  3. They are attracted to earthed metal plates and stick.
  4. The plates are tapped; dust falls into boxes.
How much?A big power station removes 50–60 tonnes of dust per hour.

Dangers of static

Sparks near fuel can cause fires and explosions. Earthing makes it safe.

Physics only
Dangers of static

Sparks when refuelling

The danger

Friction with the air charges a flying aircraft. After landing, the charge could escape to earth as a spark.

During refuellingA spark could ignite the fuel vapour → fire or explosion.
Spec 2.27P explain the potential dangers of electrostatic charges, e.g. when fuelling aircraft and tankers
Dangers of static

The fix: earthing

Aircraft

Connect the plane to earth with a conductor as soon as it lands and before refuelling.

Road fuel tankers

Must also be earthed before fuel is transferred.

Watch out

Common mistakes

“Protons moved.”Only electrons move. A + object has lost electrons.
“Rubbing creates charge.”It only transfers it. The charges are equal and opposite.
“A metal rod in my hand charged up.”The charge flows away through you to earth.
“The paper is charged.”It is neutral overall. Its electrons moved: induced charges.
