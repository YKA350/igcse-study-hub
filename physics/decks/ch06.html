Danger

Mains voltage can kill

DangerMains voltage is 220–240 V in the UK, China, India and many other countries.

That is much higher than cells and batteries.

Direct contact can give a severe, possibly fatal, electric shock.

In simple wordsNever touch mains wires. Plugs, casings and safety devices are there to protect you.

Safety devices

Fuses, circuit breakers, earthing, double insulation and where to put the switch.

Fuses

How a fuse protects you

  1. The current is too large.
  2. The thin wire gets very hot.
  3. It melts – the fuse “blows”.
  4. The circuit is broken, so there is no current.
  5. This prevents shocks and reduces the risk of fire.
After a faultFix the fault, then fit a new fuse of the same rating.

Circuit breakers

Circuit breakers (trip switches)

If too large a current flows, a switch opens automatically, breaking the circuit.

Once the fault is fixed, simply press the reset button.

Where?Circuit breakers are found in modern consumer units.

Choosing a fuse

Pick the next size up

Common UK fuses: 3 A, 5 A, 13 A.

The correct fuse lets the normal current through but blows if the current is a little larger.

4 A5 A

Worked example

Which fuse for a 1 kW hairdryer?

A hairdryer is rated 230 V, 1 kW. Which fuse should it have: 3 A, 5 A or 13 A?
Write downP = 1 kW = 1000 W, V = 230 V
EquationP = I × V, so I = P ÷ V
SubstituteI = 1000 ÷ 230 = 4.35 A
Next size up → 5 A fuse

3 A ✗

Would blow in normal use.

5 A ✓

Just above 4.35 A.

13 A ✗

Would not protect the hairdryer.

Double insulation

No earth wire needed

Key idea · double insulation

The casing is made of an insulator (plastic) and all electrical parts are insulated so they can’t be touched.

Examples: plastic kettles, hairdryers.

In simple wordsIf the outside can’t conduct, it can’t become live – so no earth wire is needed.

Switches

Switches go in the live wire

Switch in live – safeOpening it cuts the appliance off from the live supply.
Switch in neutral – dangerWhen “off”, it is still joined to the live supply – a fault could give a shock.

Heating effect

Why a current heats a wire

Key idea · heating effect

When a current passes through a resistor, electrical energy is transferred to the resistor and its temperature rises.

Why? The charges collide with the atoms of the wire.

House wiring

Electrical power

What is power?

Key term · power

The rate of energy transfer: how many joules per second.

  • Unit: watt (W). 1 W = 1 J/s.
  • 1 kW = 1000 W
  • On the same mains voltage, a 100 W bulb glows more brightly than a 60 W bulb – it transfers more energy per second.

Worked example

Energy used by a 60 W bulb

a) A 60 W bulb is on for 20 s.
Write downP = 60 W, t = 20 s
EquationE = P × t
SubstituteE = 60 × 20
E = 1200 J (1.2 kJ)
b) The same bulb is on for 5 min.
Convertt = 5 × 60 = 300 s
EquationE = P × t
SubstituteE = 60 × 300
E = 18 000 J (18 kJ)
Energy transferred

Using E = I × V × t

A kettle on 230 V takes a current of 10 A for 2 minutes. How much energy does it transfer?
Convertt = 2 × 60 = 120 s
EquationE = I × V × t
SubstituteE = 10 × 230 × 120
E = 276 000 J (276 kJ)

Watch the time units!

1 min = 60 s
1 h = 60 × 60 = 3600 s

a.c. and d.c.

Alternating vs direct current

a.c. – mains

The current increases, decreases, then reverses direction, over and over – a wave shape.

d.c. – cells & batteries

Current is always in the same direction with the same value – a flat line.
