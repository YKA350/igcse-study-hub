Biomes and their global distribution

Rural environments

Biomes and their global distribution

Biomes and their global distribution (1/4)

Biosphere

All the plant and animal life on Earth. Its variety is biodiversity, divided into major units called biomes.

There are 11 biomes. Their distribution is controlled by climate, mainly temperature and moisture, so both change roughly with latitude.

Tropical rainforest

warm and humid near the equator, with the fastest plant growth and the greatest primary productivity (living matter). Largest area is the Amazon basin.

Biomes and their global distribution

Biomes and their global distribution (2/4)

Savanna

near the equator but drier, so lower productivity, with scattered bushes and grasses. Most extensive in Africa.

Desert

little vegetation because of lack of rain. Spans tropical (Sahara) and non-tropical (Asia) latitudes so temperatures vary.

Mediterranean

winter rain and summer drought, with drought-adapted small trees, scrub and grass.

Biomes and their global distribution

Biomes and their global distribution (3/4)

Deciduous forest

trees shed leaves because of cold winters. North America, Europe, East Asia.

Temperate grassland

grasses dominate in dry continental interiors: the Prairies and Steppes.

Boreal (coniferous) forest

a belt across high latitudes of North America and Eurasia, with needle leaves resisting cold and moisture loss.

Biomes and their global distribution

Biomes and their global distribution (4/4)

Tundra

long bitter winters, strong winds and little precipitation, with stunted grasses, mosses and lichens around the North Pole.

Highland

high mountains, similar to tundra, with temperature falling with altitude and snow.

Goods and services of ecosystems (2/4)

Cultural

Recreational and spiritual benefits

Ecosystem

A basic working unit of nature: living things (plants and animals) and their physical environment (sunlight, air, water, rock, soil) linked together.

It can be any size from a pond to a biome. Biomes are global ecosystems.

Goods and services of ecosystems

Goods and services of ecosystems (3/4)

Ecosystems are open systems with inputs (solar radiation, rainfall, gases, minerals from weathering), stores (plants, animals, soil, decomposers, nutrients) and outputs (heat, gases such as CO2, eroded soil).

Goods are material products taken directly, such as timber, fuelwood, fodder, food (honey, fungi, fruit, nuts), medicinal plants and water.

Services are long-term benefits, such as removing pollutants, emitting oxygen, recycling nutrients and water, maintaining biodiversity and recreation.

Goods and services of ecosystems

Goods and services of ecosystems (4/4)

Forests store carbon and take CO2 from the air; without them the climate would warm severely. Mangroves protect coasts.

Impacts of ecosystem exploitation

Rural environments

Impacts of ecosystem exploitation

Impacts of ecosystem exploitation (1/2)

The greatest human impact on ecosystems globally is farming.

Subsistence farming is usually tolerable unless populations are dense; commercial farming can wipe out whole ecosystems because of its scale, technology and drive for profit.

Arable farming feeds more people per hectare than livestock farming.

Impacts of ecosystem exploitation

Impacts of ecosystem exploitation (2/2)

Intensive farming concentrates inputs (even artificial ecosystems in glasshouses); extensive farming uses large areas (hill sheep, prairie).

About half of the world's tropical rainforest has gone, mainly for hardwood timber and for land used for other purposes.

Impacts of ecosystem exploitation

Case study: Brazilian rainforest (1/4)

Brazil holds about a quarter of the world's rainforest and over 15 per cent was lost between 1970 and 2015.

Causes of deforestation

cattle ranching about 80 per cent, plantations about 10 per cent (soy, palm oil and sugar cane for biofuels, bananas, pineapples, tea, coffee), with logging, small-scale farming and others making up the rest.

Logging is a small direct cause but sale of the timber makes clearing profitable.

Impacts of ecosystem exploitation

Case study: Brazilian rainforest (2/4)

Pasture and plantation soils fail within a few years, so new forest is cleared. Roads let in miners (gold, bauxite) and dam builders (hydro reservoirs on Amazon tributaries).

Selective logging and agro-forestry are more sustainable. Indigenous people use forest resources sustainably, with shifting cultivation that lets forest regenerate.

Indigenous people are displaced by logging, mining, ranches, plantations, reservoirs and roads. Tribes fell from 330 in 1900 to about 240 and traditional knowledge is lost.

Impacts of ecosystem exploitation

Case study: Brazilian rainforest (3/4)

Soil erosion

clearing breaks the nutrient cycle and heavy rain strips thin topsoil, silting rivers.

River pollution

mercury from gold mining poisons fish and people.

Local climate change

fewer trees means less evapotranspiration, so it becomes drier and warmer.

Impacts of ecosystem exploitation

Case study: Brazilian rainforest (4/4)

Global warming

less CO2 is absorbed and burning releases stored carbon, adding to the greenhouse effect.

Biodiversity loss

over two-thirds of plant species and about half of known animals live there. Around 137 species are lost each day (50 000 a year), and parts of Amazonia could lose 30 to 45 per cent of main species by 2030.

The deforestation rate has fallen since 2004, but there is conflict between developers and conservationists.

Characteristics of rural environments

Rural environments

Characteristics of rural environments

Characteristics of rural environments (1/3)

Urbanisation is making the world more urban and rural areas less populated. Rural environments have eight components: landscape, population density, settlement, employment, land use, accessibility, development and conservation.

Landscape

scattered settlements with farmland, shaped by geology, relief and climate.

Population density is low (but can be high in Bangladesh).

Characteristics of rural environments

Characteristics of rural environments (2/3)

Settlement

villages, hamlets and farmsteads.

Employment

mostly primary (farming, fishing, forestry, minerals), poorly paid, which drives migration to towns.

Land use

farming mainly, plus forestry, mining, and increasingly leisure and tourism.

Characteristics of rural environments

Characteristics of rural environments (3/3)

Accessibility

remoteness limits development, but better roads allow leisure.

Development

population pressure needs more food or new jobs.

Conservation

protects biodiversity and absorbs carbon.

Rural change in the UK

Rural environments

Rural change in the UK

Rural change in the UK (1/3)

Urban fringe

Suburbanisation (large housing estates), services, industrial, business and science parks. Planners protect greenfield sites with green belts, but some are released for housing

Commuter belt

Commuters attracted by cheaper, bigger housing, fast transport and quality of life; villages become dormitories with modernised houses and supermarkets replacing crafts, and communities divide

Rural change in the UK

Rural change in the UK (2/3)

Accessible countryside

Farming mechanises and merges into agri-businesses, so farmers diversify; day-trip recreation focuses on honeypots with crowds and traffic; retirement migration

Remote countryside

Depopulation and farm decline lead to a negative multiplier of lost services. Second homes do not stop it. National parks bring tourism jobs and counter-urbanisation through broadband home working

Rural change in the UK

Rural change in the UK (3/3)

The UK is in the late stage of urbanisation, with people moving out from cities. Change depends on distance and access to cities, giving four rural types: urban fringe, commuter belt, accessible countryside and remote countryside.

The challenges

changes in farming, new rural income, better rural life, protecting the environment.

Rural change in China and Kenya

Rural environments

Rural change in China and Kenya

Rural change in China and Kenya

Rural change in emerging and developing countries is driven by economic development, technology, population growth and rural-urban migration, and often causes problems.

Rural change in China and Kenya

China (1/2)

GDP is the largest in the world, but per capita GDP is $7295 with 1.35 billion people. Urban share rose from 21 per cent in 1990 to 56 per cent in 2015.

Villages lose young adults, farming loses labour and output falls, while cities need food and spread over farmland.

Low productivity because of little mechanisation, limited fertiliser, state-owned land and failed communes (Great Famine 1958–61 killed up to 40 million), then tiny family farms, and floods, droughts and earthquakes.

Rural change in China and Kenya

China (2/2)

China imports more food, and rural people live in poor housing with few services, trapped in poverty.

Rural change in China and Kenya

Kenya

GDP per head is 20 per cent of China's; a quarter of 45 million live in cities and urbanisation is over 4 per cent a year. Males leave, so those left cannot do heavy farming and suffer malnutrition.

Push factors

low farm productivity, poor schools and health care, corruption (land taken as bribes), more droughts, HIV/AIDS help only in cities, and commercial agriculture taking the best land.

Diversification of farming and farms

Diversification of farming and farms

Diversification of farming and farms (1/3)

More income from farming

Farm shops and farmers' markets, organic farming, pick-your-own, novel products (lavender, ostrich meat), B and B and camping

Non-farm uses

Leisure and recreation (shooting, off-road, mountain biking), redevelopment (barns into homes, offices), energy (wind and solar farms)

Diversification of farming and farms

Diversification of farming and farms (2/3)

UK farming gives about 1 per cent of GDP and jobs, but output rose through mechanisation, bigger farms, specialisation and technology (fertilisers, pesticides, breeding, genetic engineering).

Retailers press for efficiency, and productivity growth is slowing.

GM crops

for are higher yields, cheaper and better food, fewer herbicides. Against are contamination, health fears, costly feedstock and seedless plants.

Diversification of farming and farms

Diversification of farming and farms (3/3)

The UK grows only 60 per cent of its food because people want cheap food and year-round seasonal produce.

Farms can mix options, which helps keep people in rural areas.

Sustainable rural living

Rural environments

Sustainable rural living

Sustainable rural living

Three aspects

environmental (little damage, harmony with land), economic (supports locals and the nation) and social (good quality of life: clean water, sanitation, education, health).

It needs a small carbon and ecological footprint.

Ways to raise food output

more land, bigger holdings, mechanisation, irrigation, breeding, crop rotation, fertilisers, pesticides, GM crops.

Sustainable rural living

China

Needs more productive yet greener farming, labour kept in the countryside and better rural life. All farmable land is used and is being built over.

Water transfer serves industry and homes first, which suggests the focus is on the secondary sector. Tourism centres on cities and heritage, not the countryside.

Better housing, water, sanitation and pollution control are needed to curb migration.

Sustainable rural living

Kenya (1/3)

Exam tip Both countries are far from sustainable rural areas, and rural-urban migration is unlikely to slow.

Horticulture is two-thirds of agricultural exports, supporting half a million people (135 000 workers). Kenya is the largest developing flower exporter after Colombia.

75 per cent of flowers come from large farms around Lake Naivasha, flown to Europe.

Sustainable rural living

Kenya (2/3)

Costs

lake pressure from droughts, population rising from 50 000 to over 350 000, untreated sewage and chemical runoff, falling water and fish.

Foreign agribusinesses buy subsistence land, so less food is grown locally and prices rise. Food miles give a big carbon footprint. Benefit is foreign currency.

Arid and semi-arid lands face poverty and food insecurity, with fuelwood use and over-used water.

Sustainable rural living

Kenya (3/3)

Solutions are irrigation, services, aid (corruption worries donors), agribusinesses sharing profits and technical help.

Wildlife tourism in national parks helps, but foreign firms take the profits, so Kenya needs local-based eco-tourism.
