Coastal processes

Coastal environments

Coastal processes

Coastal processes

Coast

The transition zone between land and sea. The coastline is the actual frontier. The onshore zone reaches up to about 60 km inland and the offshore zone about 370 km out to sea.

  1. Coastal diversity comes from the sea (physical processes, sea level, water depth), the land (geology, relief, river sediment), weather and climate (wind, rain, temperature, storms, climate change) and human activity (settlement, development, management).
  2. Processes are marine (sea) and terrestrial (land).
Spec 2.1a, 2.1b Physical processes at work on the coast
Coastal processes

Marine processes (1/2)

  1. Waves are created by wind friction on the sea. Strength depends on wind strength, how long it blows and the fetch (distance it blows over).
  2. In shallow water friction with the sea bed makes the wave tip forward and break. The swash runs up the beach, then the backwash runs back down by gravity.

Coastal processes

Marine processes (2/2)

  1. Erosion by waves: hydraulic action (force of waves and compressed air in cracks), abrasion (stones hurled at cliffs) and corrosion (solution of rock by sea water). Attrition makes carried material smaller and rounder but does not erode the coast.
  2. Longshore drift: waves hit the beach at an angle, swash carries material up the beach and backwash returns it straight down, so material moves along the coast in a zigzag. Smaller material travels further.

Coastal processes

Land processes

  1. Weathering: breakdown of rock by freeze-thaw, salt crystal growth, acid rain and plant roots.
  2. Erosion by wind and rain.
  3. Mass movement: rock falls, slumping and landslides under gravity.

Coastal landforms

Coastal environments

Coastal landforms

Erosional landforms

  1. Headlands and bays form where bands of hard rock stick out and softer rock like clay is eroded back. A discordant coast has rock outcrops at right angles to the sea (headlands and bays). A concordant coast has outcrops parallel to the sea and is straighter. The Purbeck coast in southern England shows chalk and limestone headlands and bays cut in clays and sands.
  2. Cliffs and wave-cut platforms: waves undercut the cliff base to make a wave-cut notch. The overhang collapses, the cliff retreats and keeps its steep face, and a gently sloping wave-cut platform is left.
  3. Caves, arches, stacks and stumps: waves attack weaknesses such as joints and faults in a headland, by hydraulic action and abrasion, to open a cave. The cave breaks through to form an arch. The roof collapses to leave a stack. The stack erodes to a stump. Old Harry Rocks in southern England is an example.

Coastal landforms

Depositional landforms (1/2)

  1. Deposition happens where material builds up faster than waves remove it, usually with constructive waves.
  2. Beaches are the most common. Material comes from longshore drift, offshore, and river sediment. Features include storm beaches, berms (ridges marking high tide marks) and beach cusps. Finer material gives gentler slopes.
  3. Spits are long narrow beaches of sand or shingle joined to land at one end, formed by longshore drift in one main direction where the coast changes direction or at an estuary. The end may curve with changing wind. Salt marshes form behind them.
  4. Bars form when a spit grows across a bay and joins two headlands, trapping a lagoon (for example Sotavento, Fuerteventura).
Spec 2.2a, 2.1b Distributions and features of the world’s coastal ecosystems
Coastal landforms

Depositional landforms (2/2)

  1. Tombolos are spits that grow out to join an island (Sharp Island to Kiu Tau, Hong Kong).
  2. Cuspate forelands are triangular accumulations that may form where two drifts meet.
  3. Sand dunes form from dry beach sand blown inland into ridges. Older ridges are colonised by plants and stabilised.
  1. People use landforms for tourism (Jurassic coast World Heritage Site), golf courses on dunes, marinas behind spits, wind farms and power stations.

Factors affecting coastal environments

Vegetation

  1. The longer a landform exists the more likely it is to be colonised. Plants must cope with salt in air and soil. Vegetation protects and preserves landforms such as dunes and mangrove swamps.
Spec 2.1c Role of erosional and depositional processes in the development of landforms
Factors affecting coastal environments

Sea-level changes

  1. Global warming brings rising sea levels that drown low coasts, and many dense populations live on coastal lowlands. In the Ice Age sea level fell as water was locked in ice and rose again when it melted.
  2. Rising sea level gives a submerged coastline with rias (drowned river valleys) and fjords (drowned glacial valleys).
  3. Falling sea level gives an emerging coastline with raised beaches (old wave-cut platforms and beaches now above sea level), sometimes with relict cliffs, caves, arches and stacks.

Factors affecting coastal environments

Human activities

  1. Settlement on coastal lowlands.
  2. Economic development: farming, industry, fishing, ports and harbours, tourism and energy.
  3. Coastal management such as sea walls and groynes. These can change the shape of the coastline.

Coastal ecosystems of the world

Coastal environments

Coastal ecosystems of the world

Coastal ecosystems of the world

  1. Four ecosystems: coral reefs and mangroves in the tropics, salt marshes and sand dunes across the world.
Spec 2.2a Distributions and features of the world’s coastal ecosystems
Coastal ecosystems of the world

Coral reefs

  1. Reefs are built entirely of living organisms and are huge deposits of calcium carbonate.
  2. Global factors: temperature (minimum 18 degrees C, best 23 to 25), light (so shallow water), depth (mostly under 25 m) and salinity (salt water only).
  3. Local factors: wave action (oxygenated water), limited exposure to air, and little sediment (clear water).
  4. Highest biodiversity is in South-East Asia and northern Australia (Great Barrier Reef).
Spec 2.1c Role of erosional and depositional processes in the development of landforms
Coastal ecosystems of the world

Mangroves

  1. Mostly within 30 degrees of the equator, as far as North Island, New Zealand. They grow in the intertidal zone, flooded by the sea with changing salt levels and temperature, in choking mud and heat. South-East Asia has the highest mangrove biodiversity.
  2. Adaptations: salt filtration, snorkel-like roots that take in air, and prop roots or buttresses to hold trunks upright.
  3. Roots trap mud and sand and build up land. Floating seedlings drift and take root in new mud.
Spec 2.3b Causes of coastal flooding
Coastal ecosystems of the world

Salt marshes

  1. In the intertidal zone, between permanently covered mudflats and land vegetation, in sheltered places such as bay heads and estuaries (brackish water).
  2. Salinity and flooding frequency decide plants: low marsh floods twice daily, high marsh only in storms. Pioneer plants such as salicornia and spartina give way to sea purslane, sea lavender and rushes.
  3. Creeks slow tidal energy and plants slow waves, so silt builds up and the marsh grows seawards.
Spec 2.3b Causes of coastal flooding
Coastal ecosystems of the world

Coastal sand dunes

  1. Need a wide beach with lots of sand, an onshore prevailing wind and places for sand to collect.
  2. Dry sand blows to the back of the beach and collects around an obstacle such as driftwood to form an embryo dune. New dunes form seaward, giving ridges parallel to the shore.
  3. Plants stabilise them, with more cover on older ridges: embryo dunes, foredunes, yellow dunes (marram grass), fixed grey dunes, dune slack and heath. This sequence is a plant succession. First plants face salt, dryness, wind and burial by sand.

Spec 2.2b Abiotic and biotic characteristics of one named coastal ecosystem
Coastal ecosystem characteristics

Coastal ecosystem characteristics (2/2)

  1. Ecosystems have biotic parts (plants and animals) and abiotic parts (climate, minerals). All survive by nutrient cycling around three stores: the nutrient source (soil, or sea water for reefs), biomass and litter.
  2. In mangroves, marshes and dunes the soil store is mud or sand. In reefs, sea water is the soil, with nutrients from currents and rivers. Tiny algae inside the coral turn sunlight into energy.
  3. Reef biomass is coral polyps, seaweeds and a huge variety of fish, crustaceans and invertebrates. Litter is dead coral and remains of dead organisms.
  4. Reef food web: producers (plankton, ocean plants), herbivores, carnivores, scavengers, detritus feeders and decomposers such as bacteria and fungi.

Spec 2.2a, 2.2c Distributions and features of the world’s coastal ecosystems
Coastal ecosystems under threat

Coral reefs

  1. Value: biodiversity (Great Barrier Reef has 700 coral, 1500 fish and 4000 mollusc species), protection from tropical storms, fish for developing countries, and tourism with over 150 million holidaymakers a year in reef areas.
  2. Threats: pollution, overfishing, quarrying coral, touch from humans, development (urban, fishing, farming and tourism lead to sewage, fertiliser, silt, eutrophication and decline), and coral bleaching from warmer water. 27 per cent of reefs are highly threatened and 31 per cent medium threat.
Spec 2.1c Role of erosional and depositional processes in the development of landforms
Coastal ecosystems under threat

Mangroves

  1. Valuable as nurseries for fish and shellfish, as wildlife habitat, silt traps, timber, and storm surge protection. In the 2004 tsunami two people died in a Sri Lankan village with dense mangrove and up to 6000 in a similar village without.
  2. Causes of loss: just over half to aquaculture (38 per cent shrimp, 14 per cent fish), a quarter deforestation, plus land reclamation, freshwater diversion and herbicides. They are wrongly seen as disease-ridden.

Coastal ecosystems under threat

Case study: management of the mangrove (Bangladesh)

  1. Low-lying and densely populated (about 1000 per square km, up to 25 million people less than 1 m above sea level). Its Coastal Zone Policy plants mangroves on delta sediment, gaining over 120 000 hectares of new land and protecting against storm surges. The Sundarbans is the largest single mangrove tract in the world. The only real threat is clearance for shrimp and fish farms.
Spec 2.2c, 2.2a How small- and large-scale coastal ecosystems are threatened by people and their activities
Coastal ecosystems under threat

Salt marshes and sand dunes

  1. Salt marsh uses: trap silt, fish and crustacean nurseries, protection from waves and rising sea. Threats: reclamation for farmland, ports and industry, industrial and farm pollution (eutrophication), marinas and recreation, and global warming.
  2. Dunes are probably the least threatened globally but in the UK trail biking and horse riding cause blow-outs, and they are built over or used for golf and caravan sites. Trees planted to stabilise dunes destroy the dune ecosystem.

Coastal conflicts

Coastal environments

Coastal conflicts

Coastal conflicts

System

Inputs, stores, transfers and outputs. The coast is a system: sediment inputs come from rivers and cliff erosion, stores are beaches, spits, forelands and dunes, longshore drift transfers it, and loss to open sea is an output. Balance of inputs and outputs is equilibrium.

  1. The overriding conflict is between conservation and development.
  2. Stakeholders and needs: residents (housing, clean environment), employers (labour, space), farmers (drained land, shelter), fishermen (harbours, unpolluted water), port authorities, transport companies, tourists (beaches, hotels, heritage) and developers (greenfield sites).
Spec 2.3b Causes of coastal flooding
Coastal conflicts

Lyme Bay, southern England

  1. At least six stakeholders including wildlife. Scallop dredgers (about 180 000 pounds a year) damage the seabed and smother species. Potters (about 180 000) find pots tangled by dredgers. Divers (87 000) and anglers (nearly 250 000) have low impact. Trawlers target mullet, lemon sole, sprat and herring. Tourists need a healthy environment.
  2. Solution: separate uses into allocated areas, but marking and policing sea areas is difficult.

Coastal flooding

Coastal environments

Coastal flooding

Coastal flooding

  1. Storm surges are the greatest flood threat: very low air pressure raises high-tide sea height and strong onshore winds drive it at the coast. Tsunamis from earthquakes also flood (2004 Indian Ocean). Venice has a constant and rising risk, and global warming raises sea levels.
  2. Prediction: use historic records to find high-risk areas and forecast storms, surges, earthquakes and tsunamis, including scale and likely damage.
  3. Prevention: flood defences, emergency centres, moving homes and activities out of risk areas, planning that avoids high-risk areas and flood-resistant building, warning systems and public education.
CarefulKnow the difference between prediction (forecasting) and prevention (reducing the risk).

Coastal management strategies

Coastal environments

Coastal management strategies

Coastal management strategies

  1. Coastal erosion is natural. Holderness (England) cliffs of soft sands, gravels and clays retreat at about 1 m a year, sometimes 10 m, and the coast has retreated 4 km in 2000 years. Management is based on coastal cells, self-contained sections for sediment movement. Aims: resolve conflicts and manage big risks of erosion and flooding.
Spec 2.3b, 2.3c Causes of coastal flooding
Coastal management strategies

Hard engineering

  1. Sea walls, recurved walls, groynes (wooden or steel), revetments, gabions (wire cages of rocks) and rip-rap (boulders). Work against waves.
  2. Problems: costly (sea wall repair up to 3000 pounds a metre), groynes starve beaches downdrift, cannot keep pace with rising seas, spoil natural beauty. Rip-rap is cheaper but may shift and be undercut.
Spec 2.1a Physical processes at work on the coast
Coastal management strategies

Soft engineering

  1. Beach replenishment, offshore bars, fencing, hedging and replanting vegetation or restoring ecosystems, and cliff regrading to reduce slope. They absorb wave energy, are environmentally friendlier and cheaper.
Spec 2.3c There are different coastal management strategies, including soft engineering
Coastal management strategies

Managed retreat

  1. Abandon defences and let low land flood into salt marsh, a natural storm defence and more habitat. It is cheap but costs compensation, so it is not suitable for urban or high-quality farmland.
Spec 2.3b Causes of coastal flooding
Coastal management strategies

Case study: The Gambia (developing)

  1. Narrow country of 11 000 square km with an 80 km ocean coast. Most of Banjul is under 1 m above sea level, and tourist infrastructure south of the river is at risk. Erosion has speeded up over three decades with sea-level rise. A UN-funded project seeks resilience but actions are costly and funding is hard to find.
Spec 2.3b Causes of coastal flooding
Coastal management strategies

Case study: Sri Lanka (emerging)

  1. Island with 1560 km of coast and nearly half the population in the coastal zone. Problems: sewage pollution, mangrove and wetland reclamation, coral loss, aquaculture impacts, south-west erosion and tsunami and storm surge exposure. The 2004 tsunami killed over 30 000 and left over 1.5 million homeless.
  2. Coastal Zone Management Plan since 1990 has broad aims. For tsunamis it promotes a culture of safety: awareness, drills, watch towers and warning buoys. In 2012 an alert led to the successful evacuation of 1 million people. 75 warning towers were used.
Spec 2.3b Causes of coastal flooding
Coastal management strategies

Case study: the UK (developed)

  1. Risks are assessed for stakeholders, the coast is split into cells and sub-cells, and each gets a plan. Isle of Wight plan: do nothing (low risk), hold the line (hard engineering at resorts such as Cowes, Shanklin, Ventnor) or managed retreat in low-lying areas such as Bembridge.
  2. The east coast is sinking over 6 mm a year. Essex lost about 40 per cent of its salt marsh. At Abbotts Hall Farm, Blackwater Estuary (RSPB, 2002), five breaches in sea walls flooded 80 hectares of farmland to become salt marsh. Managed retreat there was easy since the land had low value. It is cheaper than sea walls, supplies sediment to other areas and looks natural, but some argue governments must protect land.
