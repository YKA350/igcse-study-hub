The hydrological cycle: a closed system

The hydrological cycle: a closed system (4/6)

Closed system

A system with no inputs or outputs across its boundary. The global water cycle is closed, so the total amount of water is fixed.

Store

A place where water is held, such as the atmosphere, land (rivers, lakes, soil, groundwater, ice) and the sea.

Flow (transfer)

Movement of water between stores.

The hydrological cycle: a closed system

The hydrological cycle: a closed system (5/6)

Exam tip Be able to say the difference between a store and a flow. A store holds water, a flow moves it between stores.

Fresh water is constantly recycled. Water neither enters nor leaves the Earth and its atmosphere, so the amount is fixed.

Over 95 per cent of the Earth's water is in the sea, mostly liquid with some ice.

The hydrological cycle: a closed system

The hydrological cycle: a closed system (6/6)

Land stores include rivers, lakes, reservoirs, vegetation, groundwater in bedrock, and snow and ice in glaciers, ice sheets and snowfields.

The amount of water cannot change but the share held in each store can, because of changes in the Sun's energy. More solar energy means more evaporation and possibly melting of ice sheets and glaciers.

Some rain that falls on land never reaches the sea. It returns to the atmosphere through transpiration and evaporation from soil and water bodies.

Drainage basins and their features

River environments

Drainage basins and their features

Drainage basins and their features (1/5)

Drainage basin (catchment)

The area drained by a river and its tributaries.

Watershed

The boundary along high ground that divides neighbouring drainage basins.

Open system

A system with inputs and outputs, so the amount of water in it varies over time. A drainage basin is an open system.

Drainage basins and their features

Drainage basins and their features (2/5)

Channel network

The system of surface and underground channels that collects and carries the rain falling on the basin. Drainage density is the number of channels per unit area.

Careful Know the difference between permeable rock, which lets water through, and impermeable rock, which does not and so increases run off.
Inputs

energy from the Sun, precipitation, and possibly water from tributary basins.

Drainage basins and their features

Drainage basins and their features (3/5)

Outputs

river discharge, and evaporation and transpiration, which later fall as rain in another basin.

The source is in high ground near the watershed where most rain falls. Tributaries join the main river at confluences. The mouth or estuary is where it meets the sea.

A basin may drain directly to the sea, may be part of a larger basin (the Rio Negro is part of the Amazon basin), or may drain to an inland sea or lake such as the Caspian Sea, the Aral Sea or Lake Victoria.

Drainage basins and their features

Drainage basins and their features (4/5)

The network can change quickly. In flood there are more and longer channels than in dry periods.

Each basin is unique in size, shape, rock type, relief and land use. These control how fast water moves through it.

Woodland holds water and slows run off. Clearing it for farming speeds run off. Towns speed it even more because roofs, roads and drains deliver water quickly to rivers.

Drainage basins and their features

Drainage basins and their features (5/5)

Rock type and relief are physical factors people cannot change. Land use is easily changed by people.

River regimes and hydrographs

River environments

River regimes and hydrographs

River regimes and hydrographs (1/2)

Discharge

The volume of water passing a point on a river each second, measured in cumecs (cubic metres per second).

River regime

The pattern of changes in a river's discharge through the year.

Hydrograph

A graph of discharge over time.

River regimes and hydrographs

River regimes and hydrographs (2/2)

A regime usually reflects local climate, especially rainfall. The Ganges in Bangladesh has high discharge from June to October because of monsoon rainfall that can exceed 2750 mm.

The Thames at Reading has its highest flows in winter (February and March) and a jagged line, because showers and short heavy rain cause day to day peaks.

Drainage basins are home to many people because of fertile soils and food, so knowing how rivers behave after heavy rain helps work out flood risk and area at risk.

River regimes and hydrographs

Storm hydrographs (1/2)

Storm hydrograph

A graph showing how a river's discharge changes after a rainstorm.

Lag time

The delay between peak rainfall and peak river discharge.

The shorter the lag time, the quicker water reaches the channel, the steeper the rise in discharge and the greater the flood chance.

River regimes and hydrographs

Storm hydrographs (2/2)

A line on the graph can mark the discharge above which the river will flood.

Base flow is the normal discharge of the river. Storm flow is the extra discharge caused by the storm.

River regimes and hydrographs

Factors affecting river regimes (1/2)

Amount and intensity of rain

heavy rain cannot sink in, so it runs off quickly.

Temperature

below freezing, precipitation is snow that can take weeks to melt. If the ground is frozen, meltwater reaches the river fast.

Slope

steep slopes give rapid run off, gentle slopes let water soak in and delay it.

River regimes and hydrographs

Factors affecting river regimes (2/2)

Rock type

impermeable rock speeds run off, permeable rock allows infiltration and percolation and slows delivery.

Vegetation and land use

plants intercept rain and delay it. Bare soil, rock, tarmac and concrete speed it up.

Human intervention

dams and reservoirs hold water back and reduce flood risk, sluice gates control outflow, and abstraction for human use changes regimes.

Fluvial processes

River environments

Fluvial processes

Fluvial processes

Rivers shape landforms through erosion, transport and deposition. Weathering and mass movement work alongside them.

Fluvial processes

Weathering and mass movement

Careful Weathering is the breakdown of rock in place. Erosion involves removal of material by a moving agent such as a river. Do not mix them up.

Slumping happens when a river cuts away the bottom of a valley side, making the slope unstable. Heavy rain makes weathered material heavier and acts as a lubricant, so it slides down. Landslides are sudden slumping.

Soil creep is slow movement of weathered material downslope under gravity. It collects at the bottom and is eroded by the river.

Fluvial processes

Erosion (1/2)

Hydraulic action

Force of water hitting the bed and banks dislodges material, most likely when discharge is high

Abrasion

Material carried by the river rubs against the bed and sides like sandpaper, widening and deepening the channel

Fluvial processes

Erosion (2/2)

Corrosion (solution)

Minerals in channel rocks are dissolved by flowing water

Attrition is different. Particles in the load collide and become rounder and smaller. It does not erode the channel.

Fluvial processes

Transport (1/2)

Traction

Large boulders rolled along the bed

Saltation

Small boulders and pebbles bounced along the bed

Suspension

Lighter material carried in the flow

Solution

Material dissolved in the water

Fluvial processes

Transport (2/2)

Load

Material carried by a river, from material washed or fallen in and eroded from the channel.

Larger particles are carried by traction or saltation, small ones in suspension and dissolved material in solution, so load size decides the method.

Fluvial processes

Deposition

Deposition is laying down of material when the river loses energy, speed and discharge.

It is most likely where a river enters a lake or the sea, and wherever the gradient falls.

A wetter climate means more discharge and more erosion and transport. Softer rocks erode more easily. Gentle slopes encourage deposition.

Long profile

Downstream changes in river landscapes

River environments

Downstream changes in river landscapes

Upland landforms (1/2)

Waterfall

A river dropping over a band of hard rock lying on softer rock. The soft rock is eroded faster, a plunge pool forms and the fall retreats upstream, leaving a gorge. Iguaçu Falls in South America is an example.

Upland landforms are steep V-shaped valleys, interlocking spurs, waterfalls and gorges, made mainly by erosion with help from weathering and mass movement.

Downstream changes in river landscapes

Upland landforms (2/2)

In the uplands the river is fast and spends most energy cutting downwards by hydraulic action and abrasion, making the valley deeper.

Mass movement brings material down the valley sides, which adds to the load and to abrasion. The valley floor is narrow and often filled by the river.

Interlocking spurs form where the river swings from side to side, cutting down between spurs of higher land on alternate sides.

Downstream changes in river landscapes

Lowland landforms (1/3)

Meander

A bend in a river channel.

Ox-bow lake

A cut-off meander loop left when the river breaks through the narrow neck.

In the lowlands the channel and valley become wider, deeper and smoother. Velocity and discharge still rise despite the gentler gradient. In plan the course becomes more winding.

Downstream changes in river landscapes

Lowland landforms (2/3)

The valley floor is a flood plain. Near the end it spreads into a delta or estuary.

Vertical erosion matters less near sea level. Lateral erosion is more important, especially on the outside of meanders. The river also deposits sediment.

When the river floods, velocity drops and sediment is deposited across the valley floor. The greatest deposition is on the banks, which build up into raised levees.

Downstream changes in river landscapes

Lowland landforms (3/3)

Meanders

the current undercuts the outside of the bend to make a steep river cliff (bluff). A spiral current carries material to the slow-flowing inside of the bend, where it is deposited as a gentle slip-off slope (point bar).

As erosion continues the neck narrows. In flood the neck is breached, the river flows straight and the old loop becomes an ox-bow lake. Deposition seals off its ends. The Mississippi shows many meanders and ox-bows.

Downstream changes in river landscapes

Deltas (1/2)

A delta is a large area of alluvium at the river mouth. The Ganges-Brahmaputra delta at the head of the Bay of Bengal is one of the largest and receives about 1.7 billion tonnes of sediment a year, mainly from the Himalayas.

River flow is slowed when it meets denser sea water and the load is dropped faster than tides can take it away.

Downstream changes in river landscapes

Deltas (2/2)

The river splits into smaller channels called distributaries, which spread sediment over a wide area and build new land.

Not all rivers form deltas. Many have open mouths called estuaries.

Case study: the River Tay (UK) and its valley

River environments

Case study: the River Tay (UK) and its valley

Case study: the River Tay (UK) and its valley (1/2)

Upland

fed by streams draining the Grampian Mountains in the Scottish Highlands. Rainfall is well over 1000 mm a year and slopes are steep, so run off is large.

The river is already about 100 m wide, curves between interlocking spurs and fills a steep V-shaped valley. Erosion is vertical.

Case study: the River Tay (UK) and its valley

Case study: the River Tay (UK) and its valley (2/2)

Lowland

the valley cross-section is a wide flat flood plain, about 600 m wide where the River Earn joins the Tay. A big meander loop on the Earn has levees marked on its banks.

Estuary

a wide channel of up to 2 km with sand and mud banks such as Abernethy Bank, areas of marsh and deeper water channels such as North Deep.

Water uses, demand and supply

Water uses, demand and supply (3/3)

Water balance

The comparison between water demand and supply for a country or area.

Only 3 per cent of Earth's water is fresh. Over 75 per cent of that is locked in glaciers and ice sheets and about 20 per cent is groundwater.

Fresh water is essential to life, vital for economic development and unevenly distributed, with water-rich and water-poor areas.

Water uses, demand and supply

Demand (1/3)

Developed

Water use per person per year: About 1200 cubic metres; Main uses: Industry first, then agriculture closely; domestic is a small share but over three times the developing share

Developing

Water use per person per year: About 400 cubic metres; Main uses: Mostly agriculture, with little industry or domestic use

Water uses, demand and supply

Demand (2/3)

Global demand has more than trebled in the last 50 years. Population rose from 5.3 billion in 1990 to about 7.4 billion in 2015, but development matters too.

Rising living standards raise domestic use through piped water, flush toilets, showers, washing machines and pools.

Rising agricultural output to feed more people raises irrigation use.

Water uses, demand and supply

Demand (3/3)

Industrialisation

factories use water for cooling and for generating power.

In many places women carry water from the well, which many see as a sign of gender inequality.

Water uses, demand and supply

Supply and surplus or deficit (1/2)

Main sources

rivers and lakes, reservoirs (artificial lakes behind dams) and aquifers and wells (water in porous rock raised by buckets, pumps or pressure).

Water-deficit areas have demand above supply. Water-surplus areas have supply above demand. Water-neutral areas are roughly balanced.

Surplus areas are typically remote, mountainous and wet with few people.

Water uses, demand and supply

Supply and surplus or deficit (2/2)

Deficit areas include Africa, the Middle East, Australia and parts of the Americas, either because rainfall is low or because population and development are high (India is an example).

Water is moved from surplus to deficit areas by hand, vehicles and tankers, but mostly by long pipelines and canals.

Water quality and supply

Water quality and supply (1/3)

Agriculture

Silage liquid and slurry in rivers; fertilisers and pesticides seeping into groundwater; deforestation run off carrying soil and silt

Industry

Warm cooling water returned to rivers; spills from plants such as oil refineries; toxic wastes from mining and ore processing

Water quality and supply

Water quality and supply (2/3)

Domestic

Untreated or even treated sewage; washing and bathing in rivers; chlorinated water from swimming pools

Safe water

Water fit for human consumption, free from pollutants and disease.

Quantity matters (a kilogram of beef takes about 15 500 litres of water) but quality matters too. Polluted water spreads cholera, bilharzia, typhoid and diarrhoea.

Water quality and supply

Water quality and supply (3/3)

Quality is poorer in dry climates or in dry seasons where water stagnates and breeds disease. Pollution is highest in urban areas, especially in developing cities.

More than 1 billion people lack safe water. Countries with under 50 per cent access are mainly in Africa and parts of southern Asia. Most developed countries have over 90 per cent.

Water quality and supply

Managing clean water supply: collection, treatment, delivery (1/3)

Collection from rivers, reservoirs and lakes, aquifers and wells.

Treatment is nearly always needed. Rivers are often polluted, reservoirs can be polluted by acid rain and seepage, and groundwater can be contaminated, for example by arsenic in parts of Bangladesh.

Water quality and supply

Managing clean water supply: collection, treatment, delivery (2/3)

Treatment processes

chlorination (controls algae), aeration (removes dissolved iron and manganese), sedimentation (removes suspended solids), filtration (removes fine sediments) and disinfection (kills bacteria).

Delivery

pipes in developed countries (costly to install and maintain, and leaks cause losses); standpipes and wells in many developing places, where buckets can be dirty and collection takes time;

Water quality and supply

Managing clean water supply: collection, treatment, delivery (3/3)

bottled water, which is very expensive and sold mostly in developed countries.

Dams and reservoirs have many purposes

electricity, irrigation, flood control and improved navigation. This multi-purpose river management is shown by projects in Spain and China.

Water quality and supply

Case study: river management lessons from Spain (1/2)

Spain has a Mediterranean climate with low rainfall mostly in winter and summer droughts. Water supply and demand do not coincide, so water was moved from the sparsely populated centre to the south-east, mainly for irrigation.

Tagus-Segura project

286 km of aqueducts and canals, finished in 1978.

Problems

most irrigation water went to agribusiness rather than local farmers, much was used by urban development and tourist resorts, and cheap water encouraged waste.

Water quality and supply

Case study: river management lessons from Spain (2/2)

Ebro project

proposed in 2001, with over a hundred dams and hundreds of km of canals to transfer 100 billion litres a year.

Abandoned in 2004 because of the Tagus-Segura experience and the threat to the Ebro delta, an agricultural area and wildlife wetland. Spain turned to coastal desalination plants.

Case study: river management lessons from China (1/4)

Exam tip Common lesson from both countries: transfer schemes seem effective but have financial, social and environmental costs. Spain moved to desalination while China seeks more efficient and sustainable use.
Three Gorges Dam (Yangtze)

started 1997, finished 2009, still the world's largest. It is 185 m high and almost 2 km wide, with a reservoir nearly 600 km long.

It makes about 10 per cent of China's electricity, helps flood control and improves transport.

Case study: river management lessons from China (2/4)

Costs

one million people resettled, towns and fertile land drowned, silt trapped (farmland downstream becomes less fertile), water quality falling as waste collects, and earthquake risk.

South-North Water Transport Project (SNWTP): moves water from the wet south to the dry North China Plain (over 200 million people, heavy industry and irrigation around Beijing and Tianjin).

Water quality and supply

Case study: river management lessons from China (3/4)

Planned in 1952 and begun 50 years later to move 12 trillion gallons a year over 1000 km on three routes. Eastern and central routes opened in 2013 and 2014. The western route is halted.

SNWTP benefits

supplies industry and irrigation, improves health and showcases engineering. Costs: the South may face shortages, people displaced, ecosystems damaged, evaporation loss from open canals and very high expense.

Case study: river management lessons from China (4/4)

China now focuses on cutting demand

reduce evaporation and irrigation waste, recycle water, control pollution, recharge groundwater and raise prices.

Water quality and supply

Case study: plans to manage the Blue Nile, Ethiopia

Ethiopia is short of water and energy. The Blue Nile starts at Lake Tana in the highlands and falls 1300 m over nearly 850 km to the Sudan border.

The Grand Renaissance Dam, started in 2011 and due to finish in 2017, will generate 6000 megawatts and supply irrigation water. It is funded by the Ethiopian government.

Sudan and Egypt downstream feared it would take too much water, though it is now accepted it will benefit them too.

Flooding: causes and control (1/4)

Weather

Prolonged heavy rain saturates ground and exceeds infiltration; sudden warming melts snow fast

Rock

Impermeable rock limits percolation and speeds run off

Soil

Clay has low infiltration so run off is fast

Flooding: causes and control

Flooding: causes and control (2/4)

Relief

Steep slopes cause rapid run off

Drainage density

Many tributaries carry water quickly to the main river

Vegetation

Sparse plant cover absorbs little and does not slow run off

Deforestation

Less interception, faster run off

Flooding: causes and control

Flooding: causes and control (3/4)

Urbanisation

Concrete, tarmac and drains deliver rain quickly

Agriculture

Bare soil, overgrazing, monoculture and ploughing down slopes raise risk

Burning fossil fuels

Raises global temperatures, melting ice and increasing rainfall and storms

Flooding: causes and control

Flooding: causes and control (4/4)

A flood happens when discharge exceeds the capacity of the channel and water spills across the flood plain. Causes are usually a mix of natural and human factors, and people often make natural flooding worse.

The key is how quickly rain reaches the river. A shorter lag time means a higher flood chance.

Long persistent rain raises the water table and saturates soil, so water collects on low land. In December 2015 storms flooded northern England at a cost of about 2 billion pounds, and many were under-insured.

Flooding: causes and control

Consequences (1/2)

Immediate effects

loss of life, destroyed property and crops, homelessness, disrupted transport and communications, and loss of water and sewage services.

Environmental effects include landslides, soil erosion, sewage contamination of soil, lost habitats and destroyed vegetation.

Flooding: causes and control

Consequences (2/2)

Long term

replacing losses and removing silt. Developed countries often have insurance.

Poor people in developing countries may lose everything, and famine and diseases such as typhoid and cholera can follow. Recovery often depends on international aid.

Flooding: causes and control

Control (1/3)

Construction (hard engineering)

Dams, flood embankments (levees), sluice gates, relief channels and channel straightening; effective but expensive

Adjustment (soft engineering)

Working with nature: restoring rivers, keeping marshes and wetlands as flood stores, afforestation, planning controls on the flood plain, flood insurance, warnings and public advice

Flooding: causes and control

Control (2/3)

Prediction

Forecasting flood extent and depth to decide embankment height and where not to build

Risk assessment

Deciding which size of flood to protect against. Small floods may come every five years, large ones every 50, and rare giant floods every 1000.

Managers often aim for the 50 to 100 year flood. Under-assessing the risk leaves protection inadequate.

Flooding: causes and control

Control (3/3)

Careful Be clear on hard versus soft engineering and why soft engineering is more environmentally friendly.
