Defining development and human welfare

Development and human welfare

Defining development and human welfare

Defining development and human welfare (1/6)

Development

A process of change that affects countries and their peoples, making progress in several 'strands'. The most important is economic development, which provides the power that drives the rest.

Human welfare

The general condition of a population.

Defining development and human welfare

Defining development and human welfare (2/6)

It is usually described as quality of life, which includes four related parts: economic (secure job, income, affluence, standard of living), physical (diet, housing, clean environment, health, mobility), social (education, welfare services, family and friends, leisure), and psychological (satisfaction, happiness, security).

Defining development and human welfare

Defining development and human welfare (3/6)

Economic development comes from using resources (minerals, energy, climate, soils) plus capital, technology, enterprising people and good government.

Defining development and human welfare

Defining development and human welfare (4/6)

Other strand groups

demographic (life expectancy, birth control, mobility), social (welfare services, equal opportunities, quality of life), cultural (education, heritage, ethnic respect), political (vote, democracy, free speech) and environmental (pollution control, conservation, small ecological footprint).

Defining development and human welfare

Defining development and human welfare (5/6)

Factors generating economic development

resources, internal boosters (government intervention, national ambition, business culture) and external boosters (globalisation, TNCs and international agencies, geopolitics).

Outcomes include higher productivity, rising living standards, better quality of life, more democracy, but also spatial inequality and environmental impacts.

Defining development and human welfare

Defining development and human welfare (6/6)

Cycle of wealth

economic growth makes wealth, which becomes taxes (spent on roads, schools, health if governance is good), wages and disposable income (spent on goods and services), and reinvestment by businesses, repeating the cycle.

Benefits are not shared equally, so the rich-poor gap often widens.

Contributors to development and human welfare

Development and human welfare

Contributors to development and human welfare

Contributors to development and human welfare (1/2)

Natural resources

a country needs water, food and energy security, ideally from within its borders. Imports create vulnerability because supplies can be cut by war or politics, or prices raised as pressure.

Human resources

technology (finding new ways to use resources or alternatives, so reducing imports) and the labour force (skills, enterprise, commitment), improved through education.

Contributors to development and human welfare

Contributors to development and human welfare (2/2)

Government

development flourishes with a democratic, stable government that encourages enterprise and investment. Capitalist (USA) or socialist (China) regimes both can top the rankings but differ in per capita GDP.

Equality (of opportunities, gender, ethnicity, class) avoids tension and conflict.

Economic growth, technology and enterprise are central 'cable strands'.

Measuring development and quality of life

Development and human welfare

Measuring development and quality of life

Measuring development and quality of life (1/2)

GDP

Total value of a country's economic output in a year

GNI

GDP plus net income from abroad (formerly GNP)

Per capita GDP or GNI

Divided by population so that countries of different sizes can be compared. Table: USA $55 837, UK $43 734, China $7925, Haiti $820 (2015)

Measuring development and quality of life

Measuring development and quality of life (2/2)

Sector shift indicates development

primary first, then secondary, then tertiary, with the quaternary appearing in developed countries (Ethiopia primary, China secondary driving growth, UK tertiary).

Measured by employment or by contribution to the economy.

Other indicators

energy consumption (rises with development) and population rates (birth rate, death rate and infant mortality fall, life expectancy rises).

Measuring development and quality of life

Quality of life indicators (1/3)

Human Development Index (HDI)

The most used composite measure of quality of life and development, averaging three equally weighted strands: income per capita, literacy (education) and life expectancy.

It ranges from 0 to 1. High in North America, Europe, southern South America and Australasia; low in Africa.

Measuring development and quality of life

Quality of life indicators (2/3)

Housing

floor space, dwellings with water, electricity, indoor toilet, owner-occupation. Education: spending, years in school, literacy, teachers per 1000.

Health

infant mortality, life expectancy, doctors, hospital beds, calories.

Examples

one in eight people lacks safe water (Afghanistan 13 per cent access, Ethiopia 22, Somalia 42). Illiteracy highest in Niger 85, Guinea 75, South Sudan 73, Chad 72.

Measuring development and quality of life

Quality of life indicators (3/3)

Daily calories

Ireland 3847 and Greece 3815 top, Somalia 1400 and Afghanistan 1529 lowest; below 2000 usually means malnutrition.

Gini index measures income inequality

low means equal, high means unequal.

Corruption perception index (Transparency International, 2014): from clean (high value) to corrupt (low value). Asia, Africa and much of Latin America score corrupt.

Uneven development

Development and human welfare

Uneven development

Uneven development

By GDP per head at purchasing power parity (PPP): developing under $10 000, emerging $10 000 to $30 000, developed over $30 000. On this measure India is developing, though widely seen as emerging.

Range is huge, from under $1000 (eight African countries) to Qatar at $132 000.

Uneven development

Development pathway (1/2)

Countries move along a pathway from least to most developed at different speeds. The UK started early, with empire resources and the Industrial Revolution (coal, iron ore, enterprise, labour), as did western Europe and the USA.

Emerging countries advance through the global shift in manufacturing, drawn by much lower labour costs.

Uneven development

Development pathway (2/2)

Some countries are barely started because of obstacles. Political: misrule (North Korea, Zimbabwe), civil war (Angola, Somalia, Sudan) or the Soviet break-up.

Economic

lack of resources, energy, trained labour, capital, technology and enterprise (Ethiopia, Bangladesh).

Uneven development

Dependency theory (1/2)

Dependency theory

Poor countries (the periphery) stay poor because rich countries (the core) control the global economy.

Evidence

resource exploitation since colonial times, now by TNCs.

Reasons

interference in politics, unfair trade (cheap resources sold, expensive goods bought), selling non-essentials, bilateral aid with strings, and debt with interest.

Uneven development

Dependency theory (2/2)

But not all poor countries were colonies, and some blame socialism.

Uneven development

Within countries (1/2)

National averages hide variation. Wealth is concentrated in a favoured region, usually with the capital or leading city (the core), leaving a periphery.

Core-periphery theory says that cores first grow at the expense of peripheries, then wealth trickles or spreads out and the gap narrows.

Uneven development

Within countries (2/2)

Examples

US states (high HDI in the north-east, low in the south), Italy and India. Governments sometimes use this as an excuse to do little.

Impacts of uneven development: Italy

Development and human welfare

Impacts of uneven development: Italy

Impacts of uneven development: Italy (1/3)

Population 60 million, per capita GDP about $30 000. The North (core, Po basin) is wealthier than the South (periphery, the Mezzogiorno).

North advantages

manufacturing, services and quaternary jobs, fertile lowland with irrigation, cities in the Turin industrial triangle (Milan, Turin, Genoa) with good transport, nearness to European markets, better housing and services, gas from the Po basin and HEP from the Alps.

Impacts of uneven development: Italy

Impacts of uneven development: Italy (2/3)

South disadvantages

mountains hindering communication, a hot dry summer and cold wet winter, thin limestone soils, poor grazing, weak transport, little employment outside farming and emigration.

The South also receives migrants from North Africa.

Since 1950 the government and the EU invested in motorways, irrigation (tomatoes, citrus, vegetables) and relocated heavy industry (steel, vehicles).

Impacts of uneven development: Italy

Impacts of uneven development: Italy (3/3)

But since 2007 the gap has widened in a recession because poorer areas suffer more.

Demographic characteristics (5/5)

Population pyramid

A diagram of horizontal bars showing numbers or percentages of males and females in age groups, usually 5-year bands, youngest at the bottom.

As countries develop, fertility, infant and maternal mortality fall, while life expectancy and the share of older people rise. Death rates can rise in older developed populations.

Strategies to reduce uneven development

Development and human welfare

Strategies to reduce uneven development

Aid (1/2)

Aid is the transfer of money, goods and expertise. Multilateral aid is given through organisations (World Bank, UNESCO) that allocate it.

Bilateral aid goes government to government, often with conditions (the USA's oil-search aid to Peru for jets and fishing access).

Strategies to reduce uneven development

Aid (2/2)

Loans are the least satisfactory, and technical help is better if it matches skills: intermediate technology (such as a basic water pump) is easy to use and needs no foreign experts.

Voluntary aid from NGOs (Oxfam, Red Cross, Save the Children) is often emergency aid.

Providers follow the North-South divide, with Russia and Eastern Europe neither, and China and India providing aid with strings attached for resources.

Strategies to reduce uneven development

Trade (1/2)

Trade helps development by funding imports, but trade barriers (tariffs, quotas) and terms favour developed and emerging countries.

The Fairtrade Foundation (1992) obtains fair prices for coffee, cocoa, sugar, tea, bananas, honey and similar goods from small producers.

Inter-governmental agreements involve the World Bank, IMF and WTO.

Strategies to reduce uneven development

Trade (2/2)

Debt relief means no longer demanding full repayment, but critics say money may go to governments without proof of good use, may not reach the needy, and may encourage new debts.

Closing the gap: contrasting approaches

Closing the gap: contrasting approaches (2/3)

Core-periphery model

growth begins at a core and drains the rest, until the core is congested and spreads, creating new sub-cores. Some governments use it as an excuse to do nothing.

Top-down is government- or IGO-led, concerned with national or regional economy and large infrastructure or industry projects, hoping benefits reach local areas, with little local involvement.

Closing the gap: contrasting approaches

Closing the gap: contrasting approaches (3/3)

Bottom-up is grass-roots, centred on people helping themselves, with NGOs providing finance and technical help.

Closing the gap: contrasting approaches

Top-down: Nigeria and UK (1/3)

Nigeria coastal railway

signed in 2014 with China Railway Construction Corporation, 1400 km from Lagos to Calabar across the Niger delta to create a coastal growth corridor;

about US$12 billion, 85 per cent Chinese loans and 15 per cent Nigerian; 200 000 construction jobs and 30 000 permanent. Oil price fall in 2016 put it on hold, though it could cut oil dependence.

Closing the gap: contrasting approaches

Top-down: Nigeria and UK (2/3)

UK HS2

high-speed line London to Birmingham (by 2026) then Manchester, Leeds and Sheffield (by 2033), part of the 'northern powerhouse' and the attempt to reduce the North-South divide.

Funded by government and private money. Opponents cite small time savings and environmental costs; the cost was estimated at US$128 billion.

Closing the gap: contrasting approaches

Top-down: Nigeria and UK (3/3)

Problems of top-down

unsustainable debt, loan conditions giving outsiders control, little local benefit beyond construction jobs, imported machinery and expertise, and costly operation and maintenance.

Closing the gap: contrasting approaches

Bottom-up: Kenya and South Africa (1/3)

Exam tip Both approaches have costs and benefits: top-down may suit the global gap in economic development, while bottom-up suits the welfare gap and gaps within countries. They are best used in partnership.

Bottom-up schemes are planned and controlled locally, cheaper, use appropriate technology, less damaging, run by NGOs and sustainable.

Closing the gap: contrasting approaches

Bottom-up: Kenya and South Africa (2/3)

WaterAid in Kenya

17 million lack safe water and 32 million lack sanitation; water goes first to planned urban areas and wealthy rural communities; up to a third of pumps are broken.

WaterAid (UK trust, 1981) partners with communities for hand-dug wells, hand pumps, rainwater harvesting, and community latrines.

Closing the gap: contrasting approaches

Bottom-up: Kenya and South Africa (3/3)

South Africa's Manyeding agricultural co-operative ('End Hunger' scheme): communal fallow land grows maize, beans, wheat, sunflower, groundnuts and potatoes;

aims to restore 1 million hectares in five years; funded by a mining company (about R10 million) and municipalities; 160 partners and 24 staff; organic farming and marketing training;

a storage facility. It tackles hunger, food insecurity and poverty.
