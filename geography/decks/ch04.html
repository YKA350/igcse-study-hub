Spec 4.2b, 4.1a Informal employment
Economic sectors and employment

Changes over time: the Clark-Fisher model

  1. Pre-industrial: the primary sector leads and may employ over two-thirds of workers, mostly agriculture.
  2. Industrial: secondary and tertiary grow, primary declines, secondary peaks but rarely exceeds half of workers. Cottage industries are replaced by factories (industrialisation).
  3. Post-industrial: tertiary is largest, primary and secondary keep falling and the quaternary appears. The UK passed through these phases from about 1750 to 1970.
  4. Agriculture mechanises and becomes commercial, freeing labour to move to towns and factories. Wages raise disposable income, which is spent on services.
  5. The quaternary emerges when leading countries invest in higher education, R and D and new technology.
  6. Levels of development: low income, lower middle, upper middle and high income. Emerging countries (Brazil, China, India, Russia) lie between developing (low income) and developed (high income).

Factors affecting the location of economic activities

Factors affecting the location of economic activities (1/3)

  1. Location factors are space (land), raw materials, labour and access to markets (transport, customers). Their mix varies by activity.
  2. Commercial farming: suitable land, soil, climate and water, then nearness to markets.
  3. Heavy industry (steel, shipbuilding, chemicals): raw materials, energy supply and transport links.
  4. Light industry: less tied to raw materials, uses energy, may need skilled labour.
  5. Retailing: accessibility to customers and labour.
  6. R and D: highly skilled workers and access to university research.

Changes in sector employment

Economic activity and energy

Changes in sector employment

Changes in sector employment (1/2)

  1. Raw materials: sources run out. Manufacturing is no longer tied to coalfields because electricity grids, pipelines and tankers supply energy anywhere.
  2. New technology: mechanisation cut labour needs in farming and manufacturing, and workers moved to later sectors. Transport and communications cut the friction of distance. High-tech industries (aerospace, biotechnology, robotics, telecoms) and new products and services (smartphones, broadband, software) have appeared.
  3. Globalisation: countries are drawn into one global economy through improved transport and communications, growth of trade, investment and aid, transnational corporations, labour migration and expanding markets. Kenya grows flowers for Europe, South Korea ships cars, India hosts call centres.
Spec 4.1a, 4.3a Classification of employment by economic sector
Changes in sector employment

Changes in sector employment (2/2)

  1. Government policies: UK policy supported farming and services after manufacturing job losses. China concentrated on expanding manufacturing for the global market.
  2. Demographic and social change: population growth raises demand and labour supply. Higher incomes create disposable income for non-essentials, so a cycle of tertiary growth develops. Shopping shifts to superstores and retail parks.

Sector shifts in three countries

Ethiopia (pre-industrial, developing)

  1. Ranked 173 of 186 in development. Primary sector provides 75 per cent of jobs but under 50 per cent of GDP, because most farming is subsistence. Coffee is the main commercial crop, with oil seeds and flowers rising, which takes land from subsistence farmers and worsens food shortages. Gold mining is growing.
  2. Manufacturing is small but growing: agricultural processing, textiles and leather. The government invests in roads and energy to attract TNCs seeking affordable labour. Over 75 per cent live in rural areas and rural-urban migration to Addis Ababa is rising. Tertiary is small but is nearly as large as the primary sector in GDP; the African Union and the UN Economic Commission for Africa are based in Addis Ababa.

Sector shifts in three countries

China (rapidly emerging)

  1. The world's largest economy but only lower middle income per person. The shift from primary to secondary came when the government joined the global economy. The secondary sector is half of GDP but a quarter of jobs, based on cheap labour and energy. Agriculture still employs many but its GDP share is shrinking.
  2. Benefits: higher wages, living standards and impressive megacities such as Shanghai and Beijing. Costs: serious pollution from industry and traffic, a growing rural-urban gap and controlled internet access. If wages rise, some manufacturing may move to cheaper countries such as Ethiopia.

Sector shifts in three countries

The UK (post-industrial, developed)

  1. First industrial nation. Fifty years ago manufacturing gave 40 per cent of wealth and one-third of jobs. Now it gives under 25 per cent of wealth and under 20 per cent of jobs, because of the global shift of manufacturing to China, India and other countries (de-industrialisation).
  2. Tertiary provides 80 per cent of jobs and 75 per cent of wealth (the quaternary maybe 10 to 15 per cent). Farming makes about 60 per cent of UK food with few workers because of mechanisation.
  3. Costs: redundancy, unemployment, families uprooted, derelict industrial land and the struggle of northern towns to re-image themselves.

Informal employment

Informal employment (1/2)

Informal sector

Unofficial, unregulated work not in government figures (the black economy), employing millions especially in developing countries.

Spec 4.2b, 4.1a Informal employment
Informal employment

Informal employment (2/2)

  1. Causes: rural-urban migration for work, more job seekers than jobs, underemployment and unemployment, and wages too low to live on.
  2. Examples: street selling, shoe-shining, rubbish collecting and scavenging, begging and petty crime. Mostly tertiary and linked to shanty towns.
  3. Paratransit is informal transport (minibuses, rickshaws, scooters, pedicabs) that meets cheap transport demand but adds congestion.
  4. In some countries over 70 per cent work informally.
Spec 4.2b, 4.1a Informal employment
Informal employment

Case study: Dhaka, Bangladesh

  1. Megacity of 16 million; about 25 per cent live in slums; the most densely populated megacity; maybe over half depend on informal work. Benefits: cheap goods and services and a means of survival. Costs: no healthcare or unemployment benefits, work risks, uncertain legal status, discrimination, and no escape from the cycle of poverty. About half a million children work, for about 50 US cents a day, in dangerous conditions and without schooling. Dhaka is the rickshaw capital of the world.

Population and resources

Population and resources

Population and resources (1/2)

  1. Resource use is driven by population growth and development. The population is over 7 billion and the economy grows about 3 per cent a year, while many resources are finite.
TheoryArgument
Malthus (1798)Population grows faster than food, so growth stops through lower births or higher death from famine, disease and war
Boserup (1965)Population growth stimulates innovation and technology that raise food production
Club of Rome (1972)Limits to growth would be reached within 100 years at 1970s rates, but regulation could give a sustainable balance of population, development and resources

Rising energy demand

Economic activity and energy

Rising energy demand

Rising energy demand

  1. Primary energy is used without conversion (coal, natural gas, fuelwood). Secondary energy is made from primary fuels (electricity, petrol, coke). Electricity is the leading energy source.
  2. Non-renewable sources (coal, oil, gas) cannot be replaced. Renewable sources (solar, wind, tidal) can be reused and are sustainable.
  3. Demand rises with population and development. Energy use is an indicator of development. Europe and North America use 70 per cent of world energy with 20 per cent of the people. Warm regions need less heating, but HICs use much energy for cooling.
Spec 4.3b Non-renewable, e.g. coal, oil, natural gas, uranium and shale gas/oil, and renewable…
Rising energy demand

Production and security

  1. Three-quarters of energy comes from oil, gas and coal. Major producers: USA, Canada, Western Europe, Russia, parts of the Middle East, Australia, New Zealand. The big consumers are also big producers, giving two energy worlds.
  2. Energy security means meeting all needs reliably from within (affordable, accessible, reliable supply). Most countries have an energy gap and import. The UK's gap is widening as it phases out fossil fuels and imports cheaper coal, oil and gas.
  3. Countries with surpluses gain geopolitical and economic power. The immediate problem is the mismatch between where energy is produced and consumed.

Sustainable energy

Economic activity and energy

Sustainable energy

Sustainable energy

  1. Energy must be used sparingly and efficiently, and non-renewables conserved.
  2. Personal actions: walk or cycle, insulate homes (about 50 per cent of heat is lost through walls and loft), use curtains and double-glazing, fill freezers, run full washing loads, hibernate or unplug gadgets.
Spec 4.3c Energy can be managed in a sustainable way through education, efficiency and conservation
Sustainable energy

Case study: Qatar

  1. Has energy security. Oil and gas made it rich; the largest exporter of LNG (the UK is a major importer); energy exports are half of GDP and it has the world's highest income per person. Desert climate with over 40 degrees C summers means heavy electricity demand for air conditioning, heavy industry, desalination and transport. Electricity use grew from 8.0 to 32.7 billion kWh from 2000 to 2012.
  2. Plans a 1800 MW solar plant to give about 15 per cent of power by 2020. It does not want nuclear.

Sustainable energy

Case study: India

  1. 18 per cent of the world's people but 6 per cent of primary energy; consumption doubled since 2000. Aims: expand the economy, supply rural electricity, improve transport and infrastructure. Three-quarters of energy is from fossil fuels (coal over half). Coal imports are rising. Hydro gives 20 per cent of electricity, nuclear 5 per cent, with wind starting and big untapped solar potential.

Sustainable energy

Case study: Nepal

  1. A low-income land-locked mountainous country with no fossil fuels, so imports are costly. Fuelwood caused deforestation; biomass is used for cooking. Aim: electricity for 28 million people. Over 1000 micro-hydro plants in 52 districts, built and run by communities with World Bank support, divert river water to a fore-bay, then a penstock and turbine, with no dam. Wind trials in the Kathmandu valley suffer gusts, and solar suffers few sunshine hours and cost.
Exam tipConclusion: all three need energy, but only Nepal gets almost all of it from renewable hydro. Qatar is starting on solar, while India stays dependent on fossil fuels.
