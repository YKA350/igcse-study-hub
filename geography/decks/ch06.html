Urbanisation and its processes (1/2)

Urbanisation

An increasing share of a country's population living in towns and cities, so urban settlements grow.

Spec 6.3a, 6.2c Development of the rural-urban fringe
Urbanisation and its processes

Urbanisation and its processes (2/2)

  1. Urban settlements differ from rural ones in economy (manufacturing and services, not farming), size, density of people and buildings, and way of life.
  2. Emerging and developed countries are the most urbanised; Africa and South-East Asia the least.
  3. The world population more than doubled from 1950 to 2015 but the urban population more than trebled, and about half now live in cities.
  4. Rates are much higher in developing countries because new economic development is in big cities, push-pull factors drive rural-urban migration, and natural increase is high. In developed countries the level is higher but growth slow; the urban way of life spreads into the countryside (rural dilution).
Spec 6.3a, 6.2c Development of the rural-urban fringe
Urbanisation and its processes

The urbanisation pathway

  1. A country moves from developing through emerging to developed along an S-shaped curve. Stage 1 early urbanisation (very low level), Stage 2 accelerating urbanisation as development speeds up, Stage 3 mature urbanisation as it slows, flattening at about two-thirds urban, then Stage 4 counter-urbanisation as the curve may flatten.
  2. Speed depends on the pace of economic development, and not every country goes far along.

Rise of megacities

Urban environments

Rise of megacities

Rise of megacities (1/2)

  1. Rate of urbanisation is driven by the pace of economic development (secondary and tertiary growth) and population growth, mainly rural-urban migration. There is a multiplier effect: growth attracts people, who need services, which creates more growth.
  2. Millionaire cities: 2 in 1900 (London, Paris), about 500 now. Cities over 5 million: 8 in 1950, over 50 now.
  3. Megacity: over 10 million people; 4 in 1970, 35 now, over half in Asia. Fifty years ago half of the ten largest cities were in the developed world, but by 2000 only two.
  4. Reasons: economic development, population growth (the buzz for young people), economies of scale (cheaper transport and communication) and the multiplier effect.
  5. Downside: all urban problems are acute and megacities become powerful cores that create large peripheries, growing at the expense of other regions.
Spec 6.3a, 6.1c Development of the rural-urban fringe
Rise of megacities

Rise of megacities (2/2)

Global (world) city

A city of any size recognised as a hub of prestige, power and influence in the global economy. There are 31, all over 1 million, seven being megacities. London, New York and Tokyo are the top three, each heading a network. Rio, Sao Paulo, Buenos Aires and Sydney lie outside the three networks.

Problems of rapid urbanisation

Urban environments

Problems of rapid urbanisation

Problems of rapid urbanisation (1/2)

  1. São Paulo nearly doubled between 1970 and 2015 to over 12 million, the largest city in South America.
  2. Housing: no homes for migrants, so shanty towns and squatter settlements form; housing costs are high and the best is near the centre.
  3. Water and electricity: services lag, with people using fires and polluted streams.
  4. Traffic and transport: overloaded transport, congestion, air pollution and smog.
  5. Health: too few doctors and clinics; dirty water spreads typhoid and cholera; breathing problems.
Spec 6.1c Problems associated with rapid urbanisation
Problems of rapid urbanisation

Problems of rapid urbanisation (2/2)

  1. Education: too few schools; children work.
  2. Employment: many unemployed or in the informal sector, and factories are far from shanty areas.
  3. Social problems: crime (murder, rape, robbery), gangs and drugs.
  4. Environment: air, water, noise, visual pollution, waste, and damage to surrounding countryside.

Urban land-use pattern

Urban environments

Urban land-use pattern

Urban land-use pattern (1/2)

  1. Land uses separate because the land market sells sites to the highest bidder, usually retailers. Land values fall outwards from the peak land-value intersection but stay high along major roads and ring roads, with small peaks where radial and ring roads cross.
  2. Similar uses cluster since they share locational needs and can afford the same land values. Shops and offices cluster in the accessible centre, making the CBD. Manufacturing needs access but is less capital intensive, so is found along main roads outside the CBD. Housing is less competitive and is pushed outward, becoming more spacious as land cheapens.
Spec 6.2a, 6.3c Factors affecting urban land use patterns
Urban land-use pattern

Urban land-use pattern (2/2)

ZoneFeatures
CoreOldest part, with the CBD and earliest buildings
Inner-city ringEarly suburbs with old housing and some non-residential uses
Suburban ringPresent suburbs, mostly housing
Urban fringeCountryside eroded by outward spread
  1. Moving outwards, age of buildings falls, architecture changes and density falls. This is the concentric zone model. The character of each zone varies around the world.
  2. Residential sorting: people group by social class, occupation and ethnicity, mainly according to wealth. The poor rent small spaces at high density.

Spec 6.2b, 6.3a Urban challenges in a named developed country
Urban challenges in the developed world

Case study: Hong Kong (1/2)

  1. About 1000 sq km and over 7 million people, part of China since 1997 but autonomous: a global city, a developed place within an emerging country.
  2. Economic: gains from globalisation, trade and finance, little manufacturing so little de-industrialisation; the world's eleventh largest trader and largest re-export centre.
  3. Food: farming is 0.1 per cent of GDP, so most food is imported, mostly from outside China.
  4. Energy: about 75 per cent from fossil fuels (more coal than gas) and nearly a quarter nuclear imported from China.
Spec 6.2c, 6.2b Urban challenges in a named developing country or emerging country
Urban challenges in the developed world

Case study: Hong Kong (2/2)

  1. Transport: over 90 per cent of 11 million daily journeys by public transport (rail, bus, ferry).
  2. Social and housing: good education and health; a vertical city with about half in public high-rise housing; private housing is the third most expensive; social polarisation. About 94 per cent are Chinese and the rest are mostly Filipino, Indonesian, Nepali and Indian domestic workers, plus expatriate professionals.
  3. Poverty: slums cleared in the 1990s, but illegal rooftop (penthouse) slums remain.
  4. Environment: deep ecological footprint from fossil fuel burning, land reclaimed from the sea harming marine life, smog from the Pearl River delta, and waste of 6.4 million tonnes a year (recycling and waste-to-energy are tried). 70 per cent of water is imported from the Dongjiang River.

Urban challenges in emerging and developing worlds

Urban environments

Urban challenges in emerging and developing worlds

Urban challenges in emerging and developing worlds (1/2)

  1. Rapid migration leads to makeshift housing on land of no economic value: edges, roads, steep slopes. They are called shanty towns, squatter settlements or slums, with local names such as favelas (Brazil), barriadas (Latin America), bidonvilles (North Africa), bustees (Indian subcontinent).
  2. Share of population in slums: Nairobi about 55 per cent, Mexico City 46, Lima 36, Caracas 35, Kolkata 33, Rio 27, Jakarta 25, Santiago 25.
  3. Hazards: floods, landslides, pollution, fire, crime, disease, with no piped water. Homes use scrap materials. Even so, migrants may be better off than in rural areas.

Urban fringe developments

Urban environments

Urban fringe developments

Urban fringe developments (1/2)

  1. The rural-urban fringe is where countryside meets the built-up area and is lost to housing, industry, shopping, recreation and utilities (greenfield sites).
  2. Push from the city: old, congested, dear housing, pollution and noise, and shortage of land.
  3. Pull of the fringe: cheaper land with bigger houses, space for car parks, nearness to main roads and motorways, and car mobility.

Making urban living more sustainable

Urban environments

Making urban living more sustainable

Making urban living more sustainable

  1. Aim: shrink ecological footprints and reduce inequality by using renewable resources, efficient energy, public transport, better infrastructure (water, sanitation), social services and quality of life.
  2. Internal actions: reuse brownfield sites, compact cities with less private car use, fair society, wide participation, healthy secure living space and green infrastructure. External input actions conserve resources, use them efficiently, protect biodiversity and respect environmental capacity. Output actions minimise emissions, limit greenfield use, provide leisure space and recycle waste.
Spec 6.3b The range of possible strategies aimed at making urban living more sustainable and…
Making urban living more sustainable

Masdar City, Abu Dhabi

  1. Eco-city begun in 2008 for 40 000 residents plus 5000 commuters. Renewable power, nearly all solar (rooftop panels and one of the biggest photovoltaic plants in the Middle East). Street layout uses cooling winds, and buildings mix Arabic design with modern technology to cut air conditioning, heating and lighting. Smart water systems use 54 per cent less water than average, 75 per cent of hot water is solar, and desalination is renewable. Near-zero waste with a ban on plastic. Residents get five hours of sustainability education a year. Limits: high capital cost and it suits only places with plentiful renewable energy.
Spec 6.3b The range of possible strategies aimed at making urban living more sustainable and…
Making urban living more sustainable

Curitiba, Brazil

  1. Over 2 million people; planning began in 1968 (population then 430 000). Five main roads became dual carriageways with an express bus lane, using triple-articulated buses every 60 seconds, one fare, used by 70 per cent, now on biofuels.
  2. Parks surround the city (they deter favelas, with lakes for flood control, and sheep trim the grass). Recycling since 1980 with over two-thirds of waste recycled, and 'cambio verde' sites pay in fruit and vegetables. Self-help suburb for slum residents. 99 per cent of residents are satisfied. Idea of the former mayor Jaime Lerner, copied mostly in developed countries.
Spec 6.2b Urban challenges in a named developed country
Making urban living more sustainable

Urban gardens, Afghanistan

  1. Mazar-i-Sharif: refugees from conflict and disasters. A People in Need scheme (EU and Czech funding) trains families to grow food on 10 to 15 sq m plots (rainwater harvesting, greenhouses, storage) and sets up female self-help groups with business skills, nutrition and hygiene training, and micro-loans (shops, tailoring, poultry). It cuts food insecurity, poverty and reliance on rural food.

Managing urban challenges: slums (2/3)

Self-help and site-and-service

Self-help gives tools, training and low-interest loans to improve slum homes. Site-and-service sells or rents plots with basic services plus loans for materials.

Spec 6.2b, 6.3a Urban challenges in a named developed country
Managing urban challenges: slums

Managing urban challenges: slums (3/3)

  1. Local stakeholders: slum residents, neighbours (who favour options 1 to 3), utility suppliers, MPs and councillors, city councils (the most influential), landowners and developers, employers (who want cheap labour nearby) and planners.
  2. National: government (cost, votes, policies) and national charities (e.g. Magic Bus).
  3. International: charities (Oxfam, CAFOD, Christian Aid, MSF) mostly for option 4, and IGOs (World Bank, UNESCO, WHO) working through governments, where corruption can block aid.
  4. Local stakeholders mostly decide, national governments support or oppose, and charities help most with options 3 and 4.
