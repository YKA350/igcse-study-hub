Globalisation

The process by which countries are drawn into a single global economy through growing economic, communication and transport links, making them interdependent.

The rise of the global economy

The rise of the global economy (3/5)

Decisions in one place affect others. More exactly this is economic globalisation; it can also be cultural and political.

Trade between distant countries is old (colonial empires), but its scale has risen since the mid-20th century.

Four developments

transnational corporations (TNCs); trading blocs such as the EU and NAFTA that remove barriers; modern air, land and sea transport (a shrinking world);

The rise of the global economy

The rise of the global economy (4/5)

and communications and information technology that move data and decisions in seconds, so a London HQ can monitor branches worldwide.

Production chain (commodity or supply chain): stages where value is added.

The jeans example

cotton grown in Egypt, cloth woven in Thailand, synthetic fibre from China, buttons and zips from India, trousers made in Bangladesh, shipped to Rotterdam and distributed in the UK.

The rise of the global economy

The rise of the global economy (5/5)

Other evidence

call centres in India, the Philippines and Thailand, outsourcing of food and manufacturing where labour is cheap, the global shift of manufacturing to emerging countries, and growing economic migrants and tourists.

Global institutions: the major players

Global institutions: the major players (1/2)

The major players are the UN, World Bank, IMF, WTO, TNCs, the USA, the EU, Japan and OPEC.

IMF (1944)

keeps exchange rates stable and runs international payments.

WTO (1995)

promotes free and fair trade and polices the rules, acting as an umpire.

Global institutions: the major players

Global institutions: the major players (2/2)

World Bank (1944)

low-interest loans and technical help to cut poverty, from schools and health to farming and infrastructure.

NGOs such as CARE, Oxfam, Action Against Hunger and the Red Cross and Red Crescent target poverty and hunger. Three of these were founded in the mid-1940s to help the world recover from the war.

Global institutions: the major players

Transnational corporations (1/2)

TNCs (MNCs) are vast business empires with great influence.

Top ten by revenue in 2016

Walmart, State Grid, Samsung, China National Petroleum, Sinopec, Royal Dutch Shell, Exxon Mobil, Volkswagen, Toyota, Apple.

Over half are in oil and gas, vital as raw materials, transport fuel and electricity sources.

Global institutions: the major players

Transnational corporations (2/2)

Production chains aim to maximise sales and profits. Other reasons: be close to big markets (Nissan at Sunderland from 1986), sell inside trade barriers, use government incentives, and face fewer restrictions.

Growing volume of migration

Growing volume of migration (1/8)

Refugee

Outside their country because of a well-founded fear of persecution

Asylum seeker

Refugee who has applied for citizenship where they sought protection

Internally displaced person (IDP)

Forced from home but within the same country

Growing volume of migration

Growing volume of migration (2/8)

Returnee

Refugee, asylum seeker or IDP who has gone home

Global population growth is mainly natural increase (high births, falling deaths), with Africa and the Middle East fastest and North America, Europe and Russia little growth.

Migration has small effect on the global total but is rising with globalisation. A migrant in the UK is someone who moves and stays over a year.

Growing volume of migration

Growing volume of migration (3/8)

Types

international (between countries) or internal (within one), voluntary or forced. Circulation is a temporary absence (shopping, commuting, tourism).

Voluntary

for work, higher wages or quality of life. In developing and emerging countries it means rural to urban; in developed countries, counter-urbanisation. Economic migrants (e.g.

Eastern Europeans in the UK) often stay under a year (temporary).

Growing volume of migration

Growing volume of migration (4/8)

Other forms

retirement migration (UK retirees to Spain, Portugal, France, Australia) and sporting migrants (Messi, Argentina to Spain).

Medical tourism now flows from developed to less developed countries for cheaper treatment.

Growing volume of migration

Growing volume of migration (5/8)

Forced

natural hazards (usually temporary) or the actions of people: war and persecution (WWII Jews, Palestinian Arabs in 1948, ethnic cleansing in Yugoslavia and Rwanda, where 800 000 died and 2 million were displaced, and the Sunni-Shia conflict now).

Growing volume of migration

Growing volume of migration (6/8)

65.3 million were forcibly displaced in 2015 (about one-third refugees). Hotspots: the Middle East, Africa and Colombia (Syria 7.9 million, Colombia 6.5, Iraq 4.3, Pakistan 3.4, Sudan 2.8).

Growing volume of migration

Growing volume of migration (7/8)

Push-pull

push factors at the origin (unemployment, poverty, heavy taxes, shortage of housing, harsh climate, ill health, civil war, ethnic cleansing) and pull factors at the destination (work, high wages, a better lifestyle, welfare, personal security, freedom of speech).

Pull is often the mirror image of push.

Growing volume of migration

Growing volume of migration (8/8)

Globalisation adds modern communications (information reduces the risk), modern transport and relaxed borders for skilled workers.

Impacts of global tourism

Globalisation and migration

Impacts of global tourism

Impacts of global tourism (1/4)

Economic

Labour-intensive jobs plus a multiplier effect that benefits agriculture, manufacturing (souvenirs) and local shops. But leakage: big firms (TUI, Thomas Cook) take profits, e.g. 90 per cent in Vanuatu

Socio-cultural

Mostly negative: drunkenness, ignoring dress codes, prostitution and crime, loss of local language, disrespect in holy places. Some crafts and rituals revived. Little real cultural mixing

Impacts of global tourism

Impacts of global tourism (2/4)

Environmental

Few positives (ecotourism educates). Negatives: habitat clearance (mangrove, rainforest) for hotels, water overuse, sea and river pollution, coral damage, wildlife disturbance, traffic and noise

About 900 million international tourists a year (over a tenth of the world), up nearly 30 times in the second half of the 20th century.

Impacts of global tourism

Impacts of global tourism (3/4)

Causes

more leisure time, paid holidays (under 40-hour weeks, up to 6 weeks leave) and disposable income; faster, cheaper transport (jets, cruise ships, high-speed rail); mass media awareness;

relaxed border controls (governments earn from visas and departure taxes);

and marketing by tour operators, especially the package holiday, central to mass tourism (large-scale, commercial, popular destinations).

Impacts of global tourism

Impacts of global tourism (4/4)

Top earners in 2015

USA $204.5 billion, China 114.1, Spain 56.5, France 45.9, UK 45.5, Thailand 44.6.

Impacts of global tourism

Case study: Costa Blanca, Spain (1/2)

A 200 km coast either side of Alicante, once fishing, transformed since the 1960s package holidays into a strip of high-rise hotels.

Jobs, better infrastructure, and a multiplier effect for construction and farmers. Pressures: visitors from May to October in a small area; tourists use almost twice as much water as locals;

Impacts of global tourism

Case study: Costa Blanca, Spain (2/2)

over 50 million tonnes of waste a year; coastal urbanisation damaging ecosystems; second homes; pollution from cars, planes and boats.

Benidorm

70 000 permanent residents but over half a million at peak; planned from 1954, boomed in the 1960s with British package tourists; now all year with nightlife, theme parks, skyscrapers.

Geopolitical relationships

Geopolitical relationships

Geopolitical relationships (1/3)

Geopolitics

The study of political and economic relations between countries and how geography (distance, climate, resources) affects them, i.e. the power and influence of countries over others.

Power comes from large extent, strategic location, a large educated population, natural resources, a high level of development, modern technology and strong military.

Geopolitical relationships

Geopolitical relationships (2/3)

Trade

blocs (EU, NAFTA, ASEAN) allow free trade among members with tariffs on outsiders and favour blocs and superpowers. China buys African minerals cheaply, sometimes in exchange for railways and dams.

Migration

flows go toward more powerful, developed countries (USA, EU, Australia), which control numbers by visas and quotas (Australia chooses skilled migrants).

Geopolitical relationships

Geopolitical relationships (3/3)

Germany admitted over 500 000 Syrian refugees but the UK under 20 000, preferring camps near Syria. China and Russia attract few migrants.

Tourism

distance matters less. Superpowers have many travellers and attract visitors. Hostility or unrest deters tourists, and North Korea excludes most.

Poor countries can join tourism but companies from powerful countries often control it.

Managing migration

Globalisation and migration

Managing migration

Managing migration

Net migration

Immigration minus emigration.

Managing migration

Case study: the UK (1/2)

After 1945 labour shortages brought Commonwealth immigrants from the Caribbean and the Indian subcontinent, over 1 million by 1971, then controls were introduced.

The foreign-born population grew 2.1 million (1951) to 3.2 million (1971), 3.8 million (1991), 4.9 million (2001) and 8.0 million (2011, 12.7 per cent).

Managing migration

Case study: the UK (2/2)

In the 1990s labour shortages and the end of communism, plus the 2004 EU enlargement, brought Eastern Europeans (over half Polish, over 80 per cent aged 18 to 34), mostly temporary.

Net immigration was about 300 000 a year, and control was a key issue in the 2016 Brexit referendum, which will let the UK end free entry for EU citizens.

Policy has followed the labour situation. Concern is now growing.

Making tourism more sustainable

Making tourism more sustainable

Ecotourism

Small-scale tourism in special places (scenery, wildlife, remoteness, culture) that educates visitors, minimises damage and non-renewable resource use, and is controlled by locals, who also provide labour and produce, so profits stay local.

It is greener than mass tourism, but flights and driving still burn fossil fuels.

Making tourism more sustainable

The Gambia (1/2)

Africa's smallest country (11 000 sq km, 1.8 million people, 176 per sq km), few resources, agriculture is 30 per cent of GDP and 70 per cent of jobs; remittances and tourism are each 20 per cent.

About 100 000 tourists a year, mostly from Europe by a six-hour flight.

Making tourism more sustainable

The Gambia (2/2)

Hard to make sustainable

package tour operators take profits, few untouched areas, goods imported, tourism concentrated on the coast. Efforts: eco-lodges and camps upriver, avoiding pollution and respecting Islamic culture.

Making tourism more sustainable

Bhutan (1/2)

A remote Himalayan kingdom, isolated for 300 years, with mountains and Buddhist heritage appealing to trekkers and birdwatchers. Tourism began in 1974.

The state limits numbers (initially 2500 a year), requires escorted groups, vetted companies, a daily surcharge of US$250 per person (US$40 for solos or couples as stated), and traditional architecture.

Making tourism more sustainable

Bhutan (2/2)

Ambivalent

wants the money but tightly controls impact. One of the most sustainable industries in the world.

Making tourism more sustainable

Cyprus (1/2)

Over 2 million tourists a year (Greek part); coastal damage and displaced communities before the Cyprus Sustainable Tourism Initiative (2006).

Aims

protect the environment, use resources efficiently, cut the carbon footprint, and help disadvantaged communities.

Projects

beach greening, rural tourism away from the coast (women's skills in lodging, crafts, food), and managing water, energy (solar) and plastic.

Making tourism more sustainable

Cyprus (2/2)

Carbon footprint is hard to cut since tourists arrive by air.
