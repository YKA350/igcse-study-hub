Different types of hazard

Different types of hazard (2/4)

Hazard

An event that threatens or causes damage and destruction to people, property and settlements.

Natural hazard

A hazard produced by environmental processes. Without people there would only be natural events, not hazards.

Places differ in hazard because some have more than one type, some have more frequent or stronger events, and some cope better.

Different types of hazard

Different types of hazard (3/4)

Hazards can have mixed causes

lava can dam a river and flood upstream, tsunamis cause coastal floods, and storm surges come from low pressure not rain.

Some events become hazards indirectly

drought hits food production and causes malnutrition. Malaria is a biological hazard carried by mosquitoes, while typhoid and cholera link to pollution.

Tropical cyclones

Hazardous environments

Tropical cyclones

Causes (1/2)

Hot sea air rises, causing thunderstorms. These sometimes combine into a strong flow of warm rising air and an area of low pressure.

Conditions needed

a deep layer of humid, warm (over 27 degrees C) unstable air; energy from warm sea surface, at its warmest in late summer;

Tropical cyclones

Causes (2/2)

circulation of air, anti-clockwise in the northern hemisphere, helped by the Coriolis force; and small changes in wind speed and direction with height (wind shear).

Structure

a spiral of rising warm air with a calm eye of sinking air and clear skies, surrounded by the eye wall where energy is most destructive.

Tropical cyclones

Distribution

Over warm tropical oceans in both hemispheres, called hurricanes (North America, Central America, North Atlantic), typhoons (north-west Pacific) and cyclones (Indian Ocean and Australia).

Names come from alphabetical lists of alternating male and female names on a six-year cycle.

Tropical cyclones

Characteristics (1/2)

Very low pressure (under 950 mb), 600 to 700 km across, torrential rain, thunder and lightning, and winds over 120 km per hour.

They follow prevailing winds and ocean currents. Longer sea tracks give more strength. On reaching land the energy supply is cut, so they weaken, slow and die. Average life is 10 days, up to four weeks for the biggest.

Tropical cyclones

Characteristics (2/2)

Damage comes from strong winds, torrential rain (inland floods, landslides, mudslides) and storm surges, which are sudden sea level rises from the very low pressure, worse on shallow coastlines.

Volcanic eruptions and earthquakes

Hazardous environments

Volcanic eruptions and earthquakes

Causes: plate margins (1/2)

Constructive (divergent)

Movement: Plates move apart, magma rises; Result: New crust and submarine volcanoes, e.g. mid-Atlantic ridge

Destructive (convergent, subduction)

Movement: Plates move together, denser one sinks; Result: Volcanoes and earthquakes, e.g. Nazca and South American plates

Collision

Movement: Two plates of equal density meet head on; Result: Fold mountains and earthquakes, e.g. Himalayas

Volcanic eruptions and earthquakes

Causes: plate margins (2/2)

Conservative (transform)

Movement: Plates slide past each other; Result: Earthquakes but no volcanoes, e.g. California

Hotspot

A location under the crust with a strong plume of rising magma. Where the crust is weak a volcano forms, as in Hawaii.

Plate movement is imperceptibly slow but is the most powerful natural force, creating rocks, mountains and rift valleys by volcanic activity, folding and faulting.

Volcanic eruptions and earthquakes

Distribution

Both occur along plate boundaries. Volcanoes concentrate around the Pacific (destructive margins, Ring of Fire) and the African Rift Valley (constructive). Pacific shores also have many earthquakes, and quakes occur under oceans.

Volcanic eruptions and earthquakes

Characteristics of volcanic eruptions

Lava flows rarely go beyond 10 km and seldom kill but destroy farmland, buildings and transport.

Ash is carried by wind over wide areas (Iceland 2010 stopped European air travel), blankets crops and roads, collapses roofs and can suffocate people and animals.

Gases such as sulphur gases, carbon dioxide and cyanide are dense and stay low. Pyroclastic flows are especially lethal.

Volcanic eruptions and earthquakes

Characteristics of earthquakes (1/2)

Focus

The point underground where an earthquake starts. The epicentre is the point on the surface directly above.

Shock waves are strongest near the epicentre. Damage is worst where the focus is shallow and the rock soft, because shaking liquefies soft rock and foundations collapse.

Volcanic eruptions and earthquakes

Characteristics of earthquakes (2/2)

Buildings kill more than earthquakes

falling masonry traps people. Gas pipes and cables rupture, so fire is a hazard. Movement is vertical and horizontal.

Tsunamis follow undersea earthquakes. The 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami from off Sumatra had waves up to 30 m and nearly 300 000 casualties. Krakatoa in 1883 made waves up to 35 m that killed over 36 000.

The scale of tectonic hazards (1/2)

Volcanic eruptions

Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI), 1 to 8, logarithmic, from volume of ash and ash height; suited only to explosive eruptions

Earthquakes

Richter Scale (energy released, seismograph, logarithmic, each point is about 30 times more energy); Mercalli Scale (effects on people and buildings);

Moment Magnitude Scale (since 1979, the most used, based on fault movement and area)

The scale of tectonic hazards (2/2)

Tropical cyclones

Saffir-Simpson Scale

Damage also depends on area affected, population density, duration, warning time, preparedness (shelters, education, building design, location) and ability to cope afterwards.

Lack of capital and technology explains why disasters hit developing countries harder.

Impacts of tectonic hazards

Hazardous environments

Impacts of tectonic hazards

Impacts of tectonic hazards

Earthquake primary impacts

collapsed buildings, deaths and injuries, broken pipelines and power lines. Secondary: tsunamis, aftershocks, fires.

Volcanic primary impacts

lava destroying buildings, roads and crops, deaths from lava, ash and gas, and water contaminated by ash. Secondary: lahars (ash and mud with rain or melting snow), fires, and psychological trauma.

Impacts of tectonic hazards

Case study: earthquakes in Nepal (2015)

25 April 2015, magnitude 7.8 (collision margin): over 8500 deaths, nearly 17 000 injured, nearly 3 million displaced.

Damaged power, communications and roads, landslides blocked roads, and small airports slowed aid. A second quake of 7.3 three weeks later killed 155. Nepal is landlocked, so no tsunami.

Impacts of tectonic hazards

Case study: earthquakes in central Italy (2016)

The Apennines formed as the Adriatic plate is subducted under Eurasia. Quakes: 24 August (6.2, Amatrice, focus 4 km) killed about 300; 26 October (6.1); 30 October (6.6, strongest in 36 years);

3 November (4.8). Later quakes caused less damage, perhaps because people were alert.

Lessons

even developed countries are not quake-proof (old buildings, ignored regulations), and damage depends on epicentre location.

Impacts of tectonic hazards

Case study: Mount Merapi (2010, Indonesia) (1/2)

On Java, nearly 3000 m, on the destructive margin where Indo-Australian plate subducts under Eurasian. Eruption 26 October to 12 November.

Primary

volcanic bombs 11 km, ash 5 km high and 30 km away, pyroclastic flows 3 km, sulphur dioxide as far as Australia, lahars.

Impacts of tectonic hazards

Case study: Mount Merapi (2010, Indonesia) (2/2)

Secondary

shelters over 15 km away, 20 km danger zone, nearly 300 000 evacuated, flights grounded, crop damage. About 350 deaths, many from pyroclastic flows.

Impacts of tectonic hazards

Case study: Mount Ontake (2014, Japan)

27 September 2014, 3067 m, erupted without warning. About 50 hikers were killed by a pyroclastic flow and nearly 100 injured. Few people live there, so impact was limited.

It was a small steam and ash eruption with no usual warning signs, showing some events remain unpredictable.

Lack of education and information about the risk.

People know the risk but gain benefits.

Reasons for living in high-risk areas

Reasons for living in high-risk areas (2/2)

Optimism or resignation.

Momentum

cities have grown over centuries with family, friends, work and huge investment.

Volcano benefits

minerals (diamonds, gold, copper), fertile soils from ash, geothermal energy and tourism (Vesuvius). Flood-prone deltas such as the Ganges-Brahmaputra-Meghna (90 million people) have fertile soils.

Tropical cyclones and their impacts

Tropical cyclones and their impacts (2/3)

4: Winds (km per h): 131 to 155; Damage: Extreme

5: Winds (km per h): Over 155; Damage: Catastrophic

Storms of 119 km per h or more are cyclones, rated by Saffir-Simpson on wind speed, pressure, surge and damage. Satellites allow forecasts and warnings.

Tropical cyclones and their impacts

Tropical cyclones and their impacts (3/3)

Impacts

physical (property), social (deaths, disruption), economic (business and transport) and environmental (landslides, soil erosion, flooding). Primary are direct results of wind, rain and surge;

secondary include repair costs and waterborne disease.

Countries hit most since 1970

China, Philippines, Japan, Mexico, USA. Japan has far fewer casualties than China and the Philippines despite similar numbers of hits.

Tropical cyclones and their impacts

Case study: Hurricane Katrina (USA, 2005) (1/2)

Formed over the Bahamas, reached Category 5 at sea with winds over 300 km per h and weakened to Category 3 at landfall near New Orleans on 29 August.

Nearly 2000 killed, $84 billion damage, 1 million displaced. An 8 m surge broke the levees; 80 per cent of the city flooded up to 6 m deep.

Tropical cyclones and their impacts

Case study: Hurricane Katrina (USA, 2005) (2/2)

Criticisms

neglected levees, slow emergency response, poor coordination, food and water shortages, people left behind, looting, and missing donations. Recovery was funded largely by insurance, and the city has become gentrified.

Tropical cyclones and their impacts

Case study: Typhoon Haiyan (Philippines, 2013) (1/2)

Category 5, winds over 300 km per h. About 800 000 evacuated, 1.9 million homeless, over 6000 killed. Airport damaged and roads blocked slowed aid, and the surge destroyed shipping.

About 3 million needed food aid. Recovery slogan is Build Back Better, with no-build coastal zones, a new surge warning system and replanted mangroves.

Tropical cyclones and their impacts

Case study: Typhoon Haiyan (Philippines, 2013) (2/2)

Comparison

the Philippines had a bigger, denser population (890 per square km against 343) but a lower death rate, thanks to nearly five days of warning against under 24 hours for Katrina.

The poor and elderly suffered most in both. Insurance meant faster recovery in New Orleans, while poor Filipinos struggled. Good management matters more than wealth alone.

Predicting and preparing for earthquakes

Hazardous environments

Predicting and preparing for earthquakes

Predicting and preparing for earthquakes (1/4)

Construction

Avoid unreinforced brick and block. Use lighter materials and flexible frames with reinforced steel corners; tall buildings use steel frames, weights and shutters;

Japanese floating homes lift on airbags. Expensive for developing countries, where traditional timber, palm and thatch may suit

Services

Flexible pipes, automatic shut-offs, quake-proofed bridges, trains that halt

Predicting and preparing for earthquakes

Predicting and preparing for earthquakes (2/4)

Warning systems

Seismometers, accelerometers and satellites; tsunami warnings work better as waves are slower

Remote sensing and GIS

Satellite images aid rescue and map seismic risk, settlements and landslide areas

Education

Drills in schools, safe open spaces, advice such as drop, cover, hold

Predicting and preparing for earthquakes

Predicting and preparing for earthquakes (3/4)

Risk assessment

Judging the probability of a hazard and the scale of damage, using its distribution, frequency, scale and predictability, plus location such as exposure to tsunamis.

Hazard management steps

identify threats, risk assessment, prediction, preparation (adjustment), hazard event, recovery, appraisal.

Predicting and preparing for earthquakes

Predicting and preparing for earthquakes (4/4)

Prediction

plate tectonics shows where quakes occur but not when. Warnings after a quake begins give only minutes, mainly for distant areas.

Deaths depend on more than magnitude. Haiti 2010 (7.0) killed about 316 000 in a poor, dense, unprepared country, while Japan 2011 (9.0) killed about 20 000 as it is well prepared.

Nepal and Italy deaths were limited by rural epicentres.

Responding to hazards

Hazardous environments

Responding to hazards

Responding to hazards (1/4)

Sequence

hazard event, disruption, disaster, rescue and relief, temporary improvement (days to weeks), permanent reconstruction (weeks to years), recovery.

Emergency response

find and reach worst-hit areas fast;

Responding to hazards

Responding to hazards (2/4)

rescue trapped people, clear rubble, restore water, sewage, gas, electricity and communications, give medical help and counselling, distribute food, water and clothes, set up shelters and supply transport (often the armed forces).

Coordination often fails. International aid comes from UN agencies, Oxfam and the Red Cross. In Nepal damaged roads hindered it.

Responding to hazards

Responding to hazards (3/4)

Recovery

decide whether to restore or abandon high-risk areas. World Bank loans help rebuild homes, businesses and infrastructure.

Appraisal

review how well emergency work went and ask whether risk, warnings, evacuation, aid delivery, building standards and maps need updating, whether defences need strengthening, whether settlements should relocate and whether rebuilding is hazard-proof.

Responding to hazards

Responding to hazards (4/4)

The UNISDR gives technical advice.

People stay optimistic ('lightning never strikes here') and hazards can strike anywhere, repeatedly and more strongly.

Responding to hazards

Case study: preparing for earthquakes in India (1/2)

Half of India's land is earthquake prone and some big cities lie in Zones 4 and 5. Indo-Australian plate moves toward Eurasian at about 50 mm a year. Population about 1.3 billion at 400 per square km.

Responding to hazards

Case study: preparing for earthquakes in India (2/2)

Measures

National Disaster Management Authority and Disaster Response Force, and seismic retrofitting of existing buildings (hospitals and schools first). It is costly, loans exist, there is a huge backlog and little checking.

Earthquakes remain the most hazardous and unpredictable of the three hazards, with no change in frequency.
