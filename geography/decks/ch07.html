Fragile environments

Fragile environments (1/2)

Fragile environment

A natural environment with a delicate balance between non-living parts (climate, rocks, soils) and living parts (plants, animals), easily upset.

Spec 7.1c, 7.2c Causes of natural climate change
Fragile environments

Fragile environments (2/2)

  1. Natural hazards (fire, high winds, eruptions) have always disturbed environments, but they usually recovered. Early people lived in harmony with the environment. Today people have disturbed about 90 per cent of the Earth to some degree.
  2. Fragility rises with pressure. The ecological footprint measures the land and sea needed to supply our water, energy and food. A fair share is a little under 2 global hectares each, while the UK uses about 5.5, so the world would need two more planets if everyone lived like the UK. The global average is 2.85. Imports deepen the footprint of the countries supplying them.
  3. Influences on a footprint: population growth, consumption per person, and technology.
  4. Three processes shape fragile environments and are linked: desertification, deforestation and climate change.

Fragile environments

Desertification

Desertification

Once-productive land gradually turning desert-like, usually in semi-arid land on the margins of hot deserts. It is not necessarily irreversible. At risk: southern Asia, the Middle East and North Africa, including the Sahel.

  1. Characteristics: no surface water, dried ponds and watercourses, lower water table, degraded or lost vegetation, soil erosion by wind, salt build-up, less usable soil and loose dry sand.
Spec 7.1b Causes of desertification
Fragile environments

Deforestation

  1. Temperate forests (deciduous in Europe, China and the USA) were mostly cleared long ago for timber and crops. Cold coniferous forests are largely untouched. Tropical rainforest is being lost fast.
  2. Clear felling removes everything; selective felling takes only the best trees. Bare soil is exposed to wind and rain erosion.

Causes of desertification and deforestation

Fragile environments and climate change

Causes of desertification and deforestation

Causes of desertification and deforestation

  1. Natural causes of desertification: less predictable rain and droughts that last years, soil erosion removing support for plants, and short intense rain that soil cannot store.
  2. Human causes: population growth, migration (spreading pressure), overgrazing (especially round water holes), over-cultivation of marginal land, and deforestation for fuel, fencing and housing. Civil war in sub-Saharan countries adds to famine. Causes combine in a cycle of population, farming, poverty and soil exhaustion.
Spec 7.1b Causes of desertification
Causes of desertification and deforestation

Case study: the Sahel (1/2)

  1. About 20 per cent of the world's people in over 60 countries are affected. The Sahel is a belt south of the Sahara across Africa in the poorest region (sub-Saharan Africa). Semi-arid savanna, always hot, with a long dry season from June to January and just enough rain for grasses, shrubs and trees. Nomadic herders move with goats and cattle.
  2. Until the 1960s water was plentiful. Then population grew, trees were felled for fuel, and over-cultivation and overgrazing exposed ground, so no humus was added and soil held no water, drying and eroding by wind and flash floods. Since the 1970s crop failures are almost yearly, over 100 000 have starved and millions of animals died, with many migrating.

Causes of climate change

Fragile environments and climate change

Causes of climate change

Causes of climate change (1/3)

  1. The climate has changed many times; the last Ice Age ended 12 000 years ago. Natural causes: orbit eccentricity, tilt (obliquity) and wobble (precession). Milankovitch's theory says every 100 000 years these combine to cut solar radiation and cause a glacial period. Other natural causes: huge volcanic eruptions and cosmic material (meteors, asteroids) throwing up dust.
  2. Recorded temperatures: land is about 1 degree C warmer than the late 19th century, with a rise of 0.7 degrees C over 100 years and a predicted rise up to 4.5 degrees C by 2100. A 6 degree C rise in the Permian is linked to the loss of 95 per cent of species.
  3. Most scientists think the present warming is largely human, from greenhouse gases.

Causes of climate change

Causes of climate change (3/3)

  1. CO2 is nearly three-quarters of emissions. Emissions by sector (2014): electricity and heat 25 per cent, agriculture, forestry and land use 24, industry 21, transport 14, buildings 6, other energy 10. The USA alone is blamed for 36 per cent of emissions in the book's data.
  2. Greenhouse effect: sunlight passes through the atmosphere and warms the Earth, some is reflected, and greenhouse gases absorb outgoing heat and send some back, trapping warmth. More gases mean more warming (global warming).
  3. Some scientists argue today's warming is natural variation. Pollution (oil spills) and river and coastal management also stress environments.

Causes of climate change · diagram

The greenhouse effect

Natural effect

Gases trap some heat, keeping Earth warm enough for life.

Enhanced effect

Burning fossil fuels, deforestation and farming add more gases, so more heat is trapped.

Result

Global temperatures rise: melting ice, sea-level rise and more extreme weather.

Impacts of desertification

Impacts of desertification

Impacts of desertification

  1. A chain of five: soil (no nutrient cycle, fertility drops, erosion, dust), reduced agricultural output (crops fail, livestock die), malnutrition, famine and starvation, migration (die or leave), and conflict with the people already in the destination. The chain is a downward spiral unless action is taken.

Impacts of deforestation

Fragile environments and climate change

Impacts of deforestation

Impacts of deforestation

  1. Physical problems: flooding (eroded soil silts rivers), loss of plants and animals, leaching of nutrients from bare soil, loss of forests and burning raising CO2 and cutting oxygen, and tribes forced into smaller areas.
  2. Deforestation continues in all 16 tropical countries studied, rising fast in Indonesia and Peru, though 7 countries including Brazil have seen declines.
  3. Loss of biodiversity: it supplies food, timber, medicines, fibres and resins. Forests absorb CO2, so clearance adds to greenhouse gases.
  4. Some countries see economic benefits (Brazil, Guyana, Indonesia, Thailand, Philippines). But benefits are short-term unless forests are replaced, and Ivory Coast has almost nothing left while Madagascar clears forest to pay debts. Developing countries ask why they should not develop, while protests come from developed countries.

Impacts of climate change

Fragile environments and climate change

Impacts of climate change

Impacts of climate change (1/2)

  1. Everyone agrees the climate is changing. Some areas will become wetter and others drier, with greater flood risk in some.
  2. More hazards: warmer seas make tropical storms more intense (more frequent and powerful since 1980), more heavy rain and floods, heatwaves (40 degrees C for weeks killed 13 000 in France in 2003), droughts, and more frequent El Nino and La Nina. The Sahel is expanding.
  3. Ecosystem changes: biomes shift toward the poles (forest into tundra, tundra into ice desert). Gains: farming further north and higher; oil, gas and minerals become accessible and Arctic shipping routes open.
  4. Ice loss: glaciers shrink, Greenland and Arctic ice melts, Antarctic sections break off, and sea level rises. Sea level has risen 20 cm since 1880 and 1 to 2 m is predicted by 2100.
Spec 7.1c, 7.1b Causes of natural climate change
Impacts of climate change

Impacts of climate change (2/2)

  1. Drowning cities: Miami tops the list of the most threatened (a 1 metre rise leaves downtown on an island). Other US cities: New York, New Orleans, Boston, Washington, Philadelphia, Tampa, San Francisco. Elsewhere: Osaka, Kobe, Tokyo, Rotterdam, Amsterdam, Nagoya.
  2. Changing settlement: mass retreat from the coast and migration. Reduced jobs: farmland, cities, resorts and ports lost. Health and well-being: malaria spreads to higher latitudes and altitudes, dry areas have scarcer water and more cholera, typhoid and bilharzia, plus insecurity. Conflict: food and water insecurity can drive migration and fights.

Dealing with desertification

Fragile environments and climate change

Dealing with desertification

Dealing with desertification

  1. Natural causes cannot be changed; the aim is to help people cope.
  2. Education about sustainable land use.
  3. Reduce soil erosion by planting trees and perennials for permanent cover.
  4. Change farming: permaculture (trees with annual crops and hedgerows) for food security, and rock fertiliser.
  5. Fuelwood: fast-growing fuel trees (CARE), though this uses food land, and alternative fuels cost too much.
  6. Water conservation: stone-and-cement water-spreading weirs across valleys slow flow, deposit soil, raise the water table and extend farmland all year. They cost $400 to $2000 per hectare, much less than a dam, helped by micro-loans, and need community cooperation.

Managing rainforests sustainably (1/2)

Sustainability

Using resources so that each generation hands the next at least as much as it had.

Spec 7.3b Different approaches to the sustainable use and management of a rainforest in a named…
Managing rainforests sustainably

Managing rainforests sustainably (2/2)

  1. Sustainable management respects local people and cultures, uses traditional skills, gives locals control, uses appropriate (cheap, easy, harmless) technology, makes income for communities not TNCs, and protects biodiversity.
  2. Methods: protected national parks, controlled logging, selective logging (Indonesia allows 7 to 12 trees per hectare), replanting, fewer logging licences, heli-logging (Sarawak), alternative energy (biogas, solar, wind), agroforestry (crops with trees) and substitution (plantations elsewhere, grazing on savanna). Figure options: tree crops or timber, selective logging with replanting, biosphere reserve (core, buffer zone, eco-lodge), agroforestry, and less destructive mining (topsoil stored then replanted, costing about 0.7 per cent of mine costs).
Spec 7.3b Different approaches to the sustainable use and management of a rainforest in a named…
Managing rainforests sustainably

Case study: Brazil

  1. Forest Code (1965): landowners must keep up to 80 per cent forest, but it is hard to enforce. ARPA (2002): 45 million hectares of parks and reserves in 10 years. Replanting: REGUA near Rio collects seed, grows saplings and replants (70 000 saplings of 180 species a year). US-Brazil partnership: Brazil promised in 2015 to restore 12 million hectares. Timber plantations (eucalyptus) now cover 5 million hectares.
  2. International: UN-REDD (pays for leaving forest standing), UN Forum on Forests, CITES (wildlife trade) and the International Tropical Timber Agreement (2006). Satellites, Greenpeace and WWF help track illegal logging.

Responses to global warming and climate change

Fragile environments and climate change

Responses to global warming and climate change

Responses to global warming and climate change (1/3)

  1. Four basic responses: join the global effort to cut GHGs, protect forests, identify environments that will become more fragile, and identify coasts threatened by flooding or erosion.
  2. Kyoto (1997) asked for a 5 per cent cut by 2012 on 1990 levels. The Paris Agreement (2015) limits warming to well below 2 degrees C, in force when 55 countries with 55 per cent of emissions sign; China, the USA and the UK joined in 2016.
  3. Top emitters: China 22.7 per cent, USA 15.6, India 5.7, Russia 5.4, Japan 2.9, Brazil 2.6, Germany 2.1, Canada 1.7, Iran 1.6, Mexico 1.6.
