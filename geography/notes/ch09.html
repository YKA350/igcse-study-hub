1Defining development and human welfare
Defining development and human welfare
Development

A process of change that affects countries and their peoples, making progress in several 'strands'. The most important is economic development, which provides the power that drives the rest.

  1. Economic development comes from using resources (minerals, energy, climate, soils) plus capital, technology, enterprising people and good government.
  2. Other strand groups: demographic (life expectancy, birth control, mobility), social (welfare services, equal opportunities, quality of life), cultural (education, heritage, ethnic respect), political (vote, democracy, free speech) and environmental (pollution control, conservation, small ecological footprint).
  3. Factors generating economic development: resources, internal boosters (government intervention, national ambition, business culture) and external boosters (globalisation, TNCs and international agencies, geopolitics). Outcomes include higher productivity, rising living standards, better quality of life, more democracy, but also spatial inequality and environmental impacts.
Human welfare

The general condition of a population. It is usually described as quality of life, which includes four related parts: economic (secure job, income, affluence, standard of living), physical (diet, housing, clean environment, health, mobility), social (education, welfare services, family and friends, leisure), and psychological (satisfaction, happiness, security).

  1. Cycle of wealth: economic growth makes wealth, which becomes taxes (spent on roads, schools, health if governance is good), wages and disposable income (spent on goods and services), and reinvestment by businesses, repeating the cycle. Benefits are not shared equally, so the rich-poor gap often widens.
2Contributors to development and human welfare
Contributors to development and human welfare
  1. Natural resources: a country needs water, food and energy security, ideally from within its borders. Imports create vulnerability because supplies can be cut by war or politics, or prices raised as pressure.
  2. Human resources: technology (finding new ways to use resources or alternatives, so reducing imports) and the labour force (skills, enterprise, commitment), improved through education.
  3. Government: development flourishes with a democratic, stable government that encourages enterprise and investment. Capitalist (USA) or socialist (China) regimes both can top the rankings but differ in per capita GDP.
  4. Equality (of opportunities, gender, ethnicity, class) avoids tension and conflict.
  5. Economic growth, technology and enterprise are central 'cable strands'.

  2. Other indicators: energy consumption (rises with development) and population rates (birth rate, death rate and infant mortality fall, life expectancy rises).
Quality of life indicators
  1. Housing: floor space, dwellings with water, electricity, indoor toilet, owner-occupation. Education: spending, years in school, literacy, teachers per 1000. Health: infant mortality, life expectancy, doctors, hospital beds, calories.
  2. Examples: one in eight people lacks safe water (Afghanistan 13 per cent access, Ethiopia 22, Somalia 42). Illiteracy highest in Niger 85, Guinea 75, South Sudan 73, Chad 72. Daily calories: Ireland 3847 and Greece 3815 top, Somalia 1400 and Afghanistan 1529 lowest; below 2000 usually means malnutrition.
Human Development Index (HDI)

The most used composite measure of quality of life and development, averaging three equally weighted strands: income per capita, literacy (education) and life expectancy. It ranges from 0 to 1. High in North America, Europe, southern South America and Australasia; low in Africa.

  1. Gini index measures income inequality: low means equal, high means unequal.
  2. Corruption perception index (Transparency International, 2014): from clean (high value) to corrupt (low value). Asia, Africa and much of Latin America score corrupt.
4Uneven development
Uneven development
  1. By GDP per head at purchasing power parity (PPP): developing under $10 000, emerging $10 000 to $30 000, developed over $30 000. On this measure India is developing, though widely seen as emerging. Range is huge, from under $1000 (eight African countries) to Qatar at $132 000.
Development pathway
  1. Countries move along a pathway from least to most developed at different speeds. The UK started early, with empire resources and the Industrial Revolution (coal, iron ore, enterprise, labour), as did western Europe and the USA.
  2. Emerging countries advance through the global shift in manufacturing, drawn by much lower labour costs.
  3. Some countries are barely started because of obstacles. Political: misrule (North Korea, Zimbabwe), civil war (Angola, Somalia, Sudan) or the Soviet break-up. Economic: lack of resources, energy, trained labour, capital, technology and enterprise (Ethiopia, Bangladesh).
Dependency theory
Dependency theory

Poor countries (the periphery) stay poor because rich countries (the core) control the global economy.

  1. Evidence: resource exploitation since colonial times, now by TNCs. Reasons: interference in politics, unfair trade (cheap resources sold, expensive goods bought), selling non-essentials, bilateral aid with strings, and debt with interest. But not all poor countries were colonies, and some blame socialism.
Within countries
  1. National averages hide variation. Wealth is concentrated in a favoured region, usually with the capital or leading city (the core), leaving a periphery. Core-periphery theory says that cores first grow at the expense of peripheries, then wealth trickles or spreads out and the gap narrows. Examples: US states (high HDI in the north-east, low in the south), Italy and India. Governments sometimes use this as an excuse to do little.
5Impacts of uneven development: Italy
Impacts of uneven development: Italy
  1. Population 60 million, per capita GDP about $30 000. The North (core, Po basin) is wealthier than the South (periphery, the Mezzogiorno).
  2. North advantages: manufacturing, services and quaternary jobs, fertile lowland with irrigation, cities in the Turin industrial triangle (Milan, Turin, Genoa) with good transport, nearness to European markets, better housing and services, gas from the Po basin and HEP from the Alps.
  3. South disadvantages: mountains hindering communication, a hot dry summer and cold wet winter, thin limestone soils, poor grazing, weak transport, little employment outside farming and emigration. The South also receives migrants from North Africa.
  4. Since 1950 the government and the EU invested in motorways, irrigation (tomatoes, citrus, vegetables) and relocated heavy industry (steel, vehicles). But since 2007 the gap has widened in a recession because poorer areas suffer more.

Demographic characteristics
Demographic transition model

Birth and death rates change as countries develop, and so does natural increase. It has four or five stages.

7Strategies to reduce uneven development
Aid
  1. Aid is the transfer of money, goods and expertise. Multilateral aid is given through organisations (World Bank, UNESCO) that allocate it. Bilateral aid goes government to government, often with conditions (the USA's oil-search aid to Peru for jets and fishing access). Loans are the least satisfactory, and technical help is better if it matches skills: intermediate technology (such as a basic water pump) is easy to use and needs no foreign experts.
  2. Voluntary aid from NGOs (Oxfam, Red Cross, Save the Children) is often emergency aid.
  3. Providers follow the North-South divide, with Russia and Eastern Europe neither, and China and India providing aid with strings attached for resources.
Trade
  1. Trade helps development by funding imports, but trade barriers (tariffs, quotas) and terms favour developed and emerging countries. The Fairtrade Foundation (1992) obtains fair prices for coffee, cocoa, sugar, tea, bananas, honey and similar goods from small producers.
  2. Inter-governmental agreements involve the World Bank, IMF and WTO. Debt relief means no longer demanding full repayment, but critics say money may go to governments without proof of good use, may not reach the needy, and may encourage new debts.
8Closing the gap: contrasting approaches
Closing the gap: contrasting approaches
  1. Core-periphery model: growth begins at a core and drains the rest, until the core is congested and spreads, creating new sub-cores. Some governments use it as an excuse to do nothing.
  2. Top-down is government- or IGO-led, concerned with national or regional economy and large infrastructure or industry projects, hoping benefits reach local areas, with little local involvement. Bottom-up is grass-roots, centred on people helping themselves, with NGOs providing finance and technical help.

Top-down: Nigeria and UK
  1. Nigeria coastal railway: signed in 2014 with China Railway Construction Corporation, 1400 km from Lagos to Calabar across the Niger delta to create a coastal growth corridor; about US$12 billion, 85 per cent Chinese loans and 15 per cent Nigerian; 200 000 construction jobs and 30 000 permanent. Oil price fall in 2016 put it on hold, though it could cut oil dependence.
  2. UK HS2: high-speed line London to Birmingham (by 2026) then Manchester, Leeds and Sheffield (by 2033), part of the 'northern powerhouse' and the attempt to reduce the North-South divide. Funded by government and private money. Opponents cite small time savings and environmental costs; the cost was estimated at US$128 billion.
  3. Problems of top-down: unsustainable debt, loan conditions giving outsiders control, little local benefit beyond construction jobs, imported machinery and expertise, and costly operation and maintenance.
Bottom-up: Kenya and South Africa
  1. Bottom-up schemes are planned and controlled locally, cheaper, use appropriate technology, less damaging, run by NGOs and sustainable.
  2. WaterAid in Kenya: 17 million lack safe water and 32 million lack sanitation; water goes first to planned urban areas and wealthy rural communities; up to a third of pumps are broken. WaterAid (UK trust, 1981) partners with communities for hand-dug wells, hand pumps, rainwater harvesting, and community latrines.
  3. South Africa's Manyeding agricultural co-operative ('End Hunger' scheme): communal fallow land grows maize, beans, wheat, sunflower, groundnuts and potatoes; aims to restore 1 million hectares in five years; funded by a mining company (about R10 million) and municipalities; 160 partners and 24 staff; organic farming and marketing training; a storage facility. It tackles hunger, food insecurity and poverty.
Exam tipBoth approaches have costs and benefits: top-down may suit the global gap in economic development, while bottom-up suits the welfare gap and gaps within countries. They are best used in partnership.
