Elements, compounds and mixtures

Every substance is one of three kinds. Learn how to tell which.

Three kinds of substance

Element, compound or mixture?

Element

A substance that can’t be split into anything simpler by chemical means. All its atoms have the same atomic number.

Compound

Two or more elements chemically combined, always in fixed proportions, e.g. HF, MgF₂, CO₂, CH₄.

Mixture

Substances mixed together with no chemical reaction. The parts can be in any proportion.

Example 1

Iron and sulfur

The mixture

Iron is grey, sulfur is yellow. Iron reacts with dilute acid to give hydrogen; sulfur does not. A magnet separates them.

The compound FeS

In iron sulfide the proportion of iron to sulfur is always the same. It reacts quite differently with acids.

Pure or impure?

Melting and boiling points tell you if a substance is pure.

Melting and boiling points

Pure = fixed temperature

Pure substance

Elements and pure compounds melt and boil at fixed temperatures.

  • Water melts at 0 °C, boils at 100 °C.
  • Mixtures usually melt or boil over a range of temperatures.
Spec 1.9 understand that a pure substance has a fixed melting and boiling point, but that a mixture may melt or…
Impurities

Impurities change the numbers

Lower melting point

10 g of salt in 1 litre of water melts at about −0.6 °C (not 0 °C).

Higher boiling point

The same salt water boils at about 100.2 °C (not 100 °C).

RememberImpurities lower the melting point and raise the boiling point.
Exam tipA mixture is not a pure substance. A small amount of an unwanted substance in a sample is called an impurity.

Filtration and crystallisation

Getting a solid out of a liquid, and a solute out of a solution.

Filtration

Separating a solid from a liquid

Filtration

Separates an insoluble solid from a liquid, e.g. sand from water.

Residue = the solid left in the filter paper.
Filtrate = the liquid that comes through.

AlsoIt separates two solids if only one dissolves in water.

Crystallisation

Getting a solute out of a solution

Crystallisation

Separates a solute from a solution, e.g. salt from salt solution.

  • Heat gently; stop when crystals start to form.
  • Leave to cool → crystals form.
  • Filter, wash and dry them.

Method

Crystallising sodium chloride

  1. Heat the solution in an evaporating basin to boil off some of the water.
  2. Test with a glass rod: if crystals form quickly on it, the solution is almost saturated.
  3. Turn off the Bunsen burner. Crystals form as the solution cools and more water evaporates.
  4. Remove the crystals by filtration.

Glass rod test

Dip in the rod and lift it out. Crystals on the rod quickly = time to stop heating.

RememberCrystallisation keeps the solid. The water is lost to the air.

Distillation

Boil, then condense: collect the liquid as well.

Simple distillation

Pure water from salt solution

Simple distillation

Separates the parts of a solution. Unlike crystallisation, it also collects the water.

  • Water boils; the condenser turns it back into a liquid.
  • The salt stays in the flask.

Simple distillation

Details that matter

Water in at the bottom

Cooling water enters the lower end of the condenser. It fills the jacket better and stays full if the flow stops.

Keep heating?

The solution gets so concentrated that the salt crystallises out in the flask.

Two products

Pure water (the solvent) is collected. The salt (the solute) stays behind.

Careful heating

Not 100% pure

The ethanol collected is about 96% pure. The last 4% of water can’t be removed by distillation.

Paper chromatography

Separating the dyes in inks and food colourings.

Paper chromatography

The set-up

Rf = distance moved by spot ÷ distance moved by solvent
Rf = dspot ÷ dsolvent
no unitsbetween 0 and 1
RememberThe solvent must be below the pencil start line.
Spec 1.10 describe these experimental techniques for the separation of mixtures: • simple distillation • fractional…
Practical · inks or food colourings

Running a chromatogram

  1. Draw a pencil line about 1 cm from the bottom. Not pen: its colours may move up with the solvent.
  2. Put a spot of the mixture on the line (teat pipette or capillary tube). Let it dry.
  3. Stand the paper in a little solvent, with the solvent below the pencil line.
  1. Put a lid (watch glass) on. The air becomes saturated with solvent, so it doesn’t evaporate from the paper.
  2. When the solvent is about 1 cm from the top, remove the paper. Mark the solvent front in pencil.
  3. Leave the paper to dry.

Paper chromatography

Good to know

Choosing a solvent

It can be water or a non-aqueous solvent. Find a suitable one by experimenting.

Bad solvent?

A dye that stays on the line is not soluble at all. One that moves with the front is too soluble. Try a different solvent.

Colourless mixtures

Mixtures such as sugars can also be separated, but the spots must be made visible.

SafetyAvoid skin contact with the solvents and dyes.

Using chromatography in analysis

Rf values tell you which substances are in a mixture.

Rf values

Measuring for an Rf value

Rf = distance moved by a spot ÷ distance moved by the solvent front
Rf = 2.9 ÷ 3.6 = 0.81
  • Measure both from the pencil line.
  • Measure to the centre of the spot.
  • Rf stands for retardation factor.
Worked example

Calculating an Rf value

An orange spot moved 2.0 cm. The solvent front moved 3.6 cm. Calculate the Rf value.
EquationRf = distance moved by spot ÷ distance moved by solvent
SubstituteRf = 2.0 ÷ 3.6
Rf = 0.56 (no units)
Common mistakeAn Rf bigger than 1 means you divided the wrong way round.
Exam tipRf is always between 0 and 1 and has no units.
Spec 1.12 understand how to use the calculation of Rf values to identify the components of a mixture
Identifying dyes

Which dyes are in mixture m?

m contains d1, d3 and d4: same colour and same distance.

d2 is the same colour as a spot in m but moved a different distance: a different compound.

3 spots = at least 3 dyes. Two could move the same distance; check with a different solvent.
