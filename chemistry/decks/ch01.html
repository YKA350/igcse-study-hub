The particles explain everyday facts

A brick wall is solid

Strong forces of attraction stop the particles moving out of your way.

You can swim in water

Moving through air is easy

Melting and freezing

Solid ⇄ liquid

Melting: solid → liquid

Heating makes particles vibrate faster. At the melting point the forces can no longer hold them in place and they move around each other. Energy must be supplied.

Key term · melting point

The temperature at which a solid melts.

Freezing: liquid → solid

Cooling slows the particles. The forces hold them in fixed positions and they pack more closely.

RememberThe melting point and freezing point of a substance are the same temperature.

Boiling and evaporation

Liquid ⇄ gas

Boiling

The liquid is heated so strongly the particles move fast enough to overcome all the forces between them. Only at the boiling point.

Evaporation

Very fast particles at the surface escape. It happens at any temperature. No bubbles.

Condensing

Cool a gas and forces start to hold the particles together as a liquid.

RememberThe stronger the forces of attraction, the higher the boiling point: more energy is needed.
Common mistakePuddles disappear at 5 °C by evaporation, but water only boils at 100 °C.

Sublimation

Solid straight to gas

Key term · sublimation

A solid changes directly to a gas without becoming a liquid. The reverse is usually called deposition. Dry ice (solid CO₂) does this at −78.5 °C.

Exam tipThe white cloud is water vapour from the air condensing. CO₂ gas is invisible.

Diffusion

Particles spread out from high concentration to low concentration.

Diffusion

What is diffusion?

Key term · diffusion

The spreading out of particles from where they are at a high concentration to where they are at a low concentration.

Why does a smell take time?

Ammonia particles travel at about 600 m/s, but they keep bouncing off air particles. By the time the smell reaches every corner, each particle may have travelled 30 km or more.

Diffusion in gases

Bromine and air

  1. Put a gas jar of brown bromine under a jar of air.
  2. Remove the lids.
  3. The brown spreads up until both jars are evenly brown.
RememberAir particles diffuse down as well. With hydrogen and air, both jars explode the same: hydrogen does not all rise.

Diffusion in gases

Ammonia and hydrogen chloride

What you see

A white ring of ammonium chloride forms. It takes time: the particles must diffuse along the tube.

Where is the ring?

Closer to the HCl end. Ammonia is lighter (Mr 17), so it moves faster and goes further.

Common mistakeThe ring is nearer the HCl end, not the middle.

Diffusion in liquids

Diluting a coloured solution

Diffusion in liquids

Much slower than in gases: particles move more slowly and are closer together, so there is less room. A crystal in still water can take days to spread.

RememberEven after huge dilution the colour is still seen, so there must be a huge number of tiny particles.

Worked example

How small are the particles?

0.01 g of potassium manganate(VII) is dissolved in 1 cm³, then diluted to 10 000 cm³. The colour can still just be seen.
Step 11 cm³ is about 20 drops (a drop is 0.05 cm³).
Step 210 000 cm³ is 10 000 × 20 = 200 000 drops.
Step 3Each drop must hold at least one particle.
So 0.01 g has at least 200 000 particles

In fact…

There are about 38 000 000 000 000 000 000 particles in 0.01 g. Particles are far too small to see.

Exam tipState the conclusion: matter is made of very small particles.

Solutions and solubility

Dissolving a solid, and how much dissolves.

Solutions

Solution words

Solute and solvent

The solute is the substance that dissolves. The solvent is the liquid it dissolves in.

Solution

The liquid formed when a solute dissolves in a solvent.

Saturated solution

Holds as much dissolved solid as possible at that temperature. Some solid stays undissolved.

Spec 1.7C practical: investigate the solubility of a solid in water at a specific temperature
Solubility

How much dissolves?

Key term · solubility (Chemistry only)

The mass of solute that dissolves in 100 g of solvent at a stated temperature to make a saturated solution. Units: g per 100 g of solvent.

Example

Sodium chloride: about 36 g per 100 g of water at 25 °C.

Why does a solid dissolve?

The attractions between solute particles are broken and new attractions form between solvent and solute particles. It dissolves if the new forces are strong enough.

Exam tipSolubility changes with temperature, so always quote a temperature.
Spec 1.7C practical: investigate the solubility of a solid in water at a specific temperature
Practical (Chemistry only)

Solubility of potassium nitrate at 40 °C

  1. Weigh an evaporating basin.
  2. Heat a boiling tube of water to just above 40 °C.
  3. Add potassium nitrate and stir until no more dissolves and some solid is left.
  4. Let the solution cool to exactly 40 °C.
  5. Pour some solution (no solid) into the basin.
  6. Weigh the basin and solution.
  7. Heat gently to evaporate the water, then weigh.
  8. Reheat and re-weigh until the mass stops changing.

Constant mass

Heat and weigh again until the mass stops changing: then all the water has gone.

Watch outIf solid spits out when heating, the mass of crystals is too low and the solubility looks too low.

Reading a solubility curve

Most solids

Solubility goes up as temperature rises: a lot for KNO₃, only a little for KCl.

Cooling KNO₃

At 90 °C: 200 g in 100 g water. At 30 °C: 50 g. Cool the solution and 150 g of crystals form.

Exam tipA line of best fit can be straight or curved. KCl is nearly straight; KNO₃ bends up steeply.
