Electrolysis

Passing electricity through a molten or dissolved ionic compound splits it up. Positive ions gain electrons at one electrode, negative ions lose them at the other.

Chemistry only

Electrolysis words

Electrolysis

A chemical change caused by passing an electric current through a compound that is molten or in solution.

Electrolyte

The liquid or solution that undergoes electrolysis. It contains ions.

Electrodes

Carry current into and out of the electrolyte. Carbon or platinum are inert.

Anode and cathode

Anode is positive, cathode is negative (PANiC). Cations go to the cathode, anions to the anode.

Spec 1.57C know that anion and cation are terms used to refer to negative and positive ions respectively

Electrolysing molten PbBr₂

Cathode (−)

Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb. Reduction. Grey molten lead forms.

Anode (+)

2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻. Oxidation. Brown bromine gas.

Exam tipNothing happens until the PbBr₂ melts.

What you see and why

Observations

Once molten the bulb lights. Brown bromine gas at the anode. Molten lead under the cathode.

Stop heating

It solidifies, bubbling stops and the bulb goes out.

OILRIG

Oxidation Is Loss of electrons, Reduction Is Gain.

Redox

Pb²⁺ is reduced at the cathode; Br⁻ is oxidised at the anode. Electrons flow in the wire, ions in the electrolyte.

Chemistry only

Why hydrogen, not sodium?

Reactivity

Sodium is very reactive so Na⁺ is hard to turn into atoms. Hydrogen is less reactive.

Hydroxide builds up

OH⁻ ions remain near the cathode so the solution becomes alkaline.

At the anode

Many more Cl⁻ than OH⁻, so chloride is oxidised. Less Cl₂ collected as it is more soluble.

What is left

Sodium hydroxide solution, NaOH(aq).

Method

Practical method

  1. Set up the cell with carbon electrodes in a glass tube and bung.
  2. Fill with concentrated sodium chloride solution.
  3. Put a test-tube of solution over each electrode, not covering it completely.
  4. Connect the power pack in a fume cupboard.
  5. Look for bubbles or metal at each electrode and test the gases.
Exam tipNot covering the electrode completely, or no current flows.
Common mistakeChlorine is poisonous.

Rules for solutions

Cathode

Metal high in the reactivity series: hydrogen. Metal below hydrogen (copper, silver): the metal.

Anode

Halide (Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻): the halogen. Sulfate, nitrate or hydroxide: oxygen.

Recap

Chapter 10 in a nutshell

Electrolysis: current through a molten or dissolved ionic compound
Anode +, cathode − (PANiC)
Molten binary: metal at cathode, non-metal at anode
Reduction at cathode, oxidation at anode (OILRIG)
Solutions: hydrogen unless metal below hydrogen
Halide gives halogen, otherwise oxygen
NaCl(aq): H₂, Cl₂ and NaOH
H₂SO₄(aq): 2 vol H₂ : 1 vol O₂
