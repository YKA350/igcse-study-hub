Metallic structure

A lattice (regular arrangement) of positive ions in a sea of delocalised electrons.

Metallic bonding

Electrostatic attraction between each positive ion and the delocalised electrons, which are free to move.

Ideas to remember

Giant structure

No molecules: every positive ion attracts all the delocalised electrons.

Ion charge

Group 1 metals form 1+ ions in the lattice; Group 2 form 2+.

Exam tipIn equations write a metal as atoms, Na, not Na⁺. The electrons balance the positive charges.
Exam tipDraw the ions in any regular arrangement with electrons between them.

Most metals are hard with high melting points: ions and delocalised electrons attract strongly.

Sodium

1 delocalised electron and 1+ ions: weaker bonding, soft, low melting point for a metal.

Magnesium

2 electrons and 2+ ions: much stronger attraction, higher melting point.

Why metals conduct

Free electrons

The delocalised electrons are free to move throughout the structure and carry charge.

In a circuit

Electrons flow out at the positive end and more flow in from the negative terminal.

Solid and liquid

Metals conduct as solids and when molten.

Common mistakeElectrons carry the current in metals, not ions.

Why metals are malleable

Layers slide

A force makes the layers of positive ions slide over each other.

Bonding intact

Ions are still attracted to the delocalised electrons.

Ductile too

Can be drawn into wires: same explanation.

Why is aluminium malleable?
1The positive ions are in layers.
2A force makes the layers slide over each other.
3Ions still attracted to delocalised electrons, so bonding is not broken.
layers slide
Why does Mg melt higher than Na?
1Mg has 2+ ions and 2 delocalised electrons per atom; Na has 1+ and 1.
2So attraction is stronger in Mg.
3More energy is needed to overcome it.
stronger attraction
