1Measuring the rate
What rate means
Rate of reaction

how fast reactants are used up or products formed: change in amount per unit time

rate = change in concentration, volume or mass ÷ time

Units such as g/min, g/s, cm³/s

2Collision theory
The key ideas

Collide

Particles must collide.

Enough energy

At least the activation energy.

Successful

Collisions at or above it react; the rest bounce off.

Rate

depends on the frequency of successful collisions

5Temperature and pressure
Temperature: two reasons

Faster particles

Collide more often: only a small effect.

More energetic

A small rise gives a much bigger number with energy ≥ activation energy.

Exam tipAbout 10 °C rise doubles the rate.
Pressure in gases

Higher pressure

Same gas squeezed into a smaller volume: particles closer, more frequent collisions.

Solids and liquids

Pressure makes almost no difference.

6Catalysts
What is a catalyst?
Catalyst

increases the rate of a reaction but is chemically unchanged at the end

Enzyme

a biological catalyst, e.g. catalase in potatoes and liver

Hydrogen peroxide

2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂. Catalysts: MnO₂, PbO₂, catalase. Not written in the equation.

Comparing solids
  1. 100 cm³ of 2 vol H₂O₂.
  2. Weigh 0.20 g of solid; add; gas syringe.
  3. Volume every 20 s for 3 min.
  4. Repeat with other solids.
Exam tipFilter, dry, reweigh: mass unchanged.
7How a catalyst works
Reaction profile

Eₐ

Height from reactants to the top of the hump.

Catalyst

Lower hump; ΔH unchanged.

Exam tipWrite: 'alternative route with lower activation energy'.
Why catalysts matter

Tunnel analogy

A road tunnel through a mountain: the route over the top is still there.

Industry

Lower temperatures and pressures, so lower cost.

✓ Say this

More frequent collisions. Temperature: many more particles with energy ≥ Eₐ. Catalyst: alternative route, lower Eₐ.

✗ Avoid this

Just 'more collisions'; temperature lowers Eₐ; catalyst is used up; rate changes the amount of gas.
